## ABSTRACT

This  study  explores  the  potential  of  AI  Digital  Textbooks  (AIDT)  to improve educational experiences for multicultural middle school students  in  Korea.  As  the  student  population  becomes  more  diverse, traditional methods often fail to meet the unique needs of multicultural learners. The research evaluates AIDT's effectiveness in providing personalized, interactive, and culturally relevant content. By analyzing test versions of AIDT through inclusive education theory, the study identifies gaps, including the lack of multilingual support, culturally relevant content, and adaptive learning pathways. The findings stress the need for  real-time  feedback,  interactive  simulations,  personalized  study plans, collaborative learning tools, and enhanced cultural understanding through group projects. The study also emphasizes the importance of  comprehensive  teacher  training  to  integrate  AIDT  effectively  in diverse classrooms. The recommendations aim to create a more inclusive  and  supportive  environment,  ensuring  that  both  Korean  and multicultural students can thrive academically and socially.

## KEYWORDS

AI digital textbooks; multicultural education; academic achievement; adaptive learning tools; middle school student

Artificial Intelligence (AI) technology has advanced rapidly across various sectors, including  education.  Its  integration  into  learning  environments  has  changed  how  students access educational content and how teachers facilitate learning and classroom management (Hwang et al.,  2020;  Idroes  et  al.,  2023;  Yu  et  al.,  2024).  In  the  Republic  of  Korea (Korea),  the  development  of  AI  Digital  Textbook  (AIDT),  planned  to  begin  in  2025, represents a major step towards digital innovation in education.

Despite a decline in Korea's overall student population, the proportion of multicultural students  has  steadily  increased,  comprising  3.8%  of  elementary  and  secondary  school enrolment in 2023. This includes 4.4% in elementary schools, 3.3% in middle schools, and 1.7% in high schools (MoE &amp; KEDI, 2024). In 350 schools nationwide, over 30% of students come from immigrant backgrounds (Yoon et al., 2023). High-density multicultural schools, where multicultural students make up 70 to 80% of the population, are concentrated in regions  like  Gyeonggi  and  Chungcheong  (Kim,  2024).  These  schools  face  unique  challenges,  as  students  often  struggle  to  adapt,  and  traditional  teaching  methods  prove insufficient (Yoon et al., 2023).

## Inclusive education theory

Inclusive education aims to include all students, regardless of their physical, intellectual, social, emotional, linguistic, or other conditions (UNICEF, 2015). Historically, it emerged as an effort to integrate students with disabilities, focusing on removing barriers to access and participation (Graham, 2020). Since the 1970s, countries like the United States, the United  Kingdom,  Canada,  and  Australia  have  expanded  this  concept  to  address  the educational rights of marginalized groups, including African American students (Rapp &amp; Corral-Granados,  2024).  This  evolution  underscores  the  global  struggle  for  educational equity and inclusion.

Contemporary efforts to advance inclusive education, influenced by historical foundations,  have  been  shaped  by  international  organizations  such  as  the  United  Nations International Children's Emergency Fund (UNICEF). UNICEF emphasizes the fundamental right of every child to access quality education and highlights the significant challenges faced by children with disabilities and other marginalized groups in mainstream education  (UNICEF,  n.d.).  UNICEF's  multifaceted  approach  to  promoting  inclusive  education involves capacity-building, advocacy, awareness-raising, and implementation assistance. It  emphasizes the importance of structural reforms across various levels, such as policy alignment,  teacher  training,  and  community  engagement,  to  effectively  integrate  all children into mainstream educational environments (UNICEF, n.d.).

The  UN's  Sustainable  Development  Goal  (SDG)  of  'Leave  No  One  Behind  (LNOB)' emphasizes eliminating educational disparities, particularly for marginalized groups, including  multicultural  students  (UNSDG,  n.d.).  LNOB,  a  core  commitment  of  the 2030  Agenda  for  Sustainable  Development,  aims  to  eradicate  poverty,  discrimination,  and  inequality  by  prioritizing  the  marginalized.  In  education,  LNOB  highlights the  importance  of  creating  inclusive  systems  that  support  all  learners  (UNSDG,  n. d.).  Inclusive  education  theory  stresses  that  all  students,  regardless  of  their  background,  should  have  access  to  equitable  learning  opportunities  (UNICEF,  2015). AIDT  holds  promise  in  this  regard  by  incorporating  adaptive  learning  features  that could  cater  to  diverse  learning  needs.  For  instance,  personalized  learning  pathways in  AIDT  could  ensure  that  multicultural  students,  who  may  struggle  with  the Korean  language,  receive  tailored  educational  content  that  meets  their  proficiency levels.  This  approach  aligns  with  the  principle  of accessibility by  ensuring  that  all students  can  engage  with  the  material,  irrespective  of  their  linguistic  background.



## Multicultural middle schoolers' adversities

The  number  of  multicultural  students  in  Korea  has  steadily  increased.  According  to  the Korea Education Development Institute (KEDI, 2021), 98.1% of multicultural children enrol in elementary school, but this drops to 87.9% by high school, contrasting with the national average of 92.4%. Moreover, only 49.5% of multicultural students pursue higher education, compared to 67.6% of Korean students (MoE &amp; KEDI, 2024).

One  primary  reason  for  this  decline  is  the  difficulty  multicultural  students  face  in adapting to the school environment and curriculum. Many struggles with Korean, which is  essential  for  academic  success.  Studies  show  that  64%  of  multicultural  students  cite difficulty  with  schoolwork,  while  54%  report  challenges  in  socializing  with  peers  (KESS, 2018; Park &amp; Chung, 2021). In summary, their struggles with the Korean language hinder both academic performance and social integration, isolating them and preventing progression to high school.

Research  shows  that  language  proficiency  is  crucial  for  academic  success;  students who struggle with the primary language of instruction often fall behind (Christensen &amp; Stanat, 2007). For multicultural students in Korea, insufficient support in Korean language acquisition  fosters  a  cycle  of  disengagement  and  lower  performance,  leading  to  some leaving  school  before  high  school  (Kim,  2020).  Furthermore,  these  students  often  face social exclusion, which exacerbates isolation and academic challenges (Vo et al., 2018).

Research  on  multicultural  education  in  countries  like  Canada  shows  that  targeted language support programmes are crucial for helping immigrant students succeed academically and socially by bridging language gaps and offering culturally relevant learning experiences (Vo et al., 2018). Implementing similar programmes in Korea could improve retention and progression rates for multicultural students by providing the linguistic and socio-emotional support needed to thrive in middle and high school. These challenges are particularly evident in middle school, where strong societal pressure to graduate and pursue higher education can leave many multicultural students either not advancing to high school or entering labour-intensive occupations.

This  process  is  partly  driven  by  Korea's  competitive  education  system,  which  places immense pressure on academic achievement as a  pathway to  stable  employment and social  mobility.  These  high  expectations  create  significant  challenges  for  multicultural students, often leading to early disengagement from both school and society. Research shows  these  students  are  more  likely  to  enter  lower-skilled,  lower-paying  jobs  due  to insufficient academic qualifications (Choi, 2021; Hong, 2010). As a result, many end up in labour-intensive  industries  with  limited  opportunities  for  advancement,  reinforcing a cycle of socioeconomic disadvantages.

This issue is worsened by the perception that multicultural students, especially those from  immigrant  or  non-Korean  backgrounds,  lack  the  academic  drive  or  suitability  for traditional  education (Asia Society, n.d.).  The bias often limits available support to help these  students  overcome  their  challenges.  In  contrast,  countries  like  Canada  offer  targeted support, such as after-school tutoring and language classes, which improve high school retention and post-secondary aspirations among immigrant students (Christensen



&amp;  Stanat,  2007;  Vo  et  al.,  2018).  Adopting  similar  measures  in  Korea  could  help  multicultural  students  continue  their  education  beyond  high  school  and  avoid  low-wage labour.

Without comprehensive language and academic support, multicultural students may struggle  with  social  integration,  crucial  for  building  a  support  network  that  fosters educational  and  professional  aspirations.  The  article How  Korean  Language  Students Studying  in  Seoul  Navigate  Linguistic  and  Cultural  Homogeneity (Brown, 2024) highlights how  non-Korean  students  face  racialized  experiences,  limited  interaction  with  native speakers,  and  challenges  in  language  proficiency.  Even  students  of  Korean  heritage  or appearance face difficult expectations regarding language skills and cultural norms. This underscores  the  need  for  a  more  inclusive,  multicultural  approach  in  study-abroad programmes in Korea. Research shows that fostering a sense of belonging boosts motivation for higher education (Christensen &amp; Stanat, 2007). Implementing culturally responsive teaching, mentorship, and community involvement could help multicultural students envision and pursue career paths beyond labour-intensive jobs.

A national survey by the Ministry of Gender Equality and Family (2022) found that while 62% of multicultural students aspired to attend a four-year university, only 40.5% enrolled in  higher  education.  The  low  enrolment  rate  is  mainly  due  to  language  barriers,  which limit  educational information for multicultural parents (Ministry of Gender Equality and Family, 2022). The survey also revealed that 50.4% of immigrant and naturalized parents cited guidance and management of studies as their biggest challenge, followed by lack of information on further education and career paths at 37.6%. This underscores the importance  of  AIDT  as  a  vital  support  for  linguistically  and  academically  disadvantaged students.

In  summary,  while  efforts  are  being  made  to  improve  educational  opportunities  for multicultural students in Korea, significant challenges remain. To address this challenge, the Korean Ministry of Education could partner with AI developers to integrate language support  tools  in  the  AIDT  system,  providing  real-time  translation  and  context-based language  scaffolds.  These  tools  would  help  multicultural  students  overcome  language barriers, aligning with the Korean government's 'Personalized Education for All' initiative, and offering a model for multilingual education.

## Korean artificial intelligence digital textbooks

Korea  is  the  first  country  to  announce  the  use  of  AIDT.  As  societies  transition  into  the digital age, the Korean Ministry of Education has actively promoted enhanced textbooks as  part  of  broader  educational  reforms  (MoE,  2024).  This  initiative  aims  to  improve individual  student  learning  outcomes  and  foster  inclusivity.  According  to  the  MoE (2024),  AIDT  will  be  implemented  in  2025,  focusing  on  priority  subjects  such  as Mathematics, English, Informatics, and Korean (Special Education). The rollout will target 3 rd and 4 th graders in elementary school, as well as freshmen in middle school and high school.  This  pioneering  effort  presents  Korea  with  a  unique  opportunity  to  become a  global  leader  in  the  development  and  implementation  of  AIDT,  setting  a  benchmark for  other  nations  and  demonstrating its commitment to educational innovation on the world stage.



In  the past, digital supplemental materials for bilingual learners have been available, but  only  for  elementary  school  students  and  subjects  like  social  studies  and  Korean language.  Although  called  digital  textbooks,  these  resources  still  require  teachers  to print  PDF  files  daily.  In  contrast,  AIDT  uses  artificial  intelligence  technology  to  provide a more integrated educational experience, supporting all key participants-students, teachers, and parents-more effectively than traditional paper textbooks.

According to the Korea Educational Research Information Service (KERIS, n.d.), the core services  of  AIDT  encompass  a  range  of  educational  support  tailored  to  different  stakeholders, as outlined below:

- For  students ,  AIDT  provides  comprehensive  learning  diagnostics,  adaptive  paths, personalized content recommendations, and individualized support through an AI tutor.
- For  teachers ,  AIDT  offers  lesson  design  tools  and  AI  assistant  support,  including content  restructuring,  tailored  resources,  and  data-driven  management  to  track student progress.

As a pioneer in the field, Korea has the unique opportunity to harness artificial intelligence to create customized learning experiences that adapt to each student's needs. For multicultural learners,  AI  can  provide  personalized  language  support,  tailored  content,  and  culturally relevant examples, enhancing both comprehension and engagement (Son et al., 2023). This adaptability enables AI to bridge gaps for students with diverse language proficiencies, cultural backgrounds,  and  academic  skills,  making  education  more  accessible  and  inclusive  (Choi, 2024; Son et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023). By aligning educational technology with the needs of diverse  students,  Korea  has  the  chance  to  lead  the  way  in  closing  achievement  gaps  and fostering equitable learning environments, setting a global example for others to follow.

## Methods

This  study  conducted  a  detailed  analysis  of  a  test  version  of  middle  school  AIDT  and teacher support through the lens of inclusive education theory, focusing on how well it addresses the unique needs of multicultural middle school students.

## Data collection

In  this  study,  we  evaluated  publicly  available  AIDT  test  versions  from  various  subjects, selecting English, Social Studies, and Science for middle school students from the webpage at  https://dtbook.edunet.net/. These subjects were chosen to represent a cross-section of curricular content, where multicultural middle school students might experience academic language and cultural barriers. We selected one representative chapter from each subject, English, social studies, and science, focusing on student textbooks that feature a range of features and content types, including text, images, and interactive elements.

We  applied  three  key  criteria  for  analysis:  content  accessibility,  diversity  through personalization,  and  teacher  support  mechanisms.  These  criteria  were  developed  to assess  how  well  the  AIDT  addresses  the  unique  challenges  faced  by  multicultural students,  such  as  language  barriers,  social  integration,  and  differentiated  learning needs.  Images  of  some  parts  of  the  selected  chapters  were  captured  using  screen capture  tools,  ensuring  clarity  and  legibility.  This  comprehensive  approach  facilitated detailed analysis of the AIDT content, focusing on its effectiveness in helping students overcome language and cultural barriers  in  academic  settings.  It  is  important  to  note that the AIDT webpage is still in the testing phase, so some details may change before full  implementation.



For  the  teachers'  support,  we  were  only  able  to  access The  Guide  to  Using  Digital Textbooks  for  Middle  School  Teachers published by the MoE and KERIS (2018), since the researchers lacked the registered teacher licence numbers required to access additional materials for teachers on the website. However, this reliance on a single source indicates that the study's evaluation of teacher support mechanisms within the AIDT framework is based on one authoritative guide.

## Analysis criteria

The analysis evaluated the effectiveness of AIDT in supporting students in overcoming language  and  cultural  barriers  within  academic  settings,  aligning  its  framework  with international  best  practices  in  inclusive  education.  The  analysis  criteria  were  based  on the three core principles of inclusive education theory: accessibility of content, diversity through personalization, and support, all under the umbrella of equity from the Korean MoE and the United Nations (UN) as shown in Figure 1.

## Content accessibility

This criterion focused on the extent to which the AIDT provides multilingual support and culturally relevant content. The evaluation involved a comparative analysis of the AIDT's features against those implemented in countries known for strong language and cultural programmes,  such  as  Canada  (Evans,  2019;  Janakiraman  et  al.,  2019).  Specifically,  the analysis assessed:

Figure 1. Criteria for evaluating AIDT based on inclusive education theory.





- Multilingual Support :  This criterion assessed the availability of content in multiple languages, ease of switching between them, and the presence of translation tools, bilingual resources, and language-specific modules.
- Culturally  Relevant  Content :  This  criterion  focused  on  materials  that  reflect  the cultural backgrounds of diverse students, including literature, historical perspectives, and cultural practices that acknowledge and celebrate diversity.
- Language Proficiency Accommodation : This criterion assessed how AIDT supports students with varying language proficiency, focusing on scaffolding techniques such as  simplified  language  options,  visual  aids,  and  interactive  elements  to  enhance comprehension and engagement.

## Diversity through personalization

This  criterion  analysed  AIDT's  ability  to  personalize  learning  experiences  to  meet  the diverse  needs  of  multicultural  students  (Lindner  et  al.,  2019;  Shemshack  &amp;  Spector, 2020). Key aspects evaluated included:

- Adaptive Learning Pathways :  This criterion assessed the system's ability to adjust the  content  difficulty  and  based  on  individual  student  performance  and  learning pace, including the use of algorithms to tailor educational experiences.
- Immediate and Actionable Feedback : This criterion focused on providing immediate,  actionable  feedback  to  help  students  understand  their  progress  and  identify areas  for  improvement.  It  was  assessed  by  examining  the  types  of  feedback  (e.g. textual,  visual,  auditory)  and  their  relevance  to  students'  cultural  and  academic contexts.
- Personalized  Content  Recommendations :  This  criterion  assessed  the  system's ability  to  suggest  learning  materials  and  activities  that  align  with  the  student's cultural background and academic needs, focusing on the diversity of recommendations and their alignment with the student's learning history and preferences.

## Support mechanisms

This  criterion  examined  the  tools  and  functionalities  available  within  the  AIDT  to  assist teachers  in  facilitating  student  learning  (Rapp  &amp;  Corral-Granados,  2024;  Shemshack  &amp; Spector, 2020). The evaluation focused on:

- AI Tutor Functionality :  This criterion assessed the effectiveness of AI-driven tutoring features in providing personalized academic support, focusing on the AI tutor's ability  to  offer  explanations,  answer  questions,  and  provide  practice  exercises  tailored to the student's learning needs.
- Teacher Support Tools :  This criterion assessed tools designed to assist teachers in managing  multicultural  classrooms,  focusing  on  features  such  as  lesson  planning aids, student progress tracking, and resources for addressing cultural and linguistic diversity.

## Limitations

The limitations of this research stem from the test version of the AIDT, which may not fully  reflect  the  final  product's  capabilities,  potentially  affecting  the  evaluation  accuracy.  Additionally,  due  to the large number of textbook publishers, it was not feasible to  review  all  of  them,  limiting  the  generalizability  of  our  findings  across  all  digital  AI textbooks.

Despite  these  limitations,  the  research  offers  significant  benefits.  The  developed criteria offer a structured approach to assessing AIDT's effectiveness in supporting multicultural students, serving as a valuable tool for future evaluations. The insights gained lay the groundwork for future studies and can help identify potential errors in government guidelines, improving the effectiveness of AIDT on multicultural education.

## Results and discussion

The publicly accessible test versions of AIDT for middle school students include subjects like English, social studies, and science, with several versions from different publishers. To ensure diverse representation, we selected English for 7th graders, Social Studies for 8th graders, and Science for 9th graders, all from the same publisher, Mirae N. This approach enabled  a  comprehensive  analysis  of  how  AIDT  supports  multicultural  middle  school students  across  various  academic  contexts.  Additionally,  our  study  complied  with  the publisher's criteria for using their content without prior permission, ensuring ethical and legal use of the AI Digital Textbooks (Mirae N, 2021).

## English for 7th graders

The English AIDT featured multimedia resources such as audio files, video files, speaking and writing activities, and bilingual (Korean-English) interpretations. Notably, speaking and writing  activities  provided  interchangeable  options,  enabling  students  to  choose  their answers based on their preferences. Supplementary materials for fast  finishers  were  also included along with self-checking sections to allow students to monitor their progress.

Despite these interactive features, the English AIDT exhibited significant shortcomings in  supporting  multicultural  students.  The  analysis  showed  the  English  textbook  lacked translation tools and bilingual resources hindered non-native Korean speakers' ability to understand  the  material.  Furthermore,  the  absence  of  culturally  diverse  examples reduced  its  relevance  and  engagement  for  multicultural  learners.  Minimal  scaffolding, such as simplified language and visual aids, posed challenges for students with limited proficiency, as shown in Figure 2.

Figure  2  highlights  the  types  of  interactive  exercises  designed  to  enhance students'  language  skills.  Despite  its  interactive  features,  the  English  AIDT  demonstrated  significant  gaps  in  inclusivity  and  adaptability  for  multicultural  learners.  In terms  of  personalization,  the  AIDT  did  not  dynamically  adjust  content  difficulty based  on  student  performance.  This  limitation  may  restrict  its  ability  to  accommodate  students  at  various  levels  of  proficiency.  The  English  AIDT  did  not  adjust content  difficulty  based  on  student  performance,  making  it  less  effective  for accommodating  varying  learning  paces  and  styles.

Moreover,  feedback  within  the  textbook  was  limited,  textual,  and  lacked  cultural customization  essential  for  guiding  multicultural  students  in  improving  their  language skills. Effective feedback should be immediate, actionable, and culturally relevant to help students understand their progress and areas for improvement. The lack of such feedback in  the  English  AIDT  means  that  students  may  not  receive  the  guidance  they  need  to enhance their learning outcomes as shown in Figure 3.

Figure 3 demonstrates the inclusion of translated texts aimed at aiding comprehension.  Despite  this  feature,  the  analysis  found  that  the  English  AIDT  did  not  dynamically adjust  content  difficulty  based  on  student  performance,  limiting  its  effectiveness  for students with varying proficiency levels.

Lastly,  there  was  no  evidence  of  content  recommendations  aligned  with  students'  cultural  backgrounds  or  language  levels,  limiting  engagement  for  multicultural  students.  Personalized  content  recommendations  can  help  make  learning more  relevant  and  engaging  by  connecting  the  material  to  students'  own  experiences  and  interests.  The  absence  of  this  feature  in  the  AIDT  suggests  that  it  may not  fully  support  the  diverse  needs  of  its  users.  Overall,  the  findings  indicate  that while  the  English  AIDT  has  potential,  it  currently  falls  short  in  several  key  areas needed  to  effectively  support  multicultural  students.

## Social studies for 8th graders

The Social Studies AIDT focused on Korean society, covering topics such as the Korean Constitution,  labour  law,  and  related  subjects.  As  social  studies  often  involve  complex academic  terms  and  Sino-Korean  vocabulary,  substantial  background  knowledge  is required  for  comprehension  (Kim,  2020;  Kim  &amp;  Cho,  2024).  All  materials  were  written and  explained  entirely  in  Korean,  without  additional  multilingual  or  supplementary resources, as shown in Figure 4.



Figure 3. Translated reading materials from the English AIDT (Choi et al., 2018, pp. 20-21).



The analysis revealed that the absence of translation tools or bilingual options creates a  significant  barrier  for  non-native  Korean  speakers,  particularly  for  understanding advanced  social  studies  concepts  in  the  Social  Studies  AIDT.  Despite  the  potential  to explore  global  comparisons  or  contrasts,  the  content  failed  to  include  diverse  cultural perspectives,  limiting  its  inclusiveness  and  engagement.  Additionally,  the  absence  of simplified language or interactive scaffolds made it challenging for students with lower Korean proficiency to engage with the content, as shown in Figure 4.

Figure  4  shows  how  the  material  is  structured  to  provide  essential  information  and summaries in the chapter related to Korean labour law. However, the analysis revealed that the textbook lacked multilingual support and culturally relevant content, which were critical for making the material accessible and engaging for multicultural students.

In  terms  of  personalization,  the  Social  Studies  AIDT  did  not  offer  adaptive  learning pathways based on student comprehension or background. This omission could limit its ability  to  support  students  with  varying  levels  of  familiarity  with  Korean  history  and society  (Kim  &amp;  Cho,  2024).  Furthermore,  the  feedback  was  general  and  lacked  cultural relevance,  while  the  absence  of  adaptive  pathways  hindered  personalized  learning experiences for students at varying levels of familiarity with Korean history and society, as shown in Figure 5. In particular, the animated video materials did not provide language options  to  promote  understanding  for  multicultural  students,  as  well  the  scripts  were provided only in Korean language.



Figure 4. Key content with summary from the social studies AIDT (Kim et al., 2018, pp. 22-23).



Figure 5 highlights the use of multimedia to enhance learning. However, the analysis revealed that the feedback provided in Korean was general and lacked cultural relevance, limiting its effectiveness in supporting students from different cultural backgrounds.

Additionally, the personalized content recommendations within the Social Studies AIDT were not tailored  to  help  students  understand  complex  social  and  historical  content.  The absence of such tailored feedback reduces the tool's effectiveness for multicultural students.

Overall, while the Social Studies AIDT shows potential, it currently falls short in several critical areas necessary for supporting multicultural students. Improvements are needed in  multilingual  support,  culturally  relevant  content,  language  proficiency  accommodations,  adaptive  learning  pathways,  tailored  feedback,  and  personalized  content  recommendations are needed to make it a more inclusive and effective educational tool.

## Science for 9th graders

Science subjects emphasize hands-on learning and experimentation, frequently incorporating  Sino-Korean  terms  and  English  vocabulary  to  explain  foundational  concepts.  The Science AIDT incorporated animated and real experiment visuals, including augmented reality (AR) elements, to support written content. Chapter summaries and mini-tests were provided to help students review and assess their understanding, with activities requiring students to record observations and apply their knowledge, as shown in Figure 6.

The Science AIDT shares similar limitations with the English and Social Studies AIDTs in its inability to fully support multicultural students. The analysis of the Science AIDT for 9th graders revealed several critical shortcomings in supporting multicultural students. Like other subjects, the Science AIDT lacked multilingual options, making it difficult for nonnative  speakers  to  understand  technical  and  scientific  terminology.  Without  translation tools or bilingual resources, students with limited Korean proficiency may struggle to fully comprehend the content, potentially hindering their learning outcomes.

Additionally, the Science AIDT did not provide language proficiency accommodation, such as simplified language or visual aids, to support students with limited language skills. The  absence  of  these  scaffolding  techniques  makes  it  harder  for  students  to  grasp complex  scientific  key  concepts,  leading  to  challenges  in  comprehension  and  engagement. Overall,  while  the  Science  AIDT  shows  potential,  it  falls  short  in  several  key  areas necessary for  supporting  multicultural  students.  Improvements  in  multilingual  support, culturally  relevant  content,  language  proficiency  accommodations,  adaptive  learning pathways, immediate and actionable feedback, and personalized content recommendations are essential to make the textbook a more inclusive and effective educational tool.

The findings reveal that while AIDTs for English, Social Studies, and Science demonstrated promising features, they fell short in several critical areas necessary for effectively supporting multicultural  students.  One  significant  limitation  across  all  subjects  was  the  lack  of  multilingual  support,  as  the  absence  of  translation  tools  and  bilingual  resources  created  substantial  barriers  for  non-native  Korean  speakers,  significantly  impacting  accessibility. Lastly, the generic feedback mechanisms  emphasize  the  importance  of  creating  more  personalized  and  culturally sensitive support to help students track and improve their learning progress.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, while AIDT shows significant promise in supporting multicultural students, further  research  and  pilot  programmes  are  essential  to  assess  its  long-term  impact  in diverse  classrooms.  It  is  essential  to  monitor  how  these  tools  perform  in  real-world settings,  particularly  in  schools  with  high  multicultural  populations,  to  ensure  that  the technology meets the needs of all students. Collaborative efforts between policymakers, educators, and developers are necessary to refine AIDT and make it an inclusive tool that fosters academic success for multicultural students in Korea and beyond.

Additionally, teacher training is essential to effectively integrate these elements. Educators need  professional  development  focused  on  technology  use,  cultural  competencies,  and differentiated  instruction  to  foster  inclusive  environments.  Without  these  enhancements, disparities  between  Korean  and  multicultural  students  will  widen,  leaving  immigrantbackground students behind, both in Korea and their home countries. Brown's study (2024) on  linguistic  and  cultural  homogeneity  in  Korea  emphasizes  the  challenges  multicultural students face, reinforcing the need to adapt educational tools to support diverse needs.

## Recommendations

Based  on  the  findings  and  analysis  presented  in  this  study,  several  strategies  are necessary  to  enhance  the  effectiveness  of  AIDT  and  ensure  their  successful  use  in multicultural  middle  school  classrooms.  While  AIDT  holds  significant  potential  for supporting  diverse  student  populations,  there  are  key  areas  where  it  must  be improved  to  fully  address  the  unique  needs  of  multicultural  learners.  Additionally, teacher training plays a critical role in the successful integration of these technologies, as educators must be equipped with tools and knowledge to create inclusive learning environments.  Therefore,  we  present  the  following  recommendations  in  two  main areas:  1)  enhancing  AIDT  features,  and  2)  strengthening  teacher  training,  both  in  the short-term  and  long-term,  to  ensure  effective  Information  and  Communication Technology (ICT)  integration  in  multicultural  classrooms.



## Enhancing AIDT features for multicultural students

Based on the analysis of AIDT's current capabilities and limitations, several features need to be enhanced to ensure that the tool effectively supports multicultural students. While AIDT has the potential to offer personalized, interactive learning experiences, its current design does not fully address the diverse needs of multicultural learners. Key improvements are necessary in areas such as language support, cultural relevance, and adaptability to individual  learning  needs.  By  incorporating  these  enhancements,  AIDT  can  become  a  more inclusive and effective tool for students from diverse linguistic and cultural backgrounds. The following recommendations aim to refine AIDT's features, making them more responsive to the unique challenges faced by multicultural middle school students.

- (1) Real-time  Feedback :  Essential  for  identifying  and  correcting  misunderstandings quickly,  especially  in  high-paced  learning  environments.  AI-driven  feedback  can offer language tips and alternative strategies, supporting both Korean and multicultural students in subjects like math and grammar. Over the long term, real-time feedback can play a critical role in fostering academic success for both Korean and multicultural students.
- (2) Interactive  Simulations :  These  provide  immersive learning experiences, helping students grasp complex concepts through virtual hands-on practice. Simulations can  initially  focus  on  language  courses  and  gradually  expand  into  Science, Technology,  Engineering,  and  Mathematics  (STEM)  subjects,  offering  language support and practical experiences.
- (3) Personalized  Study  Plans :  AI  can  create  customized  plans  that  adjust  based  on students' progress. For Korean students, this ensures focus on areas needing improvement, while for multicultural students, it accommodates evolving language proficiency. Personalized plans reduce repetition, boosting efficiency and student success.
- (4) Collaborative Learning Tools : These foster teamwork and social skills are essential in  diverse  classrooms.  For  multicultural  students,  collaboration  tools  help  them develop interpersonal skills in a new environment. In the short-term, teacher-led collaboration can transition to more student-driven projects over time.
- (5) Enhanced Cultural Understanding through Group Projects : Projects that encourage  students  to  share  insights  from  their  cultural  backgrounds  can  enhance inclusivity and cultural understanding. Short-term, these can be teacher-led; longterm, they can be facilitated through AI with tailored support for teachers.

Incorporating these enhancements into AIDT will significantly improve its ability to support multicultural students, providing them with the tools they need to succeed in an increasingly diverse classroom environment. By focusing on real-time feedback, interactive simulations,  personalized  study  plans,  collaborative  learning  tools,  and  enhanced  cultural understanding, AIDT can become more adaptive and responsive to the needs of students from varied linguistic and cultural backgrounds. These improvements are crucial for ensuring that all students, regardless of their background, have equitable access to high-quality education.  In  the  next  section,  we  will  explore  the  critical  role  of  teacher  training  in effectively integrating these features into the classroom and ensuring their sustainable use.



## Teacher training for effective ICT integration in multicultural classrooms

Teacher  training  is  essential  to  the  successful  implementation  of  AIDT.  To  maximize  the benefits  of  AI  and  digital  tools,  educators  need  structured,  tailored  training  that  considers the  unique  dynamics  of  multicultural  classrooms  (UNESCO,  2018). Short-term  training should  focus  on  building  foundational  ICT  skills,  such  as  using  real-time  feedback  tools, collaborative  platforms,  and  multilingual  resources  to  support  diverse  learners.  In  addition, this training must address strategies for fostering inclusive environments where all students feel  represented. Long-term  professional  development programmes can  build  on  these basics by incorporating innovative teaching strategies, such as designing culturally responsive lessons,  creating  AI-driven  lesson  plans,  and  leveraging  adaptive  technologies  to  meet  the varied needs of multicultural learners (Cook &amp; Hollebeke, 2024).

For  teachers  to  effectively  integrate  ICT  into  their  classrooms,  they  must  not  only master the technical aspects of these tools but also understand how they can be used to  enhance  learning  for  students  from  diverse  linguistic  and  cultural  backgrounds (eCampusOntario,  n.d.).  This  dual  focus  on  both  technical  proficiency  and  pedagogical awareness will ensure that teachers are equipped to use ICT in ways that promote equity, inclusion, and academic success for all students.

## Short-term training goals

In the short term, teacher training programmes should prioritize building fundamental ICT skills. These foundational skills include proficiency in:

- Real-time feedback tools : Teachers can use AI-driven platforms that provide instant feedback  to  students,  helping  them  track  progress  and  address  learning  gaps efficiently.
- Collaborative platforms :  Digital  tools  that  facilitate  collaboration,  such  as  shared online workspaces, can promote teamwork and peer learning across diverse student groups.
- Multilingual resources : Familiarity with language-supportive technologies, such as translation  tools  and  multilingual  interfaces,  is  crucial  for  addressing  language barriers and ensuring equitable access to learning materials.

Additionally, short-term training should focus on equipping teachers with strategies for creating inclusive environments. This can be achieved by promoting cultural sensitivity and awareness in lesson planning and classroom interactions, using digital storytelling tools  to  incorporate  diverse  cultural  narratives,  and  encouraging  student  participation through interactive and culturally responsive activities. These short-term initiatives aim to immediately  enhance  teachers'  ability  to  integrate  ICT  tools  effectively  while  fostering a sense of inclusion and belonging among students.



## Long-term professional development

Over  time,  professional  development  programmes  should  expand  to  encompass  more advanced and innovative approaches to teaching in multicultural classrooms. Key areas of focus include:

- Innovative teaching strategies :  Teachers should learn to design lesson plans that leverage AI to personalize instruction, adapt to different learning styles, and meet the specific needs of multicultural learners.
- Culturally  responsive  pedagogy :  Long-term  training  should  guide  educators  in embedding cultural relevance into AI-driven tools, ensuring that teaching materials reflect the diverse experiences and backgrounds of students.
- Adaptive technologies : Teachers must be trained to use adaptive learning systems that  analyse  student  performance  data  and  provide  tailored  recommendations  to address individual challenges and strengths.

Furthermore,  sustained  professional  development  programmes  should  emphasize  continuous learning and collaboration among educators. This can be achieved through peer mentoring and sharing best practices within multicultural contexts, participation in workshops and conferences focused on ICT and multicultural education, and engagement in research and pilot programmes to explore emerging technologies in education.

By addressing both short-term and long-term training needs, teachers will be better prepared  to  lead  classrooms  enriched  by  ICT.  Structured  training  enables  educators  to create learning environments that not only incorporate advanced technologies but also prioritize equity and inclusion (UNESCO, 2018). This dual focus ensures that students from all  cultural  and  linguistic  backgrounds  have  access  to  high-quality  education,  bridging gaps and fostering mutual understanding. As the global education landscape continues to  evolve,  the  role  of  teachers  as  facilitators  of  technology  and  inclusion  will  become increasingly  necessary.  Investing  in  their  training  is  an  essential  step  towards  building classrooms where every student has the opportunity to succeed.

Abstract Large  Language  Models  (LLMs)  have  been  identified  as  having  the  unrivaled  potential  to  support  educators  and  learners  in  generating  high-quality  educational  content  at  various  levels  of  education. ## 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Background  and  Motivation

Large  Language  Models  (LLMs),  an  essential  advancement  in  Artificial  Intelligence (AI),  have  revolutionized  Natural  Language  Processing  (NLP).  The  development  of machine  learning  approaches,  intense  learning,  the  availability  of  high-end  computational  resources,  and  the  availability  of  large-scale  corpora  all  contributed  to  the emergence  of  LLMs  [1].  LLMs  are  a  type  of  language  models  (LMs)  that  use  Neural Networks  with  billions  of  parameters  that  were  trained  on  large  amounts  of  text  from websites,  books,  and  other  sources  using  self-supervised  learning  methods  [2].  LLM originated  in  early  statistical  language  models  and  is  an  outcome  of  the  traditional  ngram  [3].  Their  lack  of  capacity  to  handle  long-term  interdependences  and  contexts  in languages  led  to  the  advent  of  Neural  Networks  (NNs).  Notably,  the  advent  of  Recurrent  Neural  Networks  (RNNs)  marked  a  significant  milestone  in  modeling  sequential data  [4].  However,  the  challenges  of  vanishing  gradients  and  long-term  dependences also  limited  the  efficacy  of  these  RNNs.  The  most  significant  milestone  occurred when  the  transformer  architecture  was  introduced  [1].  This  introduces  a  self-attention mechanism,  allowing  parallelization  and  the  capacity  to  handle  long-term  dependencies  [5].  The  architecture  served  as  the  foundation  for  large-scale  production  of pre-trained  base  models.  For  instance,  OpenAI's  Generative  Pre-trained  Transformer (GPT)  series  and  Google's  Bidirectional  Encoder  Representations  from  Transformers (BERTs)  are  a  few  examples.  LLMs  have  become  ubiquitous  in  most  educational tasks.

They  have  unrivaled potential to  transform  education  [6].  LLMs  can  improve learners'  writing  skills  [7],  generate  assessment  items  [8],  support  intelligent  tutoring, automated  grading  systems,  and,  most  importantly,  generate  learning  content  [9]. Prior  research  has  demonstrated  the  significant  potential  of  LLMs  in  educational spaces, including the generation of lesson plans, content, question answering, formative  assessment,  and  multiple-choice  item  generation.

However,  using  LLMs  to  generate  such  sensitive  educational  content  is  not  without fault.  They  present  an  enticing  avenue  for  developing  educational  content,  promising unprecedented  opportunities  for  scalability  and  adaptability.  Despite  their  remarkable capabilities,  LLMs'  concerns  revolve  around  the  lack  of  specific  domain  expertise, their  propensity  to  generate  plausible-looking  content  that  is  full  of  error  manifested in  their  hallucinatory  behaviors  [10,  11],  their  tendency  to  perpetuate  bias  [12-14], and  their  lack  of  knowledge  temporality,  which  can  profoundly  impact  the  learning experiences  of  refugee  learners  [15].

The  primary  factors  include  the  absence  of  locally  curated,  high-quality  training corpora  [16]  and  the  tendency  of  LLM  to  rely  on  static  knowledge  during  training until  it  undergoes  retraining  using  new  corpora.  This  process  is  too  expensive  and resource  intensive.  Moreover,  LLMs  use  general-purpose  corpora  lacking  domainspecific  knowledge  [14].  Consequently,  the  deployment  of  LLMs  within  the  fragile contexts  of  refugee  education  may  face  critical  challenges  due  to  their  inherent  limitations  in  comprehending  complex  contexts.  In  other  words,  LLMs'  lack  of  sociocultural,  linguistic,  and  educational  backgrounds  may  lead  to  the  creation  of  instructional  materials  that  are  inaccurate  and  full  of  misinformation,  misrepresentative,  or disparate,  perpetuating  injustices  and  impeding  the  efficacy  of  LLM-based  educational  interventions  [17]  in  these  unstable  settings.  To  ensure  the  responsible  deployment  of  LLMs,  particularly  in  fragile  educational  spaces,  there  has  been  minimal effort  to  address  and  mitigate  these  concerns  [18].  The  current  study  seeks  to  answer the  question:  How  can  we  leverage  the  transformative  potential  of  REALMs  in  educational  content  creation  while  mitigating  bias  and  hallucinatory  behavior  and  ensuring retrieval  of  up-to-date  content  in  a  specific  domain  of  refugee  education?

## 1.2 LLMs  in  Educational  Content  Creation

The primary  operational premise  of LLMs  involves  training on diverse datasets, enabling  them  to  acquire  knowledge  of  intricate  aspects  of  language,  context,  and domain-specific  information.  We  enhance  these  models'  ability  to  generate  accurate, culturally, contextually  appropriate,  and  logically sound  instructional  material  by strategically prompting  and fine-tuning them for tasks like answering  questions. As  a result,  these  models  have  exhibited  a  remarkable  capacity  to  create  reliable educational  content  in  various  ways.

- Personalized  Learning  Content  Creation: LLMs  can  create  educational  materials specifically designed to meet each student's unique requirements,  capabilities,  and  preferred  learning  methods  [16].  They  may  enhance  personalized learning  by  analyzing  student  performance  data  and  establishing  adaptive  learning paths  [19].  They  can  suggest  suitable  learning  resources,  address  student  inquiries, and comment  on assignments [20]. This capability is crucial for learners in fragile  contexts.  Their  learning  styles  are  entirely  different.  They  possess  diverse linguistic  capabilities,  trauma  levels,  age,  and  past  experiences,  which  affect  the way  they  learn  in  the  classroom.
- Assessment Item Generation: LLMs have shown significant potential in enhancing  education  by  assisting  teachers  in  generating  instructional  materials such as quizzes and questions for several domains, including mathematics, programming,  and  language  learning  and  comprehension  [21].  Several  educators  have  leveraged  the  tools  for  multiple-choice  item  generation  [22-27].  Most scholars  noted  that  the  produced  multiple-choice  questions  matched  the  intended learning outcomes well. The researchers determine that instructors may use LLMs  to  capitalize  on  cutting-edge  generative  models  to  enhance  MCQ  writing endeavors  [28].  Several  others  have  utilized  the  tools  to  create  open-ended  questions [29-33]. This, therefore, means  that LLMs  can  create various forms of assessment,  including  those  requiring  higher-order  thinking  [26].  Teachers  could

- leverage  them  to  create  relevant,  equitable,  and  reliable  assessments  at  various levels  of  education  in  diverse  contexts,  including  refugee  education.
- Automated Content Generation: LLMs are currently being leveraged via automation to create top-tier educational content for various use cases. For instance,  automated  LLM-powered  systems  may  generate  valuable  information, including  curriculum,  syllabus,  assessments,  scheme  of  work,  translated  content, and  lesson  plans  [34].  Furthermore,  extensive  language  models  may  aid  educators  in  developing  comprehensive  and  inclusive  lesson  plans  and  activities  [20]. Teachers  may  provide  the  models  with  the  corpus  of  documents  they  wish  to  use as  the  foundation  for  creating  a  course  [35].  The  result  might  be  a  course  syllabus with  a  concise  summary  of  each  subject.  Language  models  can  produce  inquiries and  cues  that  stimulate  the  engagement  of  individuals  with  varying  knowledge and  skills  while  prompting  critical  thinking  and  problem-solving  [36].  Additionally,  they  can  create  specific  and  customized  exercises  and  assessments,  which may  help  guarantee  that  pupils  are  proficient  in  the  subject  matter.
- Learning  Content  Translation: LLMs have  high-level  capabilities  in  performing NLP  tasks  like  language  translation.  Multilingual  communications  are  common in  refugee  communities  [37].  Teachers  in  such  schools  could  harness  the  power  of LLM  to  make  learning  content  more  accessible  and  inclusive  [38].  They  can  translate  learning  materials  into  various  languages  that  learners  are  more  comfortable with.  This  could  reduce  attrition  rates  and  minimize  learning  curves.
- Curriculum  Development: LLMs  may  aid  in  curriculum  design,  recommending pertinent  themes,  learning  goals,  and  evaluations  [39].  LLMs  may  aid  in  formulating  measurable  learning  outcomes  at  every  stage  of  a  curriculum  and  tailoring  it to  cater  to  the  varied  requirements  of  individual  learners,  promoting  personalized and  adaptable  learning  experiences  [40].  LLMs  may  use  performance  data  analysis to  provide  targeted  interventions  and  advice  on  appropriate  resources  to  address learning  deficiencies  and  improve  educational  outcomes.  In  addition,  incorporating  LLMs  into  curriculum  planning  can  assist  teachers  in  creating,  revising,  or adjusting  a  curriculum.  LLMs  can  recommend  course  material,  learning  goals,  and pedagogical  approaches  based  on  current  trends  and  effective  methods  in  medical education  [34].  This  allows  teachers  to  allocate  more  time  to  other  teaching  responsibilities.  In  other  words,  these  tools  can  help  teachers  cater  to  the  nuanced  needs of  learners  in  fragile  contexts  like  refugee  camps.

## 1.3 Limitations  of  LLMs  in  Educational  Content  Creation

Despite  the  remarkable  potential  of  LLMs  within  the  educational  landscape,  they suffer  several  limitations.  The  summary  is  provided  in  Fig.  1.

- Hallucination: LLMs  exhibit a propensity to generate grammatically  correct content  but  are  full  of  factual  errors  [41-43].  This  is  termed  a  hallucination  [44]. Hallucinations  can be intrinsic or extrinsic. However,  factuality refers  to  how

Fig.  1 Limitations  of  LLMs  and  ethical  issues



closely LLM  replies  align  with  real-world  truths and verified facts. Factuality in  LLMs  impacts  several  processes  and  downstream  applications.  This  means employing  such  tools  in  fragile  environments  poses  a  significant  risk.

- Bias  and  Fairness: LLMs  may  inadvertently  generate  and  propagate  bias  that  is inherent  in  their  training  data  [37].  On  top  of  that,  they  can  generate  downright harmful  that  can  be  deleterious  and  discriminatory  [45].  This  means  that  the  generated  content  may  not  be  a  good  fit  for  the  diverse  groups  [12].  This  could  potentially  negatively  impact  the  learning  experience  of  these  diverse  groups  within  the fragile  educational  context.
- Lack  of  Domain-Specific  expertise: The  massive  pre-training  corpora  provide LLMs  information  but  have  limits.  Lack  of  current  information  and  specialized topic  expertise  are  the  fundamental  limitations  [46].  Employing  LLM  for  educational  content  creation  would  require  the  LLM  base  models  to  be  fine-tuned  to perform  that  task  effectively  or  add  external  information  resources.  Developing techniques  for  domain  knowledge  injection  is  critical.
- Recency  Challenges: LLMs  have  a  limited  capacity  for  up-to-date  knowledge besides  the  lack  of  domain-specific  information.  The  factually  correct  information  incorporated  in  LLMs  has  obvious  temporal  limitations  and  might  become obsolete  over  time  [46].  After  training,  these  models  do  not  update  their  internal information  [45].  This  is  a  dilemma,  given  our  world's  dynamic  and  ever-changing character.  When  faced  with  queries  that  transcend  their  time  limit  [37],  LLMs  often resort  to  falsifying  facts  or  presenting  answers  that  may  have  been  valid  in  the past  but  are  now  obsolete  [47].
- Interpretability  and  Explainability: LLMs  do  not  explain  their  outputs  when prompted.  Understanding  the  decision-making  process  of  LLMs  for  their  outputs is  a  crucial  issue  in  the  field  of  Natural  Language  Processing.  The  capability  to

- interpret  the  LLM  output  is  essential,  especially  for  education  in  fragile  environments.  Firstly,  it  can  foster  trust  [45].  It  supports  identifying  and  mitigating  biased outputs  by  teachers  and  learners  in  such  environments.
- Security  and  Privacy: LLM  integration  in  numerous  use  cases  raises  worries about  data  privacy, security ,  and  possible  abuse.  This  is  because  LLMs  are  trained on  readily  available  data  on  the  Internet,  some  of  which  are  private  data.  Yang et  al.  [47]  advocate  for  LLM  security  and  privacy  awareness  to  increase  trust.
- Expensive  Nature  of  Building  LLMs: LLMs  are  computationally  costly  and data-hungry,  making  them  challenging  to  deploy,  particularly  in  real-world  applications  with  limited  data  and  processing  resources  [47].  Building,  training,  and fine-tuning LLMs  require  extensive resources [4], which  constitute expensive computational  devices  [48].  Moreover,  the  process  has  highly  detrimental  environmental  effects  due  to  the  high  energy  required.  This  implies  that  retraining  the models  for  use  cases  in  refugee  environments  may  not  be  a  viable  option.

## 2 Overview  of  Retrieval-Augmented  Generation  (RAG)

Retrieval-augmented  generation  (RAG)  is  a  significant  breakthrough  in  the  field  of Artificial  Intelligence,  namely  in  enhancing  the  capacities  of  LLMs  [49-51].  The RAG  method  uses  external  data  in  real-time  during  the  text  generation  process  and effectively gets around many  of the problems  that come  with traditional  LLMs, such  as  issues  with  content  relevance,  timeliness,  and  factual  accuracy  [30].  RAG combines  LLMs'  fluent  and  coherent  text  generation  with  the  capacity  to  collect  and utilize  appropriate  external  knowledge  sources  [52].  RAG  can  obtain  and  use  the  most relevant  and  up-to-date  material  from  an  internally  maintained  knowledge  corpus, a  significant  benefit  [51].  In  short,  RAG  separates  parametric  and  non-parametric information.  The  model  parameters  describe  the  model's  interpretation  and  generalization  of  training  data.  External  sources  like  vector  databases,  which  offer  updatable information,  provide  non-parametric  knowledge.  This  connection  allows  LLMs  to access  domain-specific  information,  improving  answer  accuracy  and  relevance.

## 2.1 RAG  Components

## Retrieval  Component

Retrieval  refers  to  identifying  and  acquiring  pertinent  information  in  response  to  a specific  need.  Let's  concentrate  on  information  resources,  which  resemble  a  keyvalue  store  where  each  key  corresponds  to  a  specific  value  [53].  The  goal  is  to  use a similarity function  to find the  most  similar  keys to a given  query  and  get their corresponding  values  [54].  The  retriever  plays  a  crucial  role  in  the  RAG  system, efficiently  and  promptly  locating  pertinent  documents  or  data  from  a  vast  collection based  on  a  specified  query  [52].  The  primary  function  of  this  system  is  to  analyze the  documents  and  determine  which  ones  are  most  relevant  to  the  given  query  [49]. Retriever  techniques  may  be  classified  into  sparse,  dense,  and  other  categories  based on  various  similarity  functions.

Conventional retrieval techniques, like the BM25  algorithm, prioritize word frequency and presence when ranking documents, sometimes disregarding the semantic  content  of  searches  [50].  However,  most  current  solutions  use  pre-trained LMs  such  as  BERT  to  capture  the  semantic  meaning  of  queries  more  efficiently  [49]. These  models  increase  the  precision  of  search  results  by  considering  synonyms  and word  syntax,  resulting  in  improved  document  ranking  by  identifying  semantic  resemblances  [50].  Typically,  this  process  involves  calculating  vector  distances  between pages  and  queries  [55].  This  involves  combining  standard  retrieval  measures  with semantic comprehension to provide relevant search results that align with user intention.

## Generation  Component

The  generator  component  is  responsible  for  generating  text  relevant  to  the  query and  accurately  [54]  representing  the  information  obtained  from  the  retrieved  documents  [50].  The  generator  typically  combines  the  query  with  the  returned  data  and inputs  it  into  an  LLM  for  text  generation  [50].  While  ensuring  that  the  produced  text aligns  accurately  with  the  retrieved  information  is  challenging,  it  is  crucial  to  strike  a balance  between  strictly  sticking  to  the  original  material  and  injecting  the  output  with innovation  [52].  The  produced  text  must  faithfully  represent  the  information  from  the obtained  documents  and  correspond  to  the  goal  of  the  user  query  [30].  Furthermore, it  should  facilitate  the  incorporation  of  new  perspectives  or  insights  into  the  gathered data  [56].  We  achieve  these  through  customizing  and  enhancing  processes  [50].

## 2.2 RAG  Performance  Mechanisms

The  RAG  system  has  two  fundamental  components:  The  retriever  and  the  generator. The  retriever  is  responsible  for  searching  for  relevant  information  from  the  data  store, usually  a  vector  database,  while  the  generator  provides  the  needed  content.  Here's how  the  RAG  process  operates:  The  retriever  first  accepts  the  user  input  query  and searches  for  appropriate  information.  Next,  the  generator  receives  the  original  query and  retrieval  results  through  a  specified  augmentation  process.  Lastly,  the  generator generates  the  necessary  outcomes.

## 2.3 The  Role  of  Prompt  Engineering

Prompt  engineering  is  a  way  to  communicate  with  the  LLMs.  It  is  one  of  the  most important  skills  for  LLM  interactions  today  [57].  Prompts  augment  LLMs  with  taskspecific  cues,  allowing  them  to  adapt  to  new  tasks  [58].  LLMs  receive  instructions  to guarantee  generating  specific  content  qualities  in  the  output  [59].  Prompt  engineering is  the  process  of  crafting,  iteratively  refining,  and  optimizing  prompts  to  define  the user's intention for the LLM  [60].  Users  can help  LLMs  bypass  limitations  and restrictions  by  meticulously  designing  and  refining  the  prompts  [61].

LLMs  are  stimulated  using  a  variety  of  techniques.  For  instance,  prompt  engineering  uses  zero-shot  or  few-shot  settings,  eliminating  the  requirement  for  massive task-specific  datasets  [57].  In  the  few-shot  scenario,  the  model  gets  one  or  more  task samples  and  a  natural  language  description,  unlike  in  the  zero-shot  configuration [62].  Both  approaches  depend  on  LLMs'  in-context  learning  capacity  to  accomplish a  downstream  job  based  on  natural  language  instruction  and,  optionally,  a  few  task examples  [63].  In-context  learning  uses  a  prompt  to  condition  a  pre-trained  model on  a  downstream  task,  whereas  prompt  engineering  finds  effective  prompts.  It  is  a form  of  prompting  that  involves  exposing  the  model  to  a  series  of  interconnected instances or prompts, allowing it to acquire knowledge  and  generalize  based on the  given  context  [58].  Retrieval-based  prompting  is  another  strategy  that  involves selecting  prompts  or  contexts  using  retrieval  methods  [58].  This  strategy  involves  the model  retrieving  pertinent  prompts  or  context  from  a  pool  of  prompts  or  an  external knowledge  base  to  direct  its  generation  or  decision-making  process.

## 2.4 Benefits  of  REALMs  in  Educational  Content  Creation

LLMs'  primary  concerns  are  hallucinations,  a  lack  of  domain-specific  knowledge, and  the  production  of  biased  educational  content.  The  leading  causes  revolve  around the  quality  of  training  data  and  the  failure  to  access  up-to-date  content  sources.  RAG tackles  these  challenges  by  retrieving  relevant  document  chunks  from  an  external information base via semantic similarity computations [49]. It supplements the inherent training data with updated information from outside sources, retrieving accurate  information  [50].  External  information  is  conceptualized  as  non-parametric memory  that  is  easy  to  change  and  contains  broad,  long-tail  knowledge  [53].  This means  integrating  Retrieval-Augmented  LLMs  (REALMs)  in  creating  educational content for fragile educational environments  such  as refugee  camps  comes  with several  benefits.  Firstly,  it  ensures  the  production  of  accurate  and  contextually  relevant  content.  This  is  because  many  challenges  may  arise  in  their  deployment  within the  fragile  contexts  of  refugee  education  due  to  these  models'  inherent  hallucinatory behaviors  and  limitations  in  comprehending  the  nuanced  contexts  of  learners.  The lack  of  intrinsic  understanding  regarding  these  learners'  sociocultural,  linguistic,  and educational  backgrounds  significantly  hinders  the  efficient  and  ethical  generation  of educational  content  by  LLMs  [64].

Consequently,  it  may  lead  to  the  creation  of  instructional  materials  that  are  inaccurate,  misrepresentative,  or  disparate.  Second,  the  RAG  technique  improves  the transparency  of  the  generated  outputs  by  allowing  the  model  to  refer  to  and  identify data  sources.  This  significantly  promotes  user  confidence  in  automated  systems  [65]. Transparency  plays  a  crucial  role  in  establishing  credibility  and  trustworthiness  in AI-powered  tools,  as  users  often  want  assurance  about  the  accuracy  and  origin  of the  information  they  receive.  Thirdly,  RAG  presents  more  cost-effective  access  to  a handy  AI  tool  as  an  alternative  to  building  a  customized  LLM  due  to  the  resources required  for  this  task  [50].  RAG  uses  a  more  dynamic  approach  where  up-to-date educational  content  is  integrated  into  the  LLM  via  pre-trained  LMs  rather  than  finetuning  or  building  an  entirely  new  LLM  [30,  66,  67].  Lastly,  RAG  offers  LLMs  a flexible  and  scalable  feature,  making  them  available  for  various  tasks.  For  instance, if  provided  with  similar  information  in  its  external  information  reservoir,  they  could generate  lesson  plans,  assessment  items,  reading  texts,  and  quizzes.

## 3 REALMs  Use  Cases  in  Educational  Content  Creation

Previous  studies  demonstrate  various  use  cases  for  REALMs  in  educational  content creation  at  multiple  levels  of  education.

## 3.1 Challenges  and  Limitations  of  REALMs  for  Educational Content  Creation

Despite significant advancements  in  RAG,  there  remains  a need to optimize the balance  between  retrieval  speed  and  the  depth  of  accessible  information  [65].  This  is especially  important  for  sustaining  interest  in  real-time  interactive  systems.  Including RAG also  raises  computing  performance  and  scalability  issues,  underscoring  the  need for  more  optimization.

- The  Effect  of  Retrieval  Quality: The  introduction  of  RAG  in  LLM-generated educational  content  mainly  ensures  that  the  content  generated  is  factually  correct and  relevant  to  the  user's  query.  However,  the  RAG  LLMs  rely  heavily  on  the retriever's quality  [75].  The  relevance  and  quality  of  the  content  stored  on  the retrieval source  determine  the  output's  accuracy.  If  the  retrieval model  fails to retrieve  contextually  relevant  information  relevant  to  the  user  query  correctly,  then the  LLM  produces  irrelevant  and  possibly  factually  incorrect  information  [37]. This  means  that  the  overall  dependency  on  the  retrieval  component's  performance causes  severe  bottlenecks  that  require  serious  attention.

- The  Cost  of  Scalability: RAG  LLMs  often  require  access  to  extensive  corpora  to extract  pertinent  information  efficiently.  Handling  and  retrieving  these  extensive datasets  might  provide  considerable  scaling  obstacles.  Deploying  RAG  systems, especially  with  extensive  knowledge  bases,  may  need  significant  computational resources,  storage  capacity,  and  data  preparation  [75].  Ensuring  efficient  resource use  and  cost-effectiveness  while  retaining  performance  is  crucial  for  implementing RAG  solutions.
- The  Complexity  of  Integrating  with  RAG-Based  LLMs: Integrating  RAGbased LLM  solutions with pre-existing procedures and educational technologies  is  challenging.  The  complex  architectural  details  required  to  coordinate  the retrieval  and  generation  components  of  the  RAG  LLM  might  be  overwhelming [75].  Advanced  coordination  mechanisms  are  necessary  for  efficient  integration to  ensure  the  smooth  assimilation  of  acquired  knowledge  into  the  creative  process. The  system's  intricacy  may  result  in  longer  development  periods,  higher  implementation  costs,  and  difficulty  troubleshooting  and  optimizing  the  system.  Moreover,  the  need  for  multidisciplinary  proficiency  in  retrieval  techniques  and  generative  models  contributes  to  the  complexity  of  integration.  This  can  become  a  hurdle in  environments  where  there  is  less  expertise,  thus  hindering  adoption.
- The  Effect  of  Domain  Specificity: RAG  LLM  exhibits  high  levels  of  domain specificity, indicating that its performance  may  drastically  differ according  to the  individual  application  area.  For  instance,  RAG  LLM  systems  may  be  finetuned  and  supported  for  retrieving  and  generating  medical  information,  but  a  new user  may  wish  to  use  them  for  creating  mathematical  assessment  items.  When used  in  highly  specialized  domains,  RAG  systems  trained  on  data  specific  to  that domain  may  achieve  high  accuracy  and  relevance.  This  is  because  the  retrieval component  is  more  inclined  to  retrieve  relevant  information,  while  the  generative model  can  generate  contextually  appropriate  replies. Nevertheless,  the  narrow focus  of  this  domain  specialization  might  also  restrict  the  applicability  of  RAG systems  to broader contexts. Models  trained on  domain-specific  datasets  may require  assistance  in  achieving  high  performance  in  broader  or  diverse  settings because  they  may  fail  to  extract  or  create  reliable  information  outside  their  original training  domain.  The  challenge  in  developing  and  deploying  RAG  LLMs  is  finding a  balance  between  specialized  knowledge  in  each  topic  and  the  capacity  to  apply that  knowledge  to  other  situations.
- The  Complexity  of  Evaluating  RAG-Based  LLMs: A thorough evaluation is  required  when  developing  RAG  LLMs,  there  is  a  need  for  thorough  evaluation. Assessing  the  effectiveness of  RAG  LLMs  presents  distinct  difficulties. The  conventional  measures  used  for  retrieval,  such  as  accuracy  and  recall,  and generation,  such  as  BLEU  and  ROUGE,  may  not  comprehensively  measure  the efficacy  of  the  RAG  LLM  system  [75].  There  is  a  need  for  assessment  frameworks  that  comprehensively  address  how  retrieval  and  generating  components interact  with  one  another.  Furthermore,  assessment  techniques  that  prioritize  the needs  and  preferences  of  users,  like  those  in  refugee  educational  environments, and  measure  their  usefulness  and  satisfaction  are  crucial.  Still,  they  might  require significant  resources  and  may  include  subjective  judgements.

## 4 Discussion  and  Future  Directions

This chapter's main aim is to explore how  to  leverage  REALMs  transformative potential in educational content creation while mitigating bias and hallucinatory behavior  and  ensuring  retrieval  of  up-to-date  content  in  a  specific  domain  of  refugee education.  The  results  of  this  study  show  that  using  retrieval-augmented  LLMs  can help ensure that refugee education content is top-notch, correct, and relevant to culture  and  context.  The  analysis  from  Table  1  shows  that  REALMs  technologies could  be  leveraged  to  provide  personalized  learning,  improve  assessment,  provide career  guidance,  and  improve  the  overall  learning  experiences  for  STEM  education.

These  findings  emphasize  the  transformative  potential  of  REALMs  to  revolutionize the creation of instructional materials, particularly for marginalized and heterogeneous groups of learners such as refugees.  REALMs  support  achieving Society  5.0  aspirations  by  generating  personalized  learning  content.  Nevertheless, this  study  builds  upon  already  established  foundations  by  addressing  the  tedious  task of  creating  appropriate  learning  content,  especially  for  refugee  learners.  This  demographic  has  received  little  attention  in  RAG  and  LLM  applications.  Prior  research has  focused  on  the  broad  applications  of  RAG  in  generating  content,  but  this  chapter explicitly  highlights  its  significance  in  promoting  inclusion  and  cultural  sensitivity in  educational  materials.

This  study  has  flaws  despite  its  numerous  merits,  including  its  focus  on  the  oftenoverlooked  refugee  community  and  its  examination  of  REALMs  in  the  educational space. The lack of empirical data and bias-free, well-curated corpora limits the research's  ability  to  build,  fine-tune,  and  incorporate  the  RAG  pipeline  for  educational  content  generation.  In  addition,  RAG-based  LLM  efficacy  is  still  constrained  by context  window  size,  the  cost  of  scaling  up,  a  lack  of  expertise  in  these  marginalized communities,  and  dealing  with  noise  issues  that  may  be  the  source  of  misinformation, among  others.

Future  studies  should  prioritize  developing  and  verifying  unbiased  data  collections  for  use  in  REALMs  to  produce  culturally  sensitive  instructional  material.  As the  context  windows  of  LLMs  increase  enormously,  it  will  be  crucial  to  modify  RAG frameworks  to  maximize  their  efficiency  with  nearly  limitless  context  widths.  It  is essential  to  explore  approaches  that  may  improve  the  resilience  of  RAG  against  noise and  disinformation.  This  includes  the  development  of  strategies  to  filter  or  reduce  the influence  of  hostile  inputs.  Furthermore,  research  should  investigate  several  methodologies,  including  RAG  with  fine-tuning,  sequential,  alternating,  or  end-to-end  joint training,  to  identify  the  most  efficient  technique.

## 5 Conclusion

In this work, we examined  the transformative potentials  of  REALMs  in  educational  content  creation  while  mitigating  bias  and  hallucinatory  behavior  and  ensuring retrieval  of  up-to-date,  culturally,  and  contextually  relevant  educational  content  for refugee  education.  We  demonstrate  the  use  of  retrieval-augmented  LLMs  to  generate equitable  learning  content  in  refugee  education.  We  demonstrate  that  fine-tuning  base models  with  high-quality  bias-free  corpora  and  supplementing  the  architecture  with retrieval  augmentation  can  assist  in  tackling  bias  issues,  hallucinatory  behaviors,  and recency  challenges  inherent  in  non-task-specific  LLMs.  The  findings  from  our  study provide  a  significant  guide  for  academics  and  practitioners  in  the  field  of  education, particularly  in  challenging  environments.  These  insights  may  assist  them  in  creating REALMs  for  instructional  material  production.  We  anticipate  that  the  insights  from this  research  will  drive  future  research  and  innovation  in  the  realm  of  REALMs  technologies,  fostering  equitable  and  inclusive  learning  and  aiding  in  the  achievement  of SDG4.  Our  goal  is  to  expedite  instructional  material  development  by  incorporating retrieval  techniques  and  language  models,  thereby  broadening  the  scope  of  possible outcomes.  We  expect this work  to serve as a fundamental  foundation  for future research  endeavors,  fostering  the  development  of  more  sophisticated,  adaptable,  and contextually  aware  instructional  material  production.

## Abstract

The political and social instability in many parts of the world has led to large numbers of refugees forced to find a haven in Europe.  Both  social  and  political  pressure  is  felt  in  these  host  countries  as  they  see  the  resulting  situation  worsening dramatically.  In  this  article,  an  analysis  of  the  geo-sociological  assumptions  within  the  conventional  conception  of  social inclusion is undertaken. As a result, an alternative is presented based upon the knowledge-based, network-oriented nature of  modern  European  society.  This  is  referred  to  as  Virtual  Social  Inclusion  and  it  aims  primarily  at  two  fundamental dimensions  of  human  activity:  education  and  employment.  The  article  focuses  on  the  nature  and  implications  of  VSI  for inclusive  education  and  its  underlying  principles,  particularly  mobility  and  openness.  It  also  details  the  educational framework needed, and what VSI requires to turn from a theoretical framework into a practical reality and the foreseeable implications of its implementation are explored.

## 1. Introduction

The refugee problem that came to a peak in 2015 is not going away any time soon. Recent data from UNHCR (2017) show that there are around 68.5 million forcibly displaced people, of whom 25.4 million are refugees. The number of displaced people is increasing by 44,400 every day. The principal objective  of  most,  if  not  all  of  these  people,  is  to  reach  and  find  haven  and  a  better  future  in  an economically and politically stable country. However, the flow of refugees into Europe is ever-more complicated as time passes. The problem is that not all European countries are in a good position to integrate  such  larger  numbers  of  people  into their  socioeconomic  reality.  Their  economies  are  in  a plateau and any possible influx to share the already limited resources, and even put in jeopardy the existing  status-quo,  is  politically  unpopular  and  risks  a  backlash  from  a  significant  part  of  their population. Therefore, borders are closed, countries reject new refugees regardless of high-level EU agreements about admission quotas, fear of terrorism increases, and many cases of xenophobia have arisen, etc.

For these reasons, while the number of people forced to leave their countries increases with time, the majority of these refugees are unable to progress on their journeys to their destination of choice, and  are  stuck  in  holding  camps  somewhere  along  their  route  to  Europe  (De  la  Chaux,  Haugh,  &amp; Greenwood, 2018). Their immediate safety and survival needs are more or less met but there are little if  any  possibilities  for  them  to  find  the  psychological  determination  and  economic  resources  that would enable them to move out of the camps and obtain better living conditions. Furthermore, with the  continued increase in the number of refugees arriving, the under-resourced camps where they stay are rapidly filling up, making living conditions very difficult and the overall situation unsustainable. While the objective of facilitating their migration should not be relinquished and must remain a priority for the European Commission and member state governments, it seems urgent to look for a more effective way to support and empower these communities and end their suffering, once and for all. Many displaced people have chosen neither the host country nor the camps they are stuck in. Furthermore, in terms of their integration profiles, while some are ready to leave their homes on a permanent basis, many others want to go back when the situation allows it. Those who have managed to get beyond camps into countries, where they can eke out a meagre existence, are often there  because  there  was  no  better  alternative.  They  feel  culturally  isolated,  have  a  distorted  selfidentity caused by a lack of connection with both the people in the new country and those whom they left behind, a circumstance of great emotional and psychological distress that can occasionally lead to radicalization.

There  are  many  charities,  Non-Governmental  Organizations  (henceforth,  NGOs)  and  support groups already helping refugees with a wide range of difficulties, ranging from basic survival needs in the holding camps, through to education, employment and social inclusion. A particularly innovative example is  that  of  Techfugees 1 ,  an  NGO  coordinating  the  response  of  the  international  technology community to provide solutions for and with displaced people. It would be unfair to say that progress is not being made. However, something more fundamental is required that can be applied to help the refugees in the camps and goes beyond basic survival: something that prepares them to leave and participate  in  their  new  community,  whichever  that may  be,  in  a more  equalitarian  and  productive manner. In this article, the fundamental notion of social inclusion is explored and will be analysed with a view to redefining it to reflect the knowledge-based network-oriented nature of modern European society. As a result, a proposal for how refugees can embrace this new type of social inclusion will be presented. It is argued that such an inclusion mechanism can offer refugees a pathway, helping them to  belong  to  a  larger,  more  universal  society,  more  so  than  a  geographically  bound  one,  with  the associated political, economic and psychological benefits that come with it.

According to the World Bank Group 2 ,  social  inclusion  can  be  defined  as  follows:  't he process of improving  the  terms  on  which  individuals  and  groups  take  part  in  society -improving  the  ability, opportunity,  and  dignity  of  those  disadvantaged  on  the  basis  of  their  identity.'  Obtaining  such inclusion can be seen to be a fundamental objective for refugees, as they search for new and safer places to resettle, whether temporarily or permanently. In an ideal case, they are able to reach an economically  and  politically  stable  country,  and  cover  much  more  than  their  immediate  survival needs, so that they can then begin to adapt to their new surroundings, start relevant learning/training (for  example,  language  and  job-related  skills),  and  enter  the  employment  market.  Once  they  are working, and their overall situation improves, these people will be in a position to integrate into the society where they obey laws and pay taxes, which gives them access to the same services that any other citizen has (without losing their own identity and culture of origin). However, the problem that was  noted  above  is  that,  as  the  number  of  refugees  trying  to  enter  Europe  has  increased,  certain resistance and even hostility have inevitably grown on the part of host countries, and access to them is more difficult than ever. The refugees are stranded in camps en route to Europe and stuck in limbo and, therefore, unable to move forward to a better life or backward to where they came from. Given this complex scenario, the question can be raised as to whether refugees can achieve any degree of inclusion, in the general sense of belonging to and participating in a society that provides them with the same support as anyone physically there (e.g., health care, education, employment and, at a more psycho-social level, gain a sense of identity and dignity with opportunities for self-improvement). In the  next  section,  this  question  is  considered,  and  an  answer  is  provided  in  terms  of  a  new  type  of social inclusion.

## 2. Virtual Social Inclusion

The term 'society' is used generically to refer to a grouping of people living together, in a mo re or less structured, geographically located community (Castells, 2000). However, it is argued here that it is necessary to reframe what we mean by 'society' and consider it in different terms, which replace geographical  constraints  with  other  more  contemporary  ones.  In  the  1970s,  sociologist  Daniel  Bell (1973) introduced the notion of 'information society' to refer to a social grouping around the use of knowledge  rather  than  that  of  belonging  to  any  given  country.  Castells  (2006)  notes  that  modern society has been undergoing a conceptual transformation for more than two decades, moving away from an industrial society to one based upon a new technological paradigm. Similarly, Drucker (2001) refers to 'knowledge society', which can be characterised in terms of three key elements: firstly, that it  is  borderless  (knowledge  can  flow  easily  across  computer  networks);  secondly,  that  it  possesses upward mobility (anyone with the relevant  education  can  access  it);  and  thirdly  and  finally,  that  it contains the potential for failure as well as success (access to the knowledge therein does not imply a correct and beneficial use).

While the two terms 'information society' and 'knowledge society' were used interchangeably at first,  as  David  &amp;  Foray  (2003)  and  Burch  (2006)  noted,  the  distinction  needs  to  be  emphasised between information and knowledge. These authors argue that the former refers to structured and formatted data, and the latter to the capacity to use the former for intellectual or manual action. A decade  before  them,  Courrier  (1990)  differentiated  between  the  two  terms,  where  Information society  focussed  on  the  content,  in  terms  of  collecting,  processing,  and  communication,  and Knowledge society, in terms of the agents who use it. David &amp; Foray (2003) also noted the appearance of knowledge-based communities, which they define as networks of individuals who share knowledge and skills above and beyond the limits of traditional organisations, countries, etc. As they highlighted, knowledge, when reduced to information, can travel very quickly and be accessed by large numbers of people. It can be argued, therefore, that the use of Information and Communication Technology (ICT) to  access  and  participate  in  these  knowledge-based  communities  enables  people  to  be  part  of  a knowledge society that is not limited by geographical or political constraints. Castells (2006) argues that technology is a necessary but not sufficient condition for this kind of society. He states that the use  of  information  to  generate  knowledge,  power  and  wealth  is  dependent  upon  a  structural organization of the underlying technology. David &amp; Foray (2003) discuss the skill set which is necessary for participation in this knowledge society, highlighting those related to information technology and also  soft  skills  (e.g.,  teamwork,  communication,  learning  to  learn,  knowing  what  we  do  not  know, awareness of heuristic bias that can affect reasoning, etc.). Returning  to  the  case  of  refugees,  inevitably,  the  possibilities  for  any  of  them  stuck  in  holding ca mps to achieve 'real'  social  inclusion  (henceforth,  RSI)  in  a  given  country,  in  the  sense  of  being physically located there with the equivalent rights and duties as native citizens, are extremely limited. This  starts  with  the  need  to  obtain  complex  country-specific  legal  authorization  and  related documentation, whose renewal is uncertain and, therefore, any kind of middle-term planning on their part  becomes  difficult,  if  not  impossible,  and  many  other  challenges  and  difficulties  follow,  as mentioned  above.  Given  this  extraordinarily  complex  panorama,  the  hypothesis  explored  here  is whether refugees in holding camps could use their (mobile) technology to obtain at least part of the main benefits of associating with a knowledge society, which includes access to information, exchange of  communication,  education,  and  employability,  to  name  a  few  affordances.  This  would  be  a potential alternative to refugees' forced inactivity in the camps and the enormous vital investment of time  and  effort  in  the  preparation  to  live  in  the  next  country.  It  would  presumably  increase  and diversify the countries that they could access and facilitate a more positive initial response from them. This  idea  is  not  so  far-fetched  as  it  might  initially  appear.  A  key  feature of modern  society  is  its online or networked-structure, where an ever-increasing amount of our social interaction and access to services (e.g., those related to healthcare, education and employment) takes place online. We are arguably participating in the knowledge society. Hypothetically speaking, if it were possible to scaffold and structure refugee access to these online services, and provide them with the relevant skills and tools, they might be able to meet an important part of their needs. This would also provide them with a sense of belonging to a large online social community that would arguably facilitate a new sense of identity, without having to lose the old one, dignity and opportunities for self-improvement. This form of inclusion is defined here as 'Virtual Social Inclusion' (henceforth, VSI). The use of the prefix virtual- for an existing service is not new and refers to accessing it online. For example,  the  virtual  ERASMUS+  programme 3 enables  young  people  to  participate  in  relevant intercultural experiences online, as part of their formal or non-formal education, without leaving their country of origin. Similarly, 'virtual mobility' is a term 4 that in academia refers to online experiences of collaboration between students and teachers from different institutions. Finally, 'virtual attendance', as defined by Read et al. (2009), refers to a process that uses a technological mechanism to  provide  a  considerable  part  of  the  advantages  of  attending  a  taught  class  without  the  need  for students to displace themselves to where it takes place. Apart from the shortcomings of RSI, for VSI to be  an  alternative,  its  affordances  would  need  to  be  comparable  to  those  of  RSI.  So,  the  questions should be asked: is VSI possible? If so, what would be needed for it to become a reality? The results of analyses have already been presented in the literature to demonstrate the value that ICT has for refugees who already have reached their destination of choice (e.g., Andrade &amp; Doolin; 2016). As they argue, ICT provides a bridge for refuges: 'to participate in an information society, to communicate  effectively,  to  understand  a  new  society,  to  be  socially  connected,  and  to  express  a cultural identity'. It could be a rgued that refugees already use such services to stay in contact with their families and friends in their country of origin and to find information and access other services that might be of value to them. However, while these affordances are a step toward VSI, they are not sufficient  in  and  of  themselves  (cf.  Castells,  2006).  They  are  very  limited,  and  their  access  and  use require knowledge and skills that refugees may not have, and imply expenses that they cannot meet. Online access to services in a given country can be a complicated task, sometimes even for natives, often requiring digital certificates, the use of virtual private networks, or access from restricted ranges of network addresses. It  is  argued  here  that  for  VSI  to  become  a  reality,  as  a  way  to  access  and  participate  in  the knowledge society, four things are needed: firstly, the services would have to be available mostly for free, at least to begin with, but completely online and all encompassing. Secondly, an ICT architecture would be needed to facilitate access to the services, above and beyond their presence online. Such an architecture would need to provide a safe and secure access to them, which protected identity and enabled financial services to be handled. Thirdly, a legal and political framework would be needed to support and validate the aspects of the system previously highlighted. It is for this reason that VSI cannot be proposed in a vacuum. Since many refugees are trying to access the Eurozone, it would make sense that initially this model was contextualised and piloted at a European level, where existing legal and political support mechanisms could scaffold the refugees and their presence online. Fourthly and  finally,  training  and  support  would  be  required  to  help  the  refugees  learn  to  use  VSI  and  its related  services. The  reality  of  refugees  living  in  camps  is  far  from  ideal  in  this  respect.  Their  large number would require a scalable and robust architecture, where they could use their mobile devices to register with the system and subsequently be provided with recommended services that could help them  right  from  the  beginning  to  access  administrative  processes,  learning  resources  and  obtain guidance  regarding  the  best  ways  to  access  other  services  (such  as  employment)  online.  At  this moment  in  time,  the  opportunities  for  online  employment  are  distributed  between  different employment portals within Europe. For example, EURES 5 lists over a million jobs, some of which can be undertaken online. Other sites like EuroJobs 6 , Indeed 7 , and thebalancecarrers 8 also include online employment opportunities. These are examples of portals that list job vacancies only in English, but there  are  others  that  include  offers  in  other  European  languages.  The  problems  that  refugees currently  encounter  with  such  online  employment  services  are  the  presence  of  related  but  largely irrelevant  content  intermingled  in  a  confusing  maze  of  information  and,  above  all,  the  divergence between the  underlying  'Western  bias'  of  the  information  and  the  diverse  social,  e ducational,  and technological cultures of all these different potential users.

Finally,  it  should  also  be  noted  that  such  a  proposal  cannot  take  the  form  of  a  'backdoor'  for refugees  to  participate  in  an  'underground  economy'  in  EU  countries,  in  such  a  wa y  that  it undermines the education and employment possibilities of nationals there. The scenarios need to be sustainable and beneficial both for refugees and for the European countries that facilitate the need for such a workforce. This VSI initiative would arguably be well received by all parties involved, displaced people, the European Commission, and its member states, since it would provide the much-needed support without depending upon physical presence in the countries which, as was noted above, has multiple problems associated. In the next section an educational framework is described that can be seen to be central to the success of VSI.

## 3. An Educational Framework for Virtual Social Inclusion

VSI, as defined in the previous section, could enable displaced people to participate in the evolving knowledge society that unites and underlies European countries, without requiring them to physically be  in  any  of  them.  This  form  of  inclusion,  in  a  similar  way  to  other  'virtual'  processes  (Virtual ERASMUS+, Virtual Mobility, Virtual Attendance, etc.), would arguably help refugees to attain some of the advantages of RSI, sparing them the many efforts and anxieties involved when fleeing from their country  of  origin  nowadays.  Key  requirements  to  be  able  to  make  use  of  VSI  are  possessing  the relevant  language  communicative  competences  and  technical  and  work-related  skills,  not  only  to access  the  infrastructural  services  of  the  given  country,  but  also  to  undertake  the  employment opportunities  encountered  there.  In  order  for  the  refugees  to  meet  these  requirements,  an educational framework is required as part of VSI.

While a small proportion of refugees might be bi/multilingual and possess the necessary skill-set to work online,  if  they  were  able  to  find  such  work,  most  do  not.  In  a  way,  this  is  paradoxical,  since education is required to help refugees learn the relevant knowledge and skills but, generally speaking, education  is  not  free.  If  they  could  engage  in  VSI  and  work  online,  they  could  earn  the  money necessary to pay for their education. However, the problem, and hence the source of the paradox, is that they lack the knowledge and skills to get started. Open educational resources (OER) and practices (OEP) could represent a way to avoid this paradox which, when accessed from their mobile devices, would provide them with a degree of 'mobile openness' to obtain what they need. Such education, if appropriately structured, scaffolded and certified, could enable refugees to begin a VSI process. Once they have a basic revenue stream, the open education (and other freely accessible services) could be supplemented with paid alternatives. This would be an iterative process, where OER and OEP could help refugees to start to gain the relevant language and technical skills and, therefore, obtain work online  in  due  course.  Once  they  have  an  income,  they  can  carry  on  using  such  free  services  or, alternatively, pay to gain access to more specific and relevant ones, improve their competences, and encounter better employment. Presenting open education as an appropriate solution to help refugees make the most of VSI is to grossly simplify the issue. The OER and related OEP movements have been around for a very long time in  a  large  range  of  different  forms  (e.g.,  Geser,  2007;  Bliss  &amp;  Smith,  2017).  Of  all  the  initiatives undertaken,  it  is  arguably  massive  open  online  courses  (MOOCs)  that  constitute  the  learning  and training modality with the most noticeable impact. These online courses combine both OER and OEP in  a  structured  and  temporised  fashion.  While  the  literature  highlights  a  range  of  limitations  with these courses (e.g., Atiaja &amp; Proenza, 2016), the very large body of academic literature about MOOCs, and  the  immense  volume  of  courses  and  participants  are  a  tribute  to  their  staying  power  and relevance in the modern educational landscape. However, even though MOOCs are free and offer a viable general-purpose approach to online learning, they are not typically being selected by refugees as a chosen vehicle for learning. Why should that be the case? The following seven reasons can be provided as an answer to this question (Read, 2014; Read &amp; Barcena, 2014; Read et al., 2018). Firstly, most MOOCs are xMOOCs and have a pedagogical structure that resembles that of standard online courses. As such, these courses are not adapted to the learning styles of most refugees. For example, those that come from the Middle East or North of Africa are often more accustomed to a face-to-face oral approach to learning and, as experience shows, they can have  difficulties  following,  or  be  reluctant  to  participate  in,  an  eminently  written,  often  rather impersonal, online course. Secondly, a new type of digital literacy is required that encompasses the relation that refugees have with online resources and activities (Traxler, 2018; Traxler et al., 2018). An important part of learning in a MOOC requires interpersonal interaction in the courses, especially but not  only  in  cMOOCs.  The  social  nature  of  MOOCs  is  common  in  these  massive  courses  with  a potentially  enormous  imbalance  in  the  teacher  to  participant  ratio.  For  a  course  to  succeed  under these  circumstances,  a  common  interrelational  structure  and  a  certain  level  of  trust  between  the participants  are  needed,  giving  rise  to  what  is  known  as  a  form  of  'collective  intelligence'  (Levy  &amp; Bononno, 1997). Furthermore, a lot of refugees are not comfortable with online interaction and, if they have been forced to leave their countries of origin and are concerned that they might be made to return, they may  not  want  to  undertake  any  activities  online  that  might  reveal  their  identities.  Fortunately, progress  has  been  made  in  this  area  thanks  to  the  development  of  Blockchain,  that  can  act  as  a security layer that facilitates such online interaction while, at the same time, protecting their identity. As Grech &amp; Camilleri (2017) observe, this distributed system of cryptographically linked records offers a number of advantages over current online data storage and sharing mechanisms, which include selfsovereignty, trust, transparency and provenance, immutability, disintermediation, and collaboration. By using a blockchain, it would be possible for refugees to store their sensitive personal information online and share what they want with whoever they want, without running any security risks. Thirdly, the certification model present for most MOOCs does not provide refugees with learning results that are easily accredited by formal educational institutions or employers. Furthermore, many MOOC participants find the recognitio n of status in the community, in terms of 'Karma', badges, etc., to be of little value in the 'real world'. A solution to this problem may come from work that is currently being undertaken in the area of micro-certificates, as a general-purpose certification model that could enable such learning to be recognised and generally accredited internationally (Camilleri &amp; Rampelt, 2018). The granularity of the educational process here is vital, since a refugee will be more willing to study and become accredited if a single certificate leads to some kind of tangible employment. Further certificates  can  be  gained  in  an  iterative  process  as  part  of  a  modular  study  programme.  Any employment  in  a  VSI  context  would  need  to  be  undertaken  and  remunerated  completely  online. Furthermore, once a refugee is earning money, s/he can more easily access other services, being able to actually pay for them. This also has to be an iterative process: early engagement would need to be supported  locally  at  refugee  camps,  where  pilot  programmes  can  be  undertaken  to  help  refugees learn  online,  so  that  they  can  subsequently  find  and  undertake  work  online.  As  their  experience grows, the sophistication of their interactions is expected to increase as well.

6 https://www.eurojobs.com

Fourthly, most refugees have had to flee their countries in a hurry and were only able to bring with them a few belongings. Hence, they do not have computers to use to access and study in these online courses. However, they do have mobile devices (mostly smartphones although some have tablets), but  a  lot  of  MOOCs  do  not  deploy  well  on  these  devices,  making  their  use  on  small  screens,  with sporadic network connections, limited battery life, etc., very difficult. Fifthly, refugees may not value online  learning  and  are  particularly  reluctant  to  take  part  in  MOOCs  because  their  evaluation  and certification  still  have  a  large  scope  for  improvement  and  generally  lack  official  recognition.  Sixthly, daily life in refugee camps is typically noisy and chaotic, which makes any attempt at studying, online or otherwise, difficult  to  undertake.  Seventhly  and  finally,  the  complex  traumas  and  dilemmas that refugees  have  had  to  face  often  counterbalance  the  motivation  and  effort  required  for  learning, particularly if what they will obtain at the end of their study is not an immediate part of a permanent or desired life project.

Some steps have been taken in the direction of providing an educational framework for VSI in the MOONLITE  (Massive  Open  Online  courses  eNhancing  Linguistic  and  Transversal  skills  for  social inclusion and Employability; Read et al., 2018; Traeger et al., 2018) project 9 .  Amongst other goals, it focuses on how to design, develop and apply inclusive MOOCs for displaced people, supporting their specific  needs  and  circumstances,  to  potentiate  their  RSI  through  education  and  access  to  the employment market. Such an initiative  can  be  seen to  be  a  precursor  to  the  establishment  of  VSI, since  the  majority of  the  refugees  targeted  are  already  in  the  destination  country, or on  their way there, and not representative of the majority who are in holding camps. As part of this project, one of the  partners,  UNED,  worked  closely  with  several  different  not-for-profit  organisations,  NGOs, foundations, and refugee support groups in Spain, who teach and support refugees, to identify a set of criteria to make MOOCs more socially inclusive (Traxler, Read &amp; Barcena, 2019) . This 'inclusiveness' is not a binary concept, where a course qualifies, or doesn't, as being inclusive, but reflects a scale to help calibrate the degree to which a course can be used effectively by different and diverse groups. The criteria can be classified into five categories: pedagogy, linguistics, socio-culture, technology, and institutionalization.  These  criteria  were  fine-tuned  following  a  series  of  face-to-face  pilots.  A  rubric was designed as a result of this process to enable socially inclusive Language MOOCs to be developed in  as  transparent  and  systematic  a  way  as  possible  (Read  et  al.,  in  process).  The  learning  scenarios undertaken  with  refugees  in  the  MOONLITE  project  demonstrate  that  carefully  designed  and scaffolded online courses can be effective for displaced people.

However, it should be noted that the majority of the refugees who took part in these courses were already  located  in  Spain,  their  final  destination  country.  They  were,  therefore,  motivated  to  learn Spanish and obtain the sociocultural know-how that would help them achieve RSI in this country. The question can be asked as to whether refugees stranded in camps with little or no chance of actually migrating to Spain would have had the same levels of motivation to learn. Arguably not, at least not a priori.  VSI  is  not  something  that  anyone  is  already  familiar  with,  so  in  order  for  refugees  to  be motivated to learn, they need to be provided with dedicated support, particularly at the beginning. As mentioned above, this process would need to be established iteratively, whereby small pilot groups could  start  an  incremental  adoption  process  of  VSI,  leading  to  a  new  socioeconomic  model  based around participation in the knowledge society.

The  educational  framework  proposed  here  is  central  to  the  success  of  VSI,  since  it  provides  a mechanism  for  refugees  to  benefit  from  a  'level  playing  field',  whereby  they  can  gain  the  basic knowledge and skills necessary to compete in the knowledge society. Furthermore, it can also provide the training required as the needs of such a society change over time as part of a life-long learning process. There is room in such a framework for a range of educational services, the majority of which are free, but others can be paid for, something noted above that can become possible as refugees earn money.

## 4. Conclusions

In  this  article,  the  realities  of  the  refugee  situation  have  been  presented  together  with  the difficulties that they encounter when they try to reach and settle in their destination countries. It has been  argued  that  what  has  been  referred  to  here  as  real  social  interaction,  or  RSI,  is  unlikely  to happen for the majority of these people. Given the extended nature of the information society within Europe,  and  the  emerging  knowledge  society,  where  the  online  economy  represents  an  important part of our modern lives, RSI could, at least to some extent, be replaced by its virtual counterpart, termed virtual social inclusion, or VSI. For VSI to become a reality, four things are required. Firstly, relevant  open educational services would need to be available online. In a general sense, learning, training,  and  support,  based  around  OER  and  OEP,  are  the  backbone  of  this  framework,  to  help refugees acquire the knowledge, languages and technical skills necessary to participate online in the knowledge  society.  In  more  specific  terms,  the  educational  materials,  activities,  and  certification would have to be disaggregated to provide a modular structure that enables refugees to learn what

they require for their immediate needs, both linguistic and skill-related, with a view to employment. As noted above, micro-credentials play an essential role here, since they provide a way for refugees to have an 'open educational passport' logging what they have learnt. Secondly, online employment contracts would need to be available. The record of a person's learning is conceived to be a necessary entry point for gaining an online employment contract. Once a contract is successfully completed, a refugee  would  be  remunerated  and  gain  recognition  within  the  VSI  community.  This  would complement  any  recorded  learning  in  the  passport  and  serve  to  further  guarantee  his/her  skills. Thirdly,  a  service-based  technological  infrastructure  would  be  needed  to  manage  the  two  previous points  and  collate  and  highlight  the  relevant  services  for  refugees  (e.g.,  learning  and  employment opportunities),  and  guarantee  the  contractual  and  economic  details.  As  has  been  noted  previously here, given that the majority of refugees have mobile devices as their only access to online services, it is essential that the VSI framework deploys well on them, making use of mobile openness. Fourthly, a legal and political framework would be needed for refugees to work online. In the same way that the personal  details  of  refugees  are  protected  here,  there  would  be  a  need  to  guarantee  that  the companies that offer online employment contracts are both provided with the tools to remunerate for said activities and held responsible that payment is actually made. The technology for VSI largely exists although some engineering development will be needed. The

Internet  can  provide  the  backbone  to  access  the  structured  online  services  and  a  recommender system could help the refugees receive information on services that would be most relevant to them. Exactly what services the refugees will require depends upon their personal circumstances, previous learning  and  work  experience.  Their  precarious  social  and  economic  situation  within  the  holding camps would make it necessary for them to receive considerable scaffolding to begin with and make extensive use of free educational and support services. Once they begin to work and receive economic rewards, they can make use of other paid services. In order to protect their anonymity but let them interact online at the same time, a blockchain solution would be required to store personal data about the refugees and their relation to VSI. Economic transactions in this framework, such as remuneration for completed contracts, could be handled via standard banks with online services where a refugee has an account, or using cybercurrencies like Bitcoin where this is not possible. As the current form of RSI gives way to, or is complemented by, VSI, and the emerging knowledge society  becomes  consolidated,  perhaps  a  notion  of  'Virtual  Citizenship'  will  arise  to  acknowledge individuals  who  contribute  to  the  development,  welfare  and  economic  growth  of  a  given  society online, and perhaps the distinction between belonging to a geographically limited notion of society will be substituted by a broader, more 'liquid' one. A fundamental part of VSI is the presence of an educational framework that can combine free and commercial teaching and learning processes. This framework has been structured around OER and OEP in specially designed and developed inclusive MOOCs  and  will  be  extended  to  the  forthcoming  open  educational  modalities  that  will  no  doubt follow.  Since  education  is  the  universal  human  right  that  potentiates  people's  a bility  to  function effectively in society, the educational framework central to VSI will enable refugees to be on a 'level playing field' in the online knowledge society. It is only from the equality of opportunities envisaged here that we can strive together towards the limits of our human collective potential.

Abstract -As large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4 and Llama 3 become integral to educational contexts, concerns are mounting over the cultural biases, power imbalances, and ethical limitations embedded within these technologies. Though generative AI tools aim to enhance learning experiences, they often reflect values rooted in Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) cultural paradigms, potentially sidelining diverse global perspectives. This paper proposes a framework to assess and mitigate cultural bias within LLMs through the lens of applied multiplexity. Multiplexity, inspired by Senturk et al. and rooted in Islamic and other wisdom traditions, emphasizes the coexistence of diverse cultural viewpoints, supporting a multi-layered epistemology that integrates both empirical sciences and normative values. Our analysis reveals that LLMs frequently exhibit cultural polarization, with biases appearing in both overt responses and subtle contextual cues. To address inherent biases and incorporate multiplexity in LLMs, we propose two strategies: Contextually-Implemented Multiplex LLMs , which embed multiplex principles directly into the system prompt, influencing LLM outputs at a foundational level and independent of individual prompts, and Multi-Agent System (MAS)-Implemented Multiplex LLMs , where multiple LLM agents, each representing distinct cultural viewpoints, collaboratively generate a balanced, synthesized response. Our findings demonstrate that as mitigation strategies evolve from contextual prompting to MAS-implementation, cultural inclusivity markedly improves, evidenced by a significant rise in the Perspectives Distribution Score (PDS) and a PDS Entropy increase from 3.25% at baseline to 98% with the MAS-Implemented Multiplex LLMs. Sentiment analysis further shows a shift towards positive sentiment across cultures, with the MAS-Implemented Multiplex LLMs achieving 0% negative sentiment, underscoring enhanced cultural sensitivity. This pioneering study establishes a baseline for assessing and fostering cultural inclusivity in educational AI, laying the groundwork for a globally pluralistic approach that respects diverse cultural perspectives. We hope this work inspires further research toward creating AI technologies that serve a truly inclusive and multicultural educational ecosystem.

## I. INTRODUCTION

T HE rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has significantly impacted AI development, particularly as these models establish themselves as foundation models-large-scale, pre-trained systems that can be adapted for a broad array of downstream tasks [1]. Prominent examples, including GPT-4o, Claude Sonnet 3.5, Gemini 1.5

Pro, and Llama3.1 [2]-[5], are based on the Transformer architecture [6] and demonstrate high proficiency in retrievalaugmented generation (RAG), computational execution, and data synthesis. Beyond their technical achievements, these Generative AI (GenAI) models are now integral to educational AI applications, transforming learning experiences by enabling personalized support, intelligent tutoring, and resource generation [7], [8].

## A. How LLM-based Educational AI can Embody Biases?

The foundational nature of LLMs, while beneficial for scalability across tasks, also enables the propagation of inherent biases, impacting various applications and raising particular concerns in educational settings. As these models become integral to shaping learning environments globally, especially through educational platforms like KhanAmigo and Harvard's CS50 AI tutor [9], [10], it becomes crucial to evaluate not only their technical performance but also their alignment with ethical and cultural values. Many LLMs are trained on predominantly secular, materialistic, and reductionist datasets and benchmarks [11], which can inadvertently lead them to reflect biases that marginalize or overlook diverse cultural, ethical, and spiritual perspectives, especially those outside Western paradigms. These biases, far from being merely theoretical, have been shown to manifest in real-world applications, perpetuating stereotypes or reflecting negative biases toward specific communities [12]-an outcome that limits the scope of cultural and ethical diversity presented to young learners.

In global educational contexts, these biases create additional ethical and social challenges. When foundation models are applied across culturally diverse settings without sufficient adaptation, they risk misalignment with local values and worldviews, potentially alienating or misrepresenting certain communities. Recent studies have documented unintended biases in LLMs, including those related to ethnicity, cultural background, and other demographic factors [13]-[15]. These findings highlight the limitations of current LLMs in representing the pluralism inherent in human societies, underscoring the need for a framework that actively addresses these biases to support a genuinely inclusive educational AI that respects the diversity of global learners.

## B. Using Multiplexity Framework to Analyze LLMs

In this paper, we propose auditing foundation LLMs through the lens of Multiplexity , a framework proposed by Senturk et al. [16] rooted in Islamic epistemology that encompasses a pluralistic approach to knowledge by incorporating spiritual, ethical, and metaphysical dimensions [17]. The multiplexity approach serves as a corrective to the uniplex mindset, which historically emphasizes empirical and secular knowledge, often marginalizing other ways of knowing [18]. As noted by the authors in their work [17] and articulated by Senturk [16], multiplexity moves beyond a single-dimensional view of knowledge by embracing multiple layers of understanding, each valued for its unique perspective and contribution. Table I contrasts the uniplex and multiplex worldviews, highlighting key differences in knowledge, ethics, and cultural diversity, with the multiplex approach offering a more inclusive framework for culturally aligned AI [16], [17].

This study builds on the multiplexity framework to establish a robust evaluation system for assessing foundation LLMs in terms of cultural and ethical alignment across a wide range of intellectual traditions, including Western, Eastern, Islamic, African, and Latin American perspectives. Our framework systematically scans for indications of monoculture, Eurocentrism, and uniplexity-biases that often prioritize singular, predominantly Western viewpoints-thus addressing potential imbalances in LLM-generated content. By employing a MultiAgent System (MAS), where agents specializing in distinct cultural viewpoints provide insights, our framework enables a balanced synthesis of perspectives through a central Multiplex Agent that integrates the principles of multiplexity. This approach, illustrated in Figure 1, enables LLMs to generate content that is pluralistic and inclusive. Such a framework is particularly valuable in educational settings, where ensuring cultural resonance and ethical alignment is essential for fostering inclusive and diverse learning experiences.

## C. Contributions of this Paper

While previous studies have examined biases in LLMs, this paper focuses specifically on educational settings, where ethical, cultural, and local values are essential for holistic learner development. Our contribution is a culturally aligned educational AI framework, evaluating LLMs through a multiplexityinspired approach. By assessing cultural alignment, we ensure these models respect diverse identities, supporting their suitability for global educational applications. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a multiplexity framework with LLMs for a pluralistic, education-focused AI approach. This work lays the groundwork for culturally sensitive tools that foster inclusive learning across varied backgrounds.

## II. LITERATURE REVIEW

The advances in language models through LLMs have significantly reshaped natural language processing (NLP) through unsupervised training on massive corpora. Built initially on simpler models, these architectures have advanced from recurrent neural networks to transformer-based architectures, enabling LLMs to generalize beyond task-specific fine-tuning and perform effectively on diverse linguistic tasks. With this advancement, however, a critical need has emerged to scrutinize biases and ethical considerations.

## A. Bias and Ethics in LLMs

Bias in language models is a significant and welldocumented concern, affecting diverse fields such as politics, healthcare, religion, and education. Researchers categorize the associated harms into two primary types: Allocation Harm , where an AI system results in an unequal distribution of resources or opportunities, and Representation Harm , where a model reinforces stereotypes that impact societal perceptions of certain groups. As analyzed in [13], the origins of bias in LLMs can be traced to the pretraining phase, user interactions, and the broader context of the model's deployment.

A notable example of allocation harm occurs in hiring algorithms that inadvertently favor certain demographic groups over others, leading to unequal job opportunities. For instance, recruitment models trained on biased data may underrepresent qualified candidates from marginalized backgrounds, thereby perpetuating systemic inequalities. Meanwhile, an example of representation harm is documented with OpenAI's GPT3, which, in analogy-based completions involving Muslims, generated violent associations at a disproportionately high rate (66%) compared to other religious terms, as noted in [11]. These examples illustrate that, despite ongoing mitigation efforts, achieving complete neutrality is a persistent challenge, underscoring the need for continuous ethical review and a comprehensive framework to address and mitigate these imbalances in representation and power.

Cultural values shape both individuals and communities, making standardized, one-size-fits-all approaches unsuitable for addressing cultural biases in LLMs. Using the Moral Values Pluralism (MVP) framework, researchers assessed GPT3's alignment with diverse cultural narratives and found a dominant US- and Eurocentric bias, likely stemming from its predominantly English-language training data [14]. As a result, models like GPT-3 often reflect American cultural and moral values, highlighting the challenge of creating AI that fairly represents diverse perspectives.

To address cultural biases, [15] proposed persona-based prompts, encouraging LLMs to adopt perspectives aligned with specific cultural identities. Results suggested variances in moral and cultural expressions across different LLMs, with GPT-4 displaying a more secular worldview than GPT-4-turbo, which tended towards traditional values. These studies show how there's a limitation of these LLMs in representing global cultural values, thus requiring the addressing of such concerns.

## B. LLMs in Education

The integration of AI in education, particularly through LLMs [9], [10], has led to frameworks emphasizing ethical alignment and cultural sensitivity. One such framework, Multiplex AI Humanities (MAIH) [18], critiques the uniplex worldview-rooted in reductionist, secular, and empiricallydriven Enlightenment ideals-that often universalizes Eurocentric perspectives at the expense of global diversity.

In contrast, MAIH offers a pluralistic, open-civilizational framework that values both empirical and metaphysical dimensions, incorporating diverse cultural, intellectual, and religious perspectives. It is built on two pillars [16]: Multiplex Ontology , which recognizes physical, metaphysical, and divine dimensions; and Multiplex Epistemology , which includes diverse forms of knowledge, such as reason, intuition, and revelation. By respecting the multi-layered connections between technology, knowledge, and the human condition, multiplexity fosters inclusive and adaptable education that aligns with the diverse values and aspirations of communities worldwide.

Furthermore, practical applications of LLMs in education benefit from 'practical wisdom' ( phronesis ), as proposed in [19], to promote not only technical proficiency but also ethical reasoning and cultural awareness. Sawalha et al. [20] investigate how students employ ChatGPT for problem-solving, identifying effective prompting strategies that enhance accuracy and learning outcomes, with observed differences based on gender. Similarly, Johri et al. [21] discuss the transformative impact of generative AI tools in engineering education, emphasizing the importance of 'sense and knowledge checking' to prevent reliance on AI without critical oversight. Johri [22] further suggests that AI should not be viewed as a monolithic entity but as a flexible tool that requires contextual understanding to meet the needs of diverse communities.

Building on the multiplexity framework, our work presents a structured evaluation system to identify and mitigate cultural biases within LLM-generated content. By examining the educational outputs of LLMs for signs of monoculturalism, Eurocentrism, and uniplexity, we aim to support a culturally inclusive approach in AI education. Using a MAS where agents specializing in distinct cultural viewpoints provide insights to a central Multiplex Agent, our framework integrates diverse perspectives to produce pluralistic, ethically aligned educational content. This approach is essential for fostering inclusive learning experiences that respect the cultural diversity of educational settings. In the Methodology section (§III), we further discuss how we applied and implemented multiplexity principles to make LLMs more culturally inclusive.

## III. METHODOLOGY

In this study, we introduce a novel multiplexity-based AI evaluation framework designed to assess the cultural alignment of LLMs across perspectives such as Western, Eastern, Islamic, Latin American, etc. Concretely, the framework comprises three distinct strategies (1) baseline assessment; and two kinds of multiplex strategies: (2) Contextually-Implemented Multiplexity, and (3) Multi-Agent System (MAS)-Implemented Multiplexity, each employing the Perspectives Extractor, Perspective Distribution Score (PDS), and Sentiment Analyzer/ Bias Framing (see §III-C for more details) to evaluate the coverage of cultural perspectives in the models' outputs. We developed eight distinct categories of educational topics, each containing a unique set of questions (see Table [II]). In each strategy, LLMs are asked to respond to these questions, and their responses are used to extract references to each cultural perspective, which are then passed to the PDS for final score calculation, and the same responses are passed to the sentiment analyzer for bias framing towards each culture.

Fig. 2: System design for utilizing contextually-implemented multiplex LLMs to enrich baseline LLM . Baseline Assessment: LLMs respond to educational questions, and their responses are analyzed by the Perspective Extractor to identify cultural references. The Perspective Distribution Score (PDS) then quantifies the cultural representation within these responses. Contextually-implemented Multiplexity : LLMs answer similar questions with Multiplexity prompts, designed to incorporate diverse cultural perspectives. The Perspective Extractor analyzes these responses, and PDS scores are calculated to assess cultural integration. Sentiment Analysis: The sentiment and tone of LLM responses for each culture are assessed through zero-shot classification by GPT-4o, enabling bias framing across cultural perspectives.



## A. Research Questions

This study employs 'applied multiplexity' to create an evaluation system that assesses foundation LLMs for cultural and ethical alignment across diverse intellectual traditions, including Western, Eastern, Islamic, African, and Latin American perspectives. By implementing a MAS with agents specializing in distinct cultural perspectives, the framework synthesizes insights through a central Multiplex Agent, harmonizing outputs into balanced, inclusive responses. Initially, baseline cultural bias is evaluated via perspective coverage (Fig. 2), followed by applying multiplexity-informed contextual rules to ensure balanced representation. This approach enables LLMs to generate culturally resonant and ethically aligned content, which is particularly valuable in educational contexts where inclusive learning is essential. The resulting outputs support holistic educational experiences, addressing the diverse needs of learners and educators globally.

Specifically, we address three research questions (RQs):

RQ1: What forms and levels of cultural bias are present in LLMs by default, and how can these biases be effectively measured as a baseline?

RQ2: How can the Multiplexity Framework, along with a Multiplexity-inspired Multi-Agent System, be utilized to create a pluralistic approach in educational AI?

RQ3: How can the Multiplexity Framework be applied to guide LLMs in fostering positive and respectful representations of diverse cultural perspectives?

## B. Cultural Analysis and Sentiment Assessment Strategies

1) Strategy 1: Baseline Assessment: The first strategy assesses the baseline performance of various LLMs concerning cultural bias. This strategy evaluates the coverage of different cultures in the responses of these LLMs out of the box. The PDS is utilized to measure how well each perspective is represented in the models' responses, allowing for a comprehensive understanding of the cultural biases present. No additional information is provided to the models except for the questions. The output of the model is also used to extract the sentiment of each LLM towards these perspectives for bias framing (see §III-C1 and Fig. 2 for more details).

2) Strategy 2: Contextually Implemented Multiplexity: The second strategy integrates the ideas of multiplexity to provide contextual guidelines that promote balanced outputs across cultures. The core concepts of multiplexity and its rules are provided in the system prompt (context) of the models to follow for their responses. These responses are passed to the PDS to evaluate the diversity and representation of perspectives, ensuring that the outputs generated adhere to the principles of cultural inclusivity along with the sentiment of the model for bias framing and comparison with baseline assessment (More details in §III-C2 and Fig. 2).

3) Strategy 3: MAS-Implemented Multiplexity: The final strategy involves implementing a multi-agent pipeline, where agents specializing in distinct cultural perspectives give responses that are then synthesized by a Multiplex Agent according to the multiplexity principles. Each agent is asked to answer the questions with respect to its culture and these responses from agents are passed to a multiplex agent to apply the multiplexity principles ensuring a holistic viewpoint of producing knowledge. The PDS is applied to assess the coverage of each cultural perspective on the response of multiplex agent, helping to ensure a harmonious integration of diverse viewpoints. Again, the output of this system is also utilized for bias framing by analyzing the sentiment of the system towards each perspective. (see §III-C3 and Fig. 3 for more detailed implementation and working.)

In all these strategies, the PDS and Sentiment Analyzer/Bias Framing are central components that enable the framework to effectively evaluate the cultural alignment of LLMs and promote more holistic educational content. We discuss the possibility of applying these approaches during the post-training phase of the LLMs to ensure that the models are culturally inclusive and respectful of diverse viewpoints, particularly in an educational setting.

2) Strategy 2: Contextually Implemented Multiplexity: In this strategy, we crafted a system prompt that embodies the principles of Multiplexity, guiding the LLMs to operate with a nuanced, pluralistic perspective. The prompt instructs the model to adopt the persona of a critical social scientist who, while open to technological advancements, remains aware of their potential societal impacts and limitations. The second row of Figure 2 shows the proposed methodology for this strategy.

This approach incorporates key concepts from Multiplexity, including Multiplex Ontology , Multiplex Epistemology , as detailed in §II-B and proposed by authors in [16]. Our contextual design aims to foster a decision-making process that integrates diverse perspectives, respects cultural and philosophical multiplicity, and emphasizes human dignity and ethical conduct. By promoting 'both-and' thinking, this strategy encourages the model to engage in complex analyses that consider historical contexts, the wisdom of diverse traditions, and a universal outlook that values human fraternity and rights.

LLMs with this system prompt are used with the same questions Q across all topic categories T , employing the same prompts as in the baseline strategy with the same prompt template 1 to generate responses L M ( Q ) . These responses are then processed by the Perspectives Extractor, the PDS, and the Sentiment Analyzer for bias framing.

3) Strategy 3: MAS-Implemented Multiplexity: Recent studies [23]-[27] show that LLMs achieve superior performance on complex tasks when utilizing a collection of agents collaborating on different aspects of a problem, compared to using a single LLM. The concepts of collective intelligence and a collaborative approach to problem-solving, with agents negotiating to achieve an optimized solution, have demonstrated promising results. In this study, we proposed a strategy that employs Camel AI [28] framework to create an LLMbased MAS that is multi-cultural by following Multiplexity. Camel AI utilizes inception prompting techniques as part of its framework designed to facilitate task completion by guiding chat agents while ensuring that their responses remain consistent with human intentions.

Our system design, illustrated in Figure 3, leverages Camel AI's WorkForce to create a structured society of agents that collaboratively address complex tasks across diverse cultural contexts. The WorkForce employs a coordinator agent to facilitate task decomposition and distribution based on each agent's expertise. This coordinator agent, in conjunction with a task agent, dynamically assesses agent performance, identifying cases where outputs do not meet predefined standards or where repeated task failures occur. In such instances, the system adapts by reassigning tasks to agents with appropriate expertise or spawning new agents with specialized cultural focus, enhancing both task efficiency and system reliability. Each agent is developed with a culturally aligned persona (Box 2), carefully crafted to reflect specific cultural perspectives relevant to our broad question set Q across categories of educational topics T . Rather than creating static prompts (Box 1), we utilize Camel AI's Task functionality to dynamically generate culturally relevant tasks for WorkForce agents (Box 3). Each agent provides responses that reflect its assigned cultural persona and task requirements, which are then synthesized by the Multiplex Agent, guided by the Multiplexity system persona into a unified, multicultural output aimed at enriching educational content. This consolidated output, L MA ( Q ) , is subsequently evaluated by the Perspectives Extractor, PDS, and Sentiment Analyzer to assess the breadth of cultural perspectives and identify any framing biases.

4) Perspectives Extractor: The Perspectives Extractor is based on GPT-4o [2], which is used to extract all references made in the responses of the LLMs. The LLMs' responses are passed to this model to detect any references to different cultural perspectives within the output, both at the surface level and contextually based on the scenario. It is important to use a classification model that can understand content both contextually and the intent behind the content to analyze and classify the parts of the content which are referred to a culture. Since no pre-trained models are available for this specific task, we utilized GPT-4o with its impressive performance on zershot classification for this purpose.

For this step, we designed a prompt template that takes the LLMs' responses and the selected cultural perspectives, asking the model to return a Python dictionary containing all references to each cultural perspective. We have found that asking GPT-4o to provide a Python dictionary for references is more consistent and easier to manage than a text-based output, which can be inconsistent and difficult to parse.

## A. RQ1: Baseline Performance and Cultural Bias Assessment

To evaluate the baseline performance of these models in terms of cultural inclusivity, we developed a methodology aimed at uncovering hidden biases in LLM responses. Fig. 2 shows our proposed strategy for assessing the baseline performance of LLMs, specifically designed to address RQ1. Following the complete pipeline outlined in Section III-C, we compiled the results into visual representations, shown as pie charts, to facilitate understanding. Column 1 (Baseline LLMs) of Fig. 4a and Table III presents these baseline PDS for each of the four LLMs.

Across all graphs in Column 1, a distinct pattern emerges where one or at most two cultures dominate the responses, with Western cultural references overwhelmingly prevalent. This trend is also evident in Table III, which shows the PDS Entropy of just 3.25% for baseline LLMs. This reveals a narrow cultural perspective in the default LLM outputs, highlighting a Western-centric bias. Such results underscore significant cultural biases in the out-of-the-box LLMs, which are currently applied in various educational tools designed to enhance learning through Generative AI. However, our findings indicate that these LLMs, in their default configuration, heavily favor Western philosophies and ideologies, making them culturally unsuitable for educational applications without further alignment. This bias is particularly concerning for achieving globally pluralistic AI in education, as it suggests a lack of cultural pluralism in the models' knowledge base and reasoning processes.

## B. RQ2: Contextual Prompting &amp; MAS for Cultural Inclusivity

1) Contextually Implemented Multiplexity: To align the LLMs with diverse cultural viewpoints in educational responses, we embedded the core principles and operational multiplexity principles into the system prompt (context) of each LLM. This approach aimed to mitigate cultural biases by contextualizing the LLMs' responses within a pluralistic framework. Fig. 2 shows our strategy for implementing Multiplexity to reduce cultural biases. Contextually Implemented Multiplexity (the right column in Fig. 4a) shows the improved performance of multiplex LLMs when responding to educational questions. The pie charts in this column reveal a noticeable increase in the inclusion of diverse cultural perspectives, as evidenced by PDS scores for each culture rising above 0% and an increase in PDS Entropy in Table III from just 3% in baseline to 19% with this approach. This shift demonstrates that multiplex LLMs can dynamically adjust their responses to become more inclusive, integrating references and information from various cultural backgrounds. The impact of multiplexity is further evidenced by the redistribution of cultural perspectives in each graph. We observe that LLMs reduce the overemphasis on certain cultures while expanding their representation of others, indicating a genuine alignment towards balanced, multicultural responses. Multiplexity, at its core, is structured to encourage AI systems to recognize a broader range of knowledge acquisition methods-including reason, intuition, and revelation-alongside empirical evidence, accommodating diverse worldviews. These aspects resonate with many cultural traditions, in contrast to cultures where uniplex, or single-dimensional, thinking may be more common. Through multiplexity, our approach moves toward a globally pluralistic AI framework, making LLMs more suitable for educational purposes by fostering cultural respect and inclusivity in their responses. This framework not only enhances the truthfulness and relevance of AI in multicultural contexts but also supports the goal of developing an inclusive, globally accessible AI system that can serve a variety of educational needs.

2) MAS-Implemented Multiplexity: MAS is designed to tackle complex reasoning and problem-solving tasks [23][27]. While answering educational questions may seem like a simple task, the baseline assessment results in Fig. 4a clearly show that achieving cultural inclusion and integrating Multiplexity to improve LLMs for educational contexts is, in fact, a challenging goal. While adding Multiplexity in the system prompt of LLMs leads to the emergence of some new cultural references in the responses, there remains an uneven distribution, with certain cultures still disproportionately represented. This calls for an alternative approach that can reliably address this complex issue of cultural inclusion.

To tackle this, we designed a MAS incorporating multiple agents, each focused on a distinct cultural perspective. A multiplex agent then synthesizes the outputs of these individual cultural agents to create a single, culturally inclusive response (see §III-C3 for a detailed implementation). Fig. 3 illustrates the design of this MAS. We implemented this approach using the Camel AI [28] framework. However, due to some design limitations, Camel AI fully supports only OpenAI's models for all functionalities. Thus, to generate results and assess the performance of this strategy, we used GPT-4o as the backend for all agents. Fig. 4b and Table III present the performance of this strategy in producing culturally inclusive outputs from LLMs.

The graphs in the figure and table show that this proposed MAS strategy is highly effective (higher PDS) in including diverse cultural perspectives in the final output. In this study, we considered a total of eight cultural perspectives. Ideally, for a precisely multicultural output, the PDS would be evenly distributed at 12.5% for each culture in the responses. The results indicate that our MAS achieves this target, maintaining a distribution close to 12 . 5 ± 2% for each culture and an impressive 98% PDS Entropy. These findings highlight the effectiveness of using a MAS to create a globally pluralistic educational AI without the need for massive GPU clusters to train or fine-tune LLMs to achieve this level of cultural inclusivity. ## C. RQ3: Making LLMs Culturally Inclusive

As discussed in §III-C6, sentiment analysis is crucial to understanding how LLMs treat each culture, as helps us reveal the underlying biases within these models. Figure 5a illustrates the sentimental bias of these LLMs toward each culture, showing how different proposed mitigation strategies can make the LLMs more culturally appreciative. In this context, a green bar shows positive sentiment which indicates that the LLM is highly appreciative of cultures, a red bar shows negative sentiment suggesting opposition to that culture, and a blue bar shows neutral sentiment which generally implies that the model neither supports nor opposes the culture. However, as discussed in §III-C6 another perspective on neutral sentiment is that it reflects a lack of preference, displaying a nonappreciative tone toward these cultures.

In the Baseline Assessment section of Fig 5a, we observe that the responses of LLMs are predominantly neutral rather than exhibiting a more positive (appreciative) stance. Some responses even reflect negative sentiment, indicating a considerable degree of bias. This baseline assessment highlights the inherent response style of each model. In the Contextually Implemented Multiplexity assessment section, on the other hand, we notice a shift from neutral and negative sentiments toward positive sentiments, indicating a broader appreciation of multiple cultures by the LLMs. Finally, Figure 5b illustrates that MAS-Implemented Multiplexity shows a clear, dominant shift from neutral and negative values to positive values. This demonstrates that the MAS responses are multicultural and highly appreciative toward all cultures, establishing it as the most effective strategy for achieving a globally pluralistic, educational AI.

## V. CONCLUSIONS

We propose a framework to establish a baseline for assessing cultural biases in Large Language Models (LLMs), defining cultural bias as a model's tendency to favor certain cultures over others. By analyzing LLM responses to educational questions, we find that these models often exhibit cultural polarization, with biases evident in both explicit content and subtle contextual cues. To address these biases, we introduce two strategies: (1) Contextually-Implemented LLMs, which embed diverse cultural perspectives within the model's prompts to foster more inclusive responses, and (2) MAS-Implemented Multiplex LLMs, where agents representing various cultural viewpoints contribute insights that are synthesized into a balanced response. Our results show that as mitigation strategies advance-from contextual prompting to MAS implementation-the Perspectives Distribution Score (PDS) improves, reflecting enhanced cultural inclusivity. The PDS Entropy score measures how evenly cultural perspectives are represented, with high entropy indicating balanced diversity. Baseline evaluations reveal a low average PDS Entropy of 3.25%, which increases to 19% with Contextually-Implemented LLMs and reaches 98% with MASImplemented Multiplex LLMs, underscoring the effectiveness of these strategies. Sentiment analysis further reveals a shift from negative or neutral sentiment toward positive, with MASImplemented Multiplex LLMs achieving 0% negative sentiment, highlighting improved cultural sensitivity. This study represents a pioneering effort to enhance cultural inclusivity in LLMs through the Multiplexity framework, establishing a baseline methodology and introducing practical mitigation strategies for fostering multicultural responses. We hope this work inspires further development of methodologies that expand the Multiplexity framework, advancing a truly global, pluralistic AI for education that respects and represents diverse cultural perspectives.
