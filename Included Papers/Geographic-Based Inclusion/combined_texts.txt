Abstract -This  study  presents  SIPNENA,  a  novel  mobile application  designed  to  aid  the  learning  and  communication development  of  Sinhala-speaking  autistic  children  aged  six, particularly  in  rural  areas  of  Sri  Lanka.  It  offers  a  unique approach  to  teaching  challenging  subjects  like  English  and Mathematics,  tailored  to  the  specific  needs  of  children  with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). The application integrates interactive methodologies and gamification elements to facilitate better communication, understanding, and engagement. Additionally,  it  incorporates  real-time  emotion  recognition features to monitor and respond to children's emotional states during learning activities. This research evaluates SIPNENA's effectiveness  in  improving  communication  abilities,  academic skills, and emotion understanding among autistic children. The findings  indicate  promising  results  in  catering  to  the  unique educational  needs  of  this  target  population,  particularly  in under-resourced rural regions, where specialized interventions are often scarce.

Keywords -SIPNENA,  Autism  Spectrum  Disorder,  Mobile Learning, Rural education, Child development, Educational Technology

## A. Developing an Augmentative and Alternative Communication System

## 1) System implementation

The Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) system facilitates communication development by integrating basic vocabulary images and words. The core board encompasses the most frequently used vocabulary, such  as  "you,"  "I,"  "get,"  "do,"  and  "where,"  which constitute a significant portion of everyday communication. The visual representation of symbols is consistent with the user's high-tech device, fostering ease of learning and generalization. Furthermore, the grid size is tailored to  accommodate  the  user's  requirements, ensuring  an  optimal  and  user-friendly  experience.  The color  scheme  employed  in  the  creation  of  this  AAC system adheres to established standards, enhancing clarity and familiarity for the users.

Fig.1. Workflow of the AAC system implementation.



The  color  selection  for  the  AAC  system  is  a  crucial consideration. Instead of arbitrary choices, standardized colors were employed, utilizing blue for adjectives, green for verbs, yellow for pronouns, orange for nouns, white for conjunctions, pink for prepositions and social words, purple for questions, brown for adverbs, red for essential function words, negation, and emergency words, and gray for determiners.[12]

## Data collection

A  speaking-assistance  system has  been  developed  to enhance the communication abilities of children with ASD, even at the young age of six years old. The system utilizes pre-trained, concise, and straightforward conversational questions to engage with users. Data was collected through a combination  of  online  and  physical methods,  with  the primary objective of identifying the set of questions required for training the chatbot.  The  chatbot  training  involved approximately 1000 questions, with 500 questions obtained from 50 individuals through physical interactions and another 500  questions  from  50  individuals  online.  The  data  was gathered from non-ASD children above the age of 18.

The study revealed that over 20% of children struggle with expressing their opinions as shown in Fig. 2. To address this challenge,  a  custom  neural  network  was  developed  using  a Keras  sequential  model.  The  model  was  optimized  using stochastic  gradient  descent  (SGD),  with  classifier  crossentropy  as  the  loss  function  and  accuracy  as  the  training measure.  Natural  Language  Processing  (NLP)  techniques were  employed,  including  pre-processing  input  sentences, encoding them into individual words, and lemmatizing them using the Natural Language Toolkit for working with human language data.

## B. Activity monitoring using  facial imotions

The focus of this component is to track the engagement of autistic children with on-screen learning activities. While the user  interacts  with  educational  activities  on  a  tablet,  the device's front camera is utilized to monitor their involvement. The  study  employs  computer  vision  and  deep  learning techniques to monitor  children with ASD  using  facial emotions.  A  ResNet50  pre-trained  Convolutional  Neural Network (CNN) model is used for face recognition, capturing real-time video frames at specific time intervals. The model is  trained  to  predict  real-time  emotions  using  a  dataset  of 11,500 sample images and tested on 2,500 images from six classes: angry, disgusted, happy, neutral, sad, and surprised.

## C. Develop Customize English language support system

In the development of an interactive mobile application tailored for children with autism, the primary goal is  to  facilitate  the  learning  of  the  English  alphabet  and common words through an innovative approach that combines technology with pedagogical strategies. This endeavor encompasses a multifaceted methodology, structured around four pivotal stages: initial alphabet instruction, comprehension testing, the integration of common  words  with  gamified  elements,  and  continuous progress monitoring. The foundation of this educational tool is bolstered by a rich collection of image datasets, comprising over 200 sets, which cater to a spectrum of lighting conditions for the letters. Furthermore, the incorporation of a Convolutional  Neural  Network  (CNN)  model  serves  to enhance  the  effectiveness  of  spoken  language  analysis, thereby  enabling  real-time  feedback,  personalized  learning experiences, and an engaging interface that caters specifically to the needs of children with autism.

## Teaching the Alphabet

The  application will teach children to write  English alphabet  letters  on  a  dotted  line,  presenting  each  letter  in context to aid comprehension. The application will provide immediate feedback on the child's writing, awarding points for  correct  responses  and  deducting  attempts  for  incorrect ones, ensuring a comprehensive learning experience.

## Testing Comprehension

Following the completion of alphabet lessons, the application incorporates  a  short  quiz  to  evaluate  the  child's  grasp  and retention of the material. This formative assessment serves as a  diagnostic  tool,  allowing  for  the  adjustment  of  future lessons based on the child's performance. It provides valuable insights into the child's strengths and areas needing improvement,  thus tailoring the learning path to meet individual needs effectively.

Teaching  common  words  and  implementing  gamification elements

The application introduces everyday words using alphabet lessons, requiring children to write them on a dotted line. Successful completion earns points, while unsuccessful attempts  result  in  no  points.  To  increase  engagement,  the application  uses  gamification  elements  like  leaderboards, badges, and rewards, encouraging competition and a sense of achievement upon reaching certain milestones.

## Assessing progress

This research focuses on developing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) model to support interactive English learning for children with autism. The model is trained on a dataset of audio samples and preprocessed into a  Mel spectrogram  for  consistent  analysis.  The  CNN  architecture, built  with  TensorFlow  and  Keras,  consists  of  convolutional and pooling layers for feature extraction and dense layers for classification. The training process employs the  Adam optimizer  and  categorical  cross-entropy  loss  function,  with early stopping to avoid overfitting. The model's performance is  evaluated  using  a  test  set,  and  once  satisfactory,  it  is deployed within a mobile application. This application offers real-time  analysis  of  the  child's  spoken  input,  providing immediate  feedback  on  pronunciation  and  comprehension, and fostering an engaging and personalized learning environment that enhances language acquisition for children with autism.

## D. Develop a numeracy skill improvement system

In this study, a novel approach is implemented to facilitate counting skills in autistic children, focusing on the numerical range from zero to nine. The intervention involves presenting numbers to the participants and assessing their ability to make accurate judgments. Points are awarded for correct pronunciation, while instances of incorrect pronunciation are utilized as opportunities for improvement and reinforcement.

## Data collection

In  this  research  endeavor,  a  cohort  comprising  1000 students, specifically enrolled in 2nd and 3rd grades, actively participated in the process of articulating numbers spanning from zero to the name of an advanced mathematical formula. The meticulous execution of this auditory task was facilitated using Audacity software, a sophisticated audio editing tool. Within  this  digital  environment,  extraneous  sounds  were methodically eradicated, ensuring the isolation and preservation  of  the  numerical  pronunciations.  The  process yielded a comprehensive dataset, a symphony of voices from 1000 children, each contributing their unique articulation of one-digit numbers. This extensive collection resulted in the curation  of  10,000  individual  sound  samples,  forming  the bedrock of our investigation into the  nuances of  numerical pronunciation proficiency among students in the early grades. This  methodology,  blending  technology  with  pedagogy, provides a robust foundation for analyzing and understanding the  intricacies  of  verbal  numerical  expression  in  a  diverse educational setting.

## E. Providing  an  optimal  end-user  experience  by  involving users

The research focuses on UI/UX engineering technologies for the user journey of a mobile application. Design thinking is an approach that uses design methodologies to re-evaluate paradigms  and  modify  existing  technology  to  create  new procedures suitable for users. User-Centered Design (UCD) is chosen as the best solution to ensure clear and uncomplicated designs. The research uses a pragmatic strategy that includes qualitative and quantitative methods to gain a deep understanding of users. The main goal is to demonstrate highfidelity prototypes of an e-learning system on portable devices that are easy to use, have a minimal learning curve, and do not require excessive time to understand, implement, and satisfy users' expectations. The research objectives include identifying the most appropriate data collection and analysis methods, real-world problems, and interaction design concepts.  The  survey-based  technique  was  chosen  for  data collection  and  analysis,  simplifying  participant  responses. Potential problems were identified, and changes to the mobile application were made using interactive design principles.

Overall  system  design  was  finalized  and  implemented after making the modifications for the interfaces by taking all the responses of the participants and the UCID methodology into account.

## IV. DISCUSION

The proposed research endeavor offers a comprehensive solution  to  address  the  challenges  faced  by  the  target  user group.  The  user  will  be  required  to  register  for  a  system account to access the full range of features and functionalities.

SIPNENA is a mobile application that performs real-time emotion  detection  while  children  with  Autism  Spectrum Disorder (ASD) engage in learning activities. Research efforts have  been  directed  toward  emotion  recognition  in  children with  ASD,  with  the  aim  of  providing  them  with  a  deeper understanding  of emotions  to enhance  their recognition abilities.  The  application  employs  six  distinct  emotional categories: angry, disgusted, happy, neutral, sad, and surprised, tailored to the children's emotional states [13].

SIPNENA is a comprehensive platform that provides an Augmentative and Alternative Communication (AAC) system for  communication  and  speech  training,  with  the  primary objective of improving the speaking skills of young children. Unlike most AAC systems, which are typically emulated on phones  or  tablet  devices  [14],  SIPNENA  has  developed  a Sinhala version of the core board. This unique feature enables children to communicate using their voice, a departure from traditional systems that rely solely on typing [15]. SIPNENA's approach  transcends  conventional  AAC  systems,  allowing children  to  effectively  communicate  through  their  voices, fostering a more natural and engaging experience.

While  numerous  applications  have  been  developed  to enhance the communication skills of children with ASD, there is a paucity of e-learning applications designed to expand their knowledge base. Some applications focus solely on improving children's numeracy skills [16], while others are exclusively dedicated to enhancing English language proficiency. However, SIPNENA  has adopted a holistic approach, incorporating various activities to foster the development of multiple skills, including communication, English language, and mathematics.

SIPNENA is  a  user-friendly  application  tailored  for  young children,  with  a  strong  emphasis  on  functionality  and  an intuitive user interface. It offers a comprehensive solution by amalgamating features such as emotion detection, communication skill development, and subject-related activities. Unlike other applications that provide these features in  isolation,  SIPNENA  consolidates  them  into  a  unified platform,  facilitating  convenient  access  and  utilization  for children. This integrated approach distinguishes SIPNENA as a unique and valuable tool for children with ASD.

## V. CONCLUSTION

This article presents SIPNENA, an innovative e-learning and  communication  platform  designed  for  children  with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD). It integrates features like a  chatbot,  emotion  recognition,  an  AAC  keyboard,  and activities for English and Math learning. Future work aims to expand language support, broaden subject-related activities, and enhance user experience and accessibility. The goal is to provide a comprehensive, inclusive, and engaging learning environment that empowers children  with  ASD to develop essential  skills,  while  contributing  to  the  field  of  assistive technology and inclusive education practices.

## Adaptive Interventions  Reducing  Social Identity Threat to Increase Equity in Higher Distance Education: A Use Case and Ethical Considerations on Algorithmic Fairness

## Abstract

Educational disparities between traditional and non-traditional student groups in higher distance education can potentially be reduced by alleviating social identity threat and strengthening students' sense of belonging in the academic context. We present a use case of how Learning Analytics and Machine Learning can be applied to develop and implement an algorithm to classify students as at-risk of experiencing social identity threat. These students would be presented with an intervention fostering a sense of belonging. We systematically analyze the intervention's intended positive consequences to reduce structural discrimination and increase educational equity, as well as potential risks based on privacy, data protection, and algorithmic fairness considerations. Finally, we provide recommendations for Higher Education Institutions to mitigate risk of bias and unintended consequences during algorithm development and implementation from an ethical perspective.

- There are educational disparities between traditional and non-traditional students in higher distance education that can be partly explained by social identity threat
- We present a use case of an algorithmic system to identify students at risk of experiencing social identity threat and to present these students with an intervention to reduce it
- Several ethical issues concerning privacy and potential algorithmic bias must be considered during algorithm development and implementation
- We provide recommendations for Higher Education Institutions considering these ethical issues

Academic success should be determined by individual ability rather than by factors related to group membership that present structural hurdles and hinder individual students from fulfilling their academic potential. HEIs might thus seek to implement interventions to reduce group-related educational disparities. One interventional approach is based on social-psychological research on social identity threat. ## 2.  Group-Related Educational Disparities Due to Social Identity Threat

Educational disparities  between traditional and non-traditional students can partly be explained by negative stereotypes (unfavourable traits or characteristics ascribed to non-traditional student groups). The stereotype content model (e.g., Cuddy et al., 2007) posits that stereotypes can be described on two basic dimensions: warmth (how friendly an outgroup is perceived) and  competence  (how  capable  it  is  perceived).  In  the  educational  context,  the  stereotype  dimension  of  competence  is particularly relevant. Student groups that are underrepresented in education (e.g., students with migration background or disability) are often stereotyped as low on competence (e.g., Froehlich et al., 2016; Krischler et al., 2018). Bick et al. (2022) investigated stereotypes about different student groups in higher distance education. In a sample of 626 distance education students, students with migration background, non-native first language, or chronic illness and disability were perceived as low on competence. Such negative stereotypes can have detrimental consequences for members of negatively stereotyped groups.  Research  on  social  identity  threat  (Steele  et  al.,  2002)  has  shown  that  if  a  group  is  associated  with  negative stereotypes, this threatens group members' need for a positive social identity. This research is based on social identity theory (Tajfel &amp; Turner, 1979), which states that a person's identity is comprised of a personal identity (i.e., traits and characteristics differentiating the individual from others) and a social identity (i.e., being a member of a social group). Individuals strive for a positive social identity in that they want to feel a positive association between themselves and their group. However, when the group is stereotypically associated with a negative trait like low competence, this threatens the individual's positive social identity. The resulting fear of being associated with the negative traits ascribed to one's group in the academic context is called social identity threat (e.g., Steele et al., 2002). Consequences of social identity threat for affected individuals are reduced academic performance, reduced sense of belonging, disengagement from the academic domain, as well as lower motivation  to  form  relationships  with  classmates  (e.g.,  Froehlich  et  al.,  2023;  Spencer  et  al.,  2016).  From  a  societal perspective, this in turn may lead to a loss in diversity amongst high-performing students and academia in general, and a further reproduction of structural discrimination (e.g., Schmader &amp; Hall, 2014).

Research on social identity threat and its consequences was predominantly conducted in face-to-face learning contexts. Bick et al. (2024) showed that in a distance learning environment with computer-supported collaborative learning, students experiencing higher social identity threat reported a lower sense of belonging to university and the virtual study group, as well as lower motivation to initiate and maintain peer relationships. Therefore, social identity threat can also have detrimental consequences in virtual learning contexts.

## 3.  Interventions to Alleviate Social Identity Threat

Several intervention approaches to reduce social identity threat and its consequences have been developed (Liu et al., 2021). Some approaches aim at creating learning contexts in which social group memberships and associated stereotypes are less salient  and  therefore  do  not  unfold  their  negative  consequences  for  non-traditional  students.  Other  approaches  target individual learners and aim at strengthening student resilience against social identity threat. One possibility is to strengthen non-traditional students' sense of belonging to the academic domain and thereby reduce the uncertainty of whether they fit in the academic context when their group is underrepresented and negatively stereotyped in this context (Walton &amp; Cohen,

## JOURNALOFLEARNINGANALYTICS



2007).  Belongingness  interventions  often  contain  statements  from  other  students  explaining  that  belonging  uncertainty experienced at the beginning of their studies usually fades over time and is not dependent on minority group membership. Students then write such a statement themselves (e.g., Kizilcec et al., 2020).

Research investigating the effectiveness of interventions to strengthen student resilience against social identity threat has been mainly conducted in face-to-face or laboratory settings, whereas research in digital learning contexts is scarce. Kizilcec et al. (2020) showed that two brief interventions to increase a sense of belonging and decrease social identity threat were effective in eliminating performance differences between students from developed and less developed countries in MOOCs offered by the universities of Harvard and Stanford. The study demonstrated that interventions can reduce groupbased performance disparities in asynchronous, computer-mediated learning contexts, which are common in higher distance education.

## 4.  Intended Consequences of Intervention Implementation in Higher Distance Education

Considering this evidence, a major goal to reduce group-based disparities in higher distance education is to use learning analytics (LA) to adaptively present students at risk of experiencing social identity threat with such interventions. Especially in distance education contexts, where students complete individual or group assignments in virtual learning environments, it is possible to monitor learning and performance via LA. For example, machine learning (ML) algorithms can be used to target students most likely to benefit from these interventions.

Operating such a socio-technical intervention system aims at actively strengthening educational equity and supports HEIs in reducing the structural discrimination effects vis-à-vis non-traditional students. This aim will be achieved if the intervention is effective in alleviating social identity threat. Non-traditional students would then be able to perform closer to their intellectual ability. Hence non-traditional students' performance in distance education would be a purer measure of innate ability. To be precise, we must differentiate between two educational performance gaps observable in HEIs: 1) the gap  between  true  intellectual  ability  and  actual  performance  (or  development  over  time)  for  individual  students  that  is attributable  to  the  effect  of  social  identity  threat;  and  2)  the  educational  performance  gap  between  the  groups  of  nontraditional students and traditional students due to structural discrimination. Targeted interventions are assumed to benefit individual students by reducing or closing the first gap. Thereby, interventions are assumed to also have a positive effect on campus diversity by reducing the second gap. This resonates with recent calls in LA ethics to put equity at the centre of LA data science by looking 'first at those at the margins of the education system' and by actively 'doing ethics' as part of LA data science (Cerratto Pargman et al., 2023, p. 144).

However, the effects of closing the first gap by reducing individual experiences of social identity threat on overall structural  discrimination  will  be  limited.  The  concept  of  'structural  discrimination'  is  meant  to  capture  effects  that consistently  keep  saliently  underprivileged  groups  in  society  at  a  disadvantage  -  independent  of  the  intent  of  current individual or institutional actors (Altman, 2020). Hence, structural discrimination can have historical, often diffuse causes. Social identity threat can be understood as both a cause and an effect of structural discrimination: it is a cause since it is an unintended psychological effect that reproduces unjust and disproportionately disadvantageous outcomes; and it is an effect in that, in itself, it is produced as a result of discriminatory structures independent of the intent of current actors in HEIs. The group-related impact of interventions is, hence, expected to be limited to mitigating the causal effects of social identity threat. After interventions, the group of non-traditional students is unlikely to perform completely equal to the group of traditional students. Still, especially due to the empirically established positive effect on individual students, it is worth developing and implementing such an intervention system. In the following section, we describe a corresponding use case.

## 5. A Use Case of an Algorithm to Recommend an Intervention to Reduce Social Identity Threat to Non-traditional Students

To outline a use case for adaptively reducing the influence of social identity threat on student performance in higher distance education,  we  will  provide  a  realistic  description  of  an  educational  context  applicable  for  regular,  non-experimental operation. We base this use case on the educational context at the FernUniversität in Hagen in Germany. With more than 68,000 students and a sociodemographically diverse student body, the FernUniversität is like other large distance universities and  is  thus  an  ideal  educational  context  for  our  considerations.  Students  from  non-traditional  backgrounds  are  well represented in the student body. For example, students are on average 39 years old,  most of whom are employed while studying (75%), and 10% of whom have a foreign nationality (FernUniversität in Hagen, 2023a). Moreover, approximately 10% report chronic illness or disability (FernUniversität in Hagen, 2023b). Furthermore, a gap between traditional and nontraditional students' performance has been identified (Stoessel et al., 2015) and non-traditional student groups report higher social identity threat than traditional student groups (Bick et al., 2024).

## JOURNALOFLEARNINGANALYTICS



Consequently, such a higher distance education institution might aim at implementing an adaptive intervention to reduce social  identity  threat,  which  in  turn  should  increase  non-traditional  students'  performance.  Ideally,  the  group-related performance gap would be reduced, because learning and performance assessments reflect a purer measure of non-traditional students' true ability. The institution thus aims to present the intervention only to those students whose performance and belonging are likely to be negatively affected by social identity threat. By reducing or removing the influence of social identity threat, educational disparities would be levelled, as sociodemographic group memberships associated with negative ability-related stereotypes are less influential for performance and students' true individual ability is better reflected by their educational outcomes (e.g., participation in individual and group assignments, exam enrollment, grades).  This notion of fairness aims at achieving educational equity, meaning that pre-existing gaps are narrowed due to the implementation of an intervention that is only presented to student groups experiencing social identity threat.

In our use case, the higher education institution is assumed to seek the development and implementation of an ML algorithm that classifies which students are at risk of experiencing social identity threat and should therefore be assigned to the intervention condition. The ensuing prediction problem can be described as follows: Based on which group-related and individual characteristics should students receive the intervention? This prediction problem arises  since not all students belonging to negatively stereotyped non-traditional student groups are at risk of experiencing social identity threat to similar extents.  Students vary in their individual susceptibility to social identity threat, which in turn is associated with further psychological constructs. For instance, students who place high value on the negatively stereotyped academic domain or on their membership in the negatively stereotyped group are more prone to experience social identity threat (Spencer et al., 2016). Therefore, an algorithm assigning students to the intervention based only on demographic information would make considerable assignment errors. Consequently, training data for the algorithm should include assessments of psychological constructs like social identity threat and associated variables (e.g., group identification, domain identification).

A crucial issue regarding the viability of the use case concerns its potentially limited prediction accuracy. In the context of social identity threat interventions, it is still unclear whether state-of-the-art ML can develop algorithms that perform considerably  better  than  alternative  non-adaptive  intervention  recommendations.  The  dataset  of  the  study  reported  in Kizilcec et al. (2020) was used as training data for ML algorithms to target behavioural science interventions for individual students. However, the developed algorithms failed to produce better student outcomes than assigning the intervention to all or a random selection of students. Kizilcec and Lee (2021) conclude that currently there are prediction limitations of datadriven models and that stakeholders like  HEIs should not place too much confidence in algorithmic systems to reduce educational disparities. We believe that it is worth exploring this further, however, as previous data used for algorithm development did not contain direct measures of social identity threat and related psychological constructs. The inclusion of direct measures such as self-reported perceived social identity threat assessed in a survey might increase the algorithm's prediction accuracy.

Addressing such issues related to the technical implementation are planned as a next step in our research process. Currently, however, we focus on describing the use case and systematically reflecting on potential ethical issues concerning algorithmic fairness. Corresponding recommendations about how to tackle these issues can then be directly considered during  the  development  phase  (value  sensitive  design;  Franssen  et  al.,  2023).  To  identify  potential  ethical  issues,  the following section highlights unintended consequences of the ML algorithm described in our use case.

## 6. Unintended Negative Consequences of the Proposed System

## 6.1.  Privacy Impact and Other Ethical Considerations

Any operation of an ML-based intervention system must be expected to create unintended negative consequences - even when the  intended  impact  is  ethically  laudable.  This  section  describes  aspects  immediately  relevant  to  the  use  case  to highlight the need for ethical assessment in the design phase. The next section will focus in more detail on aspects related to bias and discrimination as those may directly countervail the ambitions described in the use case.

Since the use case foresees regular operation , students are not to be considered research participants . While this may be different for the design phase of the intervention system (including the training phase of the classification algorithm), operational LA applications must undergo ethical reflection beyond standards of responsible research in behavioural science, such as those outlined in the Belmont report (National Commission, 1979, pp. 23193-23194) and enforced through ethics review boards (Willis et al., 2016). For example, it has been established early on that LA's data collection, analysis methods, and  adaptive  interventions  resemble  the  dynamic  and  structure  of  surveillance activities  such  as  tracking  and  profiling (Prinsloo &amp; Slade, 2017; Slade &amp; Prinsloo, 2013).

This highlights the need to reflect potential privacy issues resulting from more personal data on students being collected and analyzed to support the algorithm's classification function as non-traditional students at risk of experiencing social identity threat or not. As Prinsloo et al. (2022) note, privacy considerations should go beyond complying with legal data protection requirements and making use of privacy-enhancing technologies. Rather, they should be responsive to norms of appropriateness governing the flow of information in the local context of the HEI (contextual integrity; Nissenbaum, 2009).

ANALYTICSRESEARCH

For example, more personal data on students such as self-report measures on group identification or social identity threat should be collected sparsely and be kept confidential.

However,  if  social  identity  threat  is  to  be  extrapolated  from  demographics  only,  its  extent  might  be  over-  or underestimated for certain students based on imperfect correlations between demographics, group identification, and social identity threat. This affects both the training data for the algorithm (which could, in principle, be obtained through informed consent)  and  the  data  needed  for  operational  implementation,  as  the  algorithm  cannot  be  trained  to  use  sets  of  data substantially different from the data to be classified. While including psychological constructs in the training data and in the operational context will certainly increase the costs of designing and operating  the algorithm, its privacy impact in the operational phase may be minimized by running the trained algorithm in a decentralized fashion on student computers to avoid  collecting  and  storing  additional  personal,  potentially  sensitive  data  on  HEI  servers.  The  detailed  technical implementation of this is beyond the scope of this article. It should be noted, however, that generating lists of identifiable non-traditional students at risk of experiencing social identity threat creates manifold privacy and discrimination risks that need to be thoroughly assessed and mitigated for the training data, but especially for the operational context, where such risks may be prohibitive.

Furthermore, as Drachsler and Greller (2016, p. 95) note, expecting constant, state-of-the-art data security measures from public institutions like universities may be unrealistic. Hence, one may need to start from the assumption that any data gathered in LA-related databases will at some point in the future be compromised . Not collecting more sensitive information on HEI servers but leaving them on student machines may be the best option to minimize risks of misuse and hacking.

As opposed to an  experimental  research  setting  (which  may  also  include  the  collection  of  training  data)  in  which informed consent is the expected norm and can only be withheld in exceptional cases, an operational setting at an HEI may have different demands. Aside from the transparency and accountability requirements, European data protection regulations, for example, allow for forms of legal basis other than consent, especially when HEIs are public institutions and process data in an official capacity laid down in the law. If the intervention is effective in reducing educational disparities, its effect would be maximized if all target students identified by the algorithm to be at risk of experiencing social identity threat participated in the intervention. Hence, it is an ethical question whether participation in the intervention should be mandatory for the students selected or whether students should be given the choice.

As correlations between individual predispositions for the experience of social identity threat and performance outcomes are imperfect, however, there is the risk of false-positive recommendations to participate in the intervention. This would be problematic in the case of adverse effects of the intervention recommendation for some students. It remains an open question what information is necessary for students to make an informed decision about  participating in the intervention. Future research should investigate how LA can be used to enable positive interventions while minimizing adverse impacts (Sclater, 2016).

From a practical perspective, since some forms of co-operation are required on behalf of the students (e.g., regarding self-report  measures)  for  the  algorithm  to  work,  and  since  subsequent  interventions  require  participation  by  students categorized as at risk of experiencing social identity threat, asking for consent seems to be the most viable option. Conducting the  interventions  on  a  non-voluntary  basis  seems  like  an  ethical  and  legal  no-go  and  would  likely  be  unproductive  by rendering the interventions unpopular and the students resistive. Subjecting students to spending time on self-reporting data for  classification  and,  for  some,  on  the  interventions  would  likely  only  be  reasonable  when  such  measures  remain  the exception on campus: incentivizing students to take part in a plethora of self-report and intervention activities could amount to accosting them.

While there are some ethics frameworks for LA applications (e.g., Drachsler &amp; Greller, 2016; Sclater, 2015), practical implementations of such approaches in education are scarce and a general gap between research and practice exists (Ladjal et  al.,  2022).  While  frameworks  have  considerable merit, we do not believe that it is possible to use them in a simple 'checking the boxes' fashion for operational LA applications, as too many contextual factors should be considered during technology  design  and  implementation.  This  section  has  addressed  some,  but  not  all  ethical  issues  relevant  for  a comprehensive  ethical  assessment.  Subsequently,  we  focus  on  bias  and  discrimination  issues,  as  they  may  directly countervail the intended benefits of the intervention.

## 6.2.  Bias and Fairness Aspects of the Classification Algorithm

To ensure that unintended outcomes of the use case do not countervail the intended positive effects, potential biases and discriminatory effects need to be taken into account as the classification algorithm is subject to the same risks of inadvertently (re-)producing inequities as any algorithmic LA application (Simbeck, 2022). Datasets used for training ML-based decisionmaking algorithms will, thus, usually reflect historic biases underlying structural discrimination (Müller, 2023). At best, such datasets can be made unbiased for one single kind of issue, which makes the common reuse of datasets for other purposes and contexts prone to inadvertently introducing bias (Müller, 2023). Hence, implementations of the use case should refrain from reusing existing datasets for what Kizilcec and Lee (2021) label the measurement and training phases of LA algorithm

## JOURNALOFLEARNINGANALYTICS



development, which precede the action phase, where the algorithm is applied in an operational context and unintended negative effects become manifest.

As Müller (2023) highlights, however, this bias isn't problematic per se : it is not the bias itself that is harmful, but the combination  of  bias  and individual  or  organizational  reliance on  the  correctness  of  the  data  in  decision-making.  This highlights  that  the  classification  algorithm  proposed  in  the  use  case  should  also  be  considered  within  its  institutional embedding as a socio-technical intervention system, rather than as an isolated piece of software. Hence, as an alternative way of addressing bias and discrimination in the design phase of the algorithm, one could also try to mitigate potential discriminatory impact by relying less on its correctness (e.g., by planning for alternative ways to identify students at risk of experiencing social identity threat or to allow self-enrollment in an intervention). While, in the following, we focus on the algorithm itself, this should be duly noted for actual implementation.

## 6.2.1. Risk of False-Negative Classification of At-Risk Students

As established above, the algorithm's use case is narrowing the performance gap between traditional and non-traditional students due to structural discrimination (in the sense that social identity threat is one of its effects). Hence, a potential source of countervailing discriminatory effects of the classification algorithm might be that it inadvertently fails to identify distinct subgroups  of  non-traditional  students  and,  consequently,  deprives  them  of  the  intervention's  positive  effect.  Are  there subgroups  of  non-traditional  students  saliently  affected  by  structural  discrimination  but  not  classified  as  at  risk  of experiencing social identity threat, but who would profit from the intervention, while other subgroups are correctly classified? This seemingly rephrases the classification problem of the algorithm (distinguishing students at risk from students not at risk of experiencing social identity threat), but it is crucial whether, in the action phase, the classification is reliable, to a similar extent, for different salient student groups. As social identity threat is highest for non-traditional student groups who are usually  in  the  minority  (e.g.,  students  with  migration  background,  chronic  illness,  or  disability;  Bick  et  al.,  2024), intersectional discrimination is of particular relevance.

Addressing  this  question  during  the  measurement  and  training  phases  entails  a  more  detailed  definition  of  nontraditionality and risk of experiencing social identity threat reflecting the local context of the student population at the HEI in question. In the measurement phase, some of the subgroups and intersectional subgroups are likely reduced to only a few cases in the training data or even fully absent, which is prone to result in bias and discriminatory impact (e.g., Chawla et al., 2002). In the model learning phase, this may lead to the prediction accuracy being lower for certain subgroups of nontraditional students.

Conceptually,  one  may  refer  to  the  idea  of  'algorithmic  fairness,'  which  was  introduced  to  answer  the  growing awareness of such issues in algorithmic decision-making and data analysis (Binns, 2020, p. 515). Algorithmic fairness can be defined as the absence of bias and discriminatory effects in such a system (Kizilcec &amp; Lee, 2021, p. 177). Since complete fairness is a regulative ideal rather than actually obtainable, fairness should 'be measured on a continuous and not binary scale, and there are multiple ways to measure fairness'  (Kizilcec &amp; Lee, 2021, p. 176). Hence, the field tries to give an operationalized account of measuring fairness in  ML  systems  (Binns,  2018,  p. 1).  From  a  statistical  point  of  view,  the question raised above would be one of adequate sufficiency: 'Sufficiency encodes the belief that algorithmic decisions should carry the same level of significance for all groups' (Kizilcec &amp; Lee, 2021, p. 187). In general, however, dealing with intersectionality of multiple kinds of discrimination as well as currently unidentified vulnerable groups poses problems for such kinds of approaches to algorithmic fairness (Binns, 2020, p. 515).

## 6.2.2. Risks of Being Classified for the Intervention

Ethical considerations are also necessary for algorithm implementation during the action phase (i.e., the operational context). First,  the  way  the  intervention  is  recommended  to  at-risk  students  needs  to  be  carefully  considered  and  evaluated. Stigmatizing responses to model predictions are possible when at-risk students are flagged for the intervention in a way that further contributes to discrimination. If non-traditional students have accumulated experiences of social identity threat and group-based discrimination during their educational biographies, being flagged for the intervention might elicit negative responses.  Informing  students  about  social  identity  threat  can  in  itself  already  work  as  an  intervention  against  its consequences (e.g., Johns et al., 2005). However, being transparent about the intervention's aim to reduce social identity threat  can  make  negatively  stereotyped  group  memberships  more  salient  and  thus  exacerbate  their  influence  in  the educational context. Communicating to non-traditional students that they were selected for an intervention as they might be affected by social identity threat might further reduce their sense of belonging to the academic context due to highlighting their minority status (e.g., Walton &amp; Cohen, 2007). To date, research empirically investigating different ways to inform participants about being selected for such interventions is lacking.

Furthermore, it needs to be assessed if traditional students from some salient social groups are incorrectly classified more often as at risk of experiencing social identity threat than others (e.g., female as opposed to male students where gender is

not a factor determining the risk of experiencing social identity threat). This can be captured by the statistical measure of separation, which 'encodes the belief that a fair algorithm makes correct and incorrect predictions at similar rates for different groups.' (Kizilcec &amp; Lee, 2021, p. 186). Similarly to non-traditional, at-risk students, the negative impact of being invited as a traditional student to participate in an intervention may vary depending on how the intervention is recommended: Being classified  as  at  risk  of  experiencing  social  identity  threat  may  give  reason  to  traditional  students  who  struggle  in  the introductory phase of their studies due to factors other than social identity threat to question their belonging and whether other members of the HEI see them as non-traditional. Whether this issue of potential negative impact (and, in case of algorithmic unfairness, its unequal distribution across salient social groups) is a mere theoretical possibility or practically relevant has not been established. However, in case of false classifications of traditional students, we believe that this risk may be sufficiently mitigated by openly communicating that the algorithm may be unreliable in this fashion.

Contrary, as (Binns, 2018, pp. 7-9) notes, for some groups the relevant question regarding algorithmic fairness may not be 'correctly' identified as at-risk of social identity threat according to a chosen definition adequate to the local context, but rather one of appropriate representation. Some groups may expect to be classified or not classified as such, irrespective of whether this really reflects proneness to social identity threat or self-image as (non-)traditional. A subgroup may consider it important to identify as a traditional constituent group at an HEI, but still be prone to social identity threat. In such a case, it is unclear whether to give precedence to group identity and forego the potential benefits of recommending interventions, or to signal non-traditionality to group members in favour of those benefits. Here, adapting the communication may avoid such dilemmas (e.g., by avoiding the connection to traditionality and focusing on stereotypes), but the choice of action may ultimately be best left to university policy and self-governance.

The third statistical measure proposed by Kizilcec and Lee (2021, p. 186), independence, is meant to reflect 'that an algorithm's decision be independent of group membership.' Since, however, the goal of the classification is the identification of students at risk of experiencing social identity threat, it would not make sense to compare the outcomes across different salient social groups, as differences in the classification rates are to be expected and the absence of such differences would probably indicate a lack of algorithmic reliability.

## 6.2.4. Group Fairness, Individual Fairness, and Counterfactual Fairness

As Binns (2020, p. 515) notes, algorithmic fairness research began with 'fairness measures based on statistical parity between protected groups (e.g., gender, race) in each outcome class, and hence are classed as group fairness measures.' The arguments presented above largely follow this line of thinking. While group fairness (GF) is currently the dominant research approach to  algorithmic  fairness,  it  'suffers  from  a  number  of  problems.  A  wide  variety  of  group  fairness  definitions  have  been proposed, and there has been little agreement about which is the most promising' (Fleisher, 2021, p. 481). Furthermore, group fairness measures use a limited number of coarse-grained, prescribed protected groups. This poses challenges for intersectionality and for currently unidentified vulnerable groups (Binns, 2020, p. 515).

Alternative  approaches  were  proposed  that  are  labelled  individual  fairness  (IF)  measures,  which  suggest  'that  the intuitive notion of fairness is expressed by the principle similar treatment: similar individuals should be treated similarly' (Dwork et al., 2012; Fleisher, 2021, p. 480). Here, two individuals are alike if their:

Combinations of task-relevant attributes are 'nearby' each other in the defined metric space. It is assumed that the distance metric is somehow defined by the people who set the policy (e.g. college admissions tutors), and possibly with broader societal agreement, and that this can be applied to individuals (e.g. college applicants) … Individual fairness says that for any two individuals, if their distance in task-relevant similarity is sufficiently small, they should receive the same outcome. (Binns, 2020, p. 516)

It is sometimes argued that notions of individual and group fairness are in conflict and that using group fairness measures to influence the measurement and model learning phases may violate conditions of individual fairness. In turn, treating similar individuals similarly may not produce outcomes that satisfy group-level notions of fairness (Kizilcec &amp; Lee, 2021, p. 190). However, both Binns (2018, 2020) and Fleisher (2021) contend that the two paradigms are complementary rather than oppositional. As Binns (2020, p. 524) shows, the main challenge in finding the right measures and choosing the right approach (be it IF or GF) lies in the situative description of the potential biases.

Consequently, group fairness measures seem to be a good fit for the described use case. Since the classification problem of  the  algorithm  is  itself  group-related,  we  consider  it  unlikely  that  individual  fairness  measures  can  capture  the  'nontraditionality'  and  the  'risk  of  experiencing  social  identity  threat'  based  on  individual  properties  that  serve as  distance metrics and that are independent from group membership.

Kizilcec  and  Lee  (2021,  p. 190)  also  note  counterfactual  measures  to  algorithmic  fairness,  where  the  decision  to recommend a social identity threat intervention is fair if the prediction for a member of a certain negatively stereotyped student group remains unchanged in the counterfactual scenario when the student belongs to another negatively stereotyped student  group,  keeping  all  other  features  constant.  Again,  it  seems  unsound  to  assume  the  algorithm  can  succeed  in classifying students as at risk of experiencing social identity threat independent of actually being a member of a specific student group (i.e., while passing the counterfactual test). Moreover, due to bias arising from limited available variables and

## JOURNALOFLEARNINGANALYTICS



ANALYTICSRESEARCH

unbalanced samples in the training data, it might occur that no student with a different group membership has the same individual features. In such a case, the algorithm needs to extrapolate outside of the training data set, which can lead to further prediction inaccuracies. Hence, as opposed to group fairness measures, we deem neither individual nor counterfactual fairness measures to be a good fit for the described use case.

## 7. Discussion: Limitations and Future Directions

Finally,  we  discuss  issues  that  should  be  considered  during  algorithm  development  and  implementation.  A  first  issue concerns the effectiveness of the proposed intervention. If the intended consequence is to mitigate the performance gap between non-traditional and traditional students in higher distance education, the success of this endeavour naturally depends on the effectiveness of the intervention in mitigating the psychological processes leading to social identity threat. If designed in  a  theory-driven  and  context-sensitive  way,  even  short  one-shot  belongingness  interventions  have  lasting  effects  by triggering a recursive cycle (e.g., Walton, 2014; Walton &amp; Wilson, 2018). However, previous attempts to scale up such educational interventions have produced heterogeneous results. Brief interventions to increase non-traditional students' sense of belonging were particularly effective when the universities or learning environments provided opportunities to experience belonging  (Walton  et  al.,  2023).  Moreover,  contextual  factors  created  heterogeneity  in  effect  sizes  when  scaling  up behavioural  science  interventions  in  MOOCs  (Kizilcec  et  al.,  2020).  In  future  research,  this  heterogeneity  must  be systematically taken into account (Bryan et al., 2021) and ML algorithms are likely to be useful in this endeavour because they can take complex multivariate associations into account when classifying students.

A  related  question  concerns  the  extent  of  the  second  performance  gap  (between  student groups due  to  structural discrimination) that can be reduced by addressing the first gap (between true ability and actual performance for individual students  due  to  social  identity  threat)  with  an  adaptive  intervention.  It  is  still  unclear  what  other  forms  of  structural discrimination beyond social identity threat contribute to group-related disparities in higher distance education and how these  forms  of  discrimination  can  be  addressed  when  investigating  the  effectiveness  of  adaptive  interventions.  Beyond specifically targeting non-traditional students and increasing their resilience against social identity threat, HEIs can also implement measures to decrease the likelihood that stereotypes are activated (e.g., by deleting stereotypical depictions in study materials and learning environments). HEIs can also implement diversity statements and collaborative learning formats to increase intergroup contact and thus reduce stereotypes and prejudice (e.g., Aronson et al., 1978). Taking contextual factors concerning the learning environment and the situation at the HEI into account will also determine the generalizability of  the  use  case  to  other  contexts.  For  example,  previous  research  has  shown  that  belongingness  interventions  reduced achievement  gaps  in  MOOCs.  Future  research  thus  needs  to  investigate  to  what  extent  these  promising  results  are generalizable.

In  the  action  phase,  it  is  unclear  what  information  is  necessary  for  students  to  make  an  informed  decision  about participating in the intervention. Even small changes in the presentation and administration of interventions can drastically change the results and sometimes even render the interventions ineffective (e.g., Walton &amp; Yeager, 2020). Future research should investigate how to present the intervention while simultaneously reducing potential adverse effects for students that were classified for intervention participation and ensuring that the intervention still achieves the intended effects (Sclater, 2016). Similarly, risks of adverse effects for traditional students who are incorrectly classified for the intervention might be mitigated by communicating that the algorithm-based decision is not 100% accurate. In turn, such communication might impede student trust in LA and the educational benefit of ML, which might also render the intervention ineffective.

## 8. Ethical Recommendations for HEIs Regarding Algorithms to Reduce Social Identity Threat

- Assess potential ethical risks beyond bias and discrimination (e.g., privacy, data security, consent procedures).
- Oversample underrepresented student groups to increase prediction accuracy.
- Include surveys measuring psychological constructs in training data.
- Think about ways to reduce reliance on the correctness of the algorithms' classifications as part of the socio-technical implementation.
- Apply group fairness measures to show that the underlying data are not neutral and highlight specific areas where the algorithm may need to compensate; consider what is not measured in the data (e.g., psychological variables associated with social identity threat).
- Pay attention to intersectional forms of structural discrimination and algorithmic bias.

## JOURNAL OFLEARNINGANALYTICS

- Include feedback loops to optimize the algorithm's prediction accuracy.
- Evaluate:  Does  the  algorithm  perform  better  than  random  assignment  of  students  to  the  intervention  or  control condition? Does the algorithm perform better in reducing educational disparities than simply presenting all students with the intervention?
- Implement discrimination-aware unit tests at each step so that fairness issues can be identified and addressed in a timely and targeted manner (d'Alessandro et al., 2017).
- Consider reasons for data collection and the way the interventions are recommended to prevent countervailing effects.
- Ensure legitimacy through student acceptance and participatory processes: Some ethical conflicts will remain, and it may not be an ethically innocent option to refrain from developing and applying an algorithmic intervention system.
- Consider  if  some  salient  social  groups  would  oppose  being  labelled  (non-)traditional  and  think  about  potential conflicts in terms of policy and self-governance rather than in prescriptive normative terms.

## A. System Architecture

BuddyBot system architecture is shown in Fig. 1 with five phases starting from  the  user interface to  adaptive learning personalization.

Fig. 1: System Architecture



These five phases are explained in brief.

- 1) User interface: It is a text-based interface where the user interacts with the BuddyBot
- 2) Preprocessing  Unit: Implementing  tokenization, The NLP  object tokenizes the input text into individual tokens (words, sentences), Normalization: It  lemmatizes  each  token  (reducing  words  to  their base or root form) and removes punctuation. Entity Extraction: It identifies named entities in the input. Named entities  are  phrases that  refer  to real-world objects .
- 3) NLP Stack: Intent recognition determines the user's intention  or  specific  action  for  a  given  input.  It involves identifying the request that the user wants to perform. It  helps  the  system  understand  the user's queries in the context of the ongoing conversation and provides relevant responses. Dialogue  Manager  Keep  track  of  the  conversation history and current context to maintain a natural flow and  avoid  repetition  and  based  on  the  Natural Language Understanding, NLU output, the dialogue manager  determines  the  appropriate  response  or action to take.
- 4) Language generation engine: Employs fine-tuned GPT-2  and  Flax  T5  models  for  content  creation. GPT-2  is  trained  on  large  web  text  corpora  and generates engaging responses for chatbot interaction, while  FLAN-T5  is  trained  on  diverse  text-to-text datasets  encompassing  various  tasks  and  excels  in summarization, and question answering.
- 5) Adaptive  Learning  Personalization: Buddy  Bot learns from its interactions with users and improves its performance over time. Build user profiles based on their preferences, interactions, and past conversations to personalize future interactions. Regularly monitor the system's performance and user feedback  to  identify  areas  for  improvement  and update the models and algorithms accordingly.

- Data Processing: Preprocesses  and  tokenizes raw data, splitting it into Training and Testing sets for language model training.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Currently, special attention is paid to improving the quality of life of people with disabilities, in particular the expansion of methods and techniques of education. In the learning process, active  communication  between  the  teacher  and  the  student  is necessary,  which  is  difficult  when  the  student  is  a  hard-ofhearing person.

Communication is a two-way process, which is the transfer of  information from the "speaker" to the "deaf-mute" (type I) and the translation of the sign language into symbolic text or speech  (type  II).  We  can  designate  the  first  type  as  a  direct problem, and the second as a reverse one.

To the first  type  translation,  you  can  now  use  the  already widespread computer technology п . speech recognition. That technology  can  convert  voice  speech  in  visual  symbols.  The formalization  of  the  second  type  communication  and  the implementation of computer programs that provide recognition and  "translation"  of  the  sign  language  are  a  much  more complex  task  requiring  the  use  of  intelligent  methods  of processing  information.  In  this  regard,  the  development  of communication systems for social accessibility for people with disabilities  on  the  basis  of  methods  of  machine  learning  is relevant.

The language  spoken by  people  with  hearing  problems  is called sign language. This is a very complex language that has many  dialects.  They  talk  in  this  language,  usually  by  two hands, but many things can be explained with one hand.

The article deals with the inverse problem associated with the development of computer-based methods and their implementation in the software package, supporting the learning process of a hearing impaired students.

## I. INTRODUCTION

In education, equity gaps  refer to the disparities in educational  outcomes  across  race/ethnicity,  socioeconomic status, gender, physical or mental abilities, and other demographic traits. [23, 24] The term was originally proposed to study the disparities in learning results by demographically diverse students in K-12 schools. In educational assessment [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8], empirical data are systematically documented and analyzed in order to draw from them the insights on how to help students learn better. There are two types of assessment: direct assessment using data  and  indirect  assessment  using  inferences  from  data. Currently, the  most  common  practice  for  evaluation  of teaching effectiveness is course evaluation, which is a direct assessment  by  students  (and  often  faculties). ## IV. DISSCUSIONS AND CONCLUSIONS

The above relative approach on teaching effectiveness to study equity gap problems represents an effort to  tackle the equity gap research using a more practical approach leveraging machine learning. It is a research direction that is still in the inception stage. In reality, there are many challenges that  must  be  met  to  provide  sufficient  conditions  for  this research to continue to develop and evolve.

## A. Data Collection and Privacy Protection

Obviously,  the  biggest  challenge  for  this  research  is  on data collection, as the major data that we need to collect for this research are considered private data about students (both the  demographic  and  academic  data).  In  US,  the  Family Educational  Rights  and  Privacy  Act  (FERPA)  forbids  the collection of sensitive student data including the demographic data and those from students' academic records. However, it provides  two  exceptions.  Exception  1  states  that  with  the consent of a student, his/her data could be used by a third party. Exception 2 states that for academic purpose, student academic  record  data  could  be  used,  provided  that  certain precautions are taken as well as with an institutional consent as well.

However, as a matter fact, due to the sensitiveness of the issue on collecting student data, the vast majority of American institutions tend to be reluctant to endorse it for the purpose of research like ours, worrying about possible negative consequences. Talking to students and hence obtaining their consents to using their data seems to be the only viable choice, but  it  is  extremely  inefficient  when  considering  the  vast amount of  data  that  we  need  to  collect  for  our  research.  In addition, more often the credibility of the data collected from voluntary student participants are also not without problems.

Similar  laws  and  regulations  on  student  data  collection also exist in Europe, China, and the other major regions.

To meet this challenge, we strongly urge the governmental agencies  and  academic  institutional  societies  to  make  and enact new laws and/or regulations to legalize/endorse appropriate usages of students' data.

## B. What Demographic Properties of Students to Collect?

While  different  sources  may  use/share  the  same  set  of definitions on  demographic properties  for  studies, there  has not been a technically complete agreement on what composes the demographic information. In addition, the granularities of some  of  the  possible  demographic  property  also  present challenges. For example, is 'Chinese' a proper granularity for the racial property of a Chinese student, while there could be more  than  50  minorities  (sub-races)  that  fall  in  this  major racial category?

Similarly, to meet this challenge, we propose to limit the research to the regions where there is an established consensus on the range and granularity of demographic properties. Only when the research in this direction become mature enough in different regions, a common  standard on them could established.

## C. Representation of The Nature of Teaching

The major technical issue of the research shall be on the representation  of  teaching  ETC α .  Is  it  true  that  the  teaching results  of  a  learning  outcome  is  determined  by  the  linear composition of all the results by the functions mapping each student  property  to  a  value?  The  answer  is  that  it  may  not necessary be true, and more research work shall be done to determine the internal mechanism (and hence the corresponding mathematical form) on how the student properties affect their learnings on a course taught by a certain teacher.

Luckily,  the  are  many  scientometric,  machine  learning, and statistical methods that we can use to produce the teaching simulation  engine  without  specifying  the  exact  ways  of  the mappings.  The  representation  given  for  ETC α in  this  paper stands for an initial attempt and it will not impact the training of the teaching simulation engine (using supervised learning). We think that it could be discovered later (with more data and experiments)  that  the  representation  should  be  more  like  a networked structure.

## D. Implementation of T-Matrices Rev II and Future Work

The implementation  of T-Matrices  Rev  II as  a  software package  is  currently  underway.  Specifically,  we  are  in  the phase of collecting domain requirements. We feel cautiously confident that the first running version of this software will be available in the latter half of the next year.

In  the  future,  we  would  like  to  explore  adding  into TMatrices Rev II the  culture and domain specific modules to enrich,  illustrate,  and  better  define  the  related  demographic properties of students.

In all, the proposed research represents a practical semiformal methodical tool levering machine learning to study and predict educational equity gaps. It is highly applicable. In the future, we would like to explore this research by looking for alternatives that will help us meet the above-mentioned major challenges.

## IV. RESULT  AND  ANALYSIS

The proposed system includes the use of various machine learning algorithms and an AI chatbot that is capable of giving answers to any query coming from a student.

The  performance  of  classification  models  is  determined using  a  confusion  matrix  and  accuracy  plot.  A  confusion matrix is used to evaluate the performance of a classification model in machine learning representing their accuracy. It gives the number of True Positives(TP), True Negatives(TN), False Positives(FP), and False Negatives(FN).

- 1) True positives (TP) are the values in which the examples are correctly determined as positive
- 2) False positives (FP) are the values in which the examples are negative but are determined as positive.
- 3) True negatives (TN) are the values in which the examples are correctly determined as negative
- 4) False negatives (FN)  are the values in which  the examples are positive but are determined as negative.

F-measure=2 * (precision*recall)/(precision+recall)

Error- rate = 1-accuracy

## Abstract

Deaf learners in the Global South struggle to access equitable education, in particular,  there  are  few  instances  where  they  can  be  facilitated  in  inclusive  classrooms. The challenges include a lack of teachers that can sign proficiently, the unavailabil -ity of interpreters and few teachers trained in Deaf education. This paper explores the  potential  and  challenges  of  an  approach  that  aims  to  facilitate  real-time  communication between Deaf students and non-signing teachers, by combining speech and language technologies, computer vision, machine translation and 3-D avatars, all  underpinned  by  artificial  intelligence. A  closed-loop  solution  is  proposed  that takes  advantage  advances  in  gesture  recognition,  machine  translation  and  speech and language technologies to allow for communication between Deaf students and hearing  teachers,  both  using  their  preferred  medium  of  communication.  A  focus group was held with members of the Deaf community to gauge their interest and concerns about the proposed system. The results revealed a fair level of curiosity and concern. Five major themes emerged: Avatar Effectiveness and Realism; Lan -guage and Cultural Considerations; Resource and Cost Concerns; Real-time Trans -lation Challenges and Community Involvement and Feedback. The results revealed important issues concerning bilingual-bicultural access for the hearing teacher and the need for sign language production systems to factor in the nuances of unfamiliar concepts, essentially emulating the role of Deaf Relay Interpreters.

## Introduction

Worldwide, it is estimated that 430 million people have hearing loss requiring rehabilitation  (World  Health  Organisation,  2024).  Factors  such  as  hearing  thresholds, type and severity of hearing loss, age of onset of hearing loss, family makeup and socio-cultural  background,  shape  the  Deaf 1 individual  and  impact  their  access  to education  (Guardino  et  al.,  2018 ;  Domagała-Zyśk, 2013 ;  Humphries  et  al., 2012 ; Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2012 ). Approximately 80% of the Deaf population, world -wide, do not have access to any education (World Federation of the Deaf, 2016 ); this figure is heavily influenced by the fact that 80% of Deaf persons live in low- and middle-income countries, where access to education is not equitable (World Health Organisation, 2024 ). This problem carries forward, as many struggle to find mean -ingful employment (O'Connell, 2023 ;  Gupta et al., 2023 );  in  one  extreme case, it has been shown that approximately 75% of secondary-level Deaf graduates have no marketable skills by school leaving age (Planning Institute of Jamaica, 2016). This leads to the unfortunate circumstance, where a significant proportion of the Deaf are unemployed or underemployed (Dammeyer, 2019 ; Garberoglio et al., 2019, Todorov, 2022). This is not surprising, as the lack of formal education, pointed to above, must surely negatively impact employability. Even where attempts are made to provide education, accessibility of the education offered is a challenge (Straaten et al., 2021). There are many reasons for this, but the most significant are a lack of teachers trained in Deaf education, interpreters, assistive devices and other tools to facilitate accessibility (Lee &amp; Low, 2013 ; Leyser et al., 2011 ; Sharma &amp; Das, 2015 ; Wapling, 2016 ; Kelly et al., 2022b). Where they do have access to education, the options for the Deaf are either to be educated at home, in specialist schools for the Deaf, inclusion in mainstream schools with no additional support, or inclusion in mainstream schools with specialist support (Coy, 2022 ; Silvestri &amp; Hartman, 2022).

In the Global North (the so-called, developed world), inclusive education often means that Deaf children attend mainstream schools where they are supported by a wide variety of technological devices and human assistance, including interpreters and teachers who sign (Humphries et al., 2012 ; Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2012). This ecosystem is important for the Deaf to make the most of their opportunity to gain an education. Despite the investment in resources to support Deaf education, it is well documented that Deaf students generally have lower levels of  educational  attainment (Järvelin et al., 1997 ; Richardson, 2001 ; Rydberg et al., 2009 ; Sharma et al., 2023 ; Muller et al., 2023). This includes the Deaf in rich nations, such as the United States, The United Kingdom, Sweden and Finland (Garberoglio, 2021 ; Marschark et al., 2015 ; Hutchinson, 2023 ; Antonson et al., 2006 ; Rydberg et al., 2009 ; Järvelin et al., 1997 ). A recent large, longitudinal, retrospective study of primary school student achievement in the Netherlands, between 2008 and 2018, showed that Deaf students had lower scores in Mathematics and Language than their hearing counterparts, even when the Deaf students attended mainstream schools (van der Straaten et al., 2021). The Deaf attending mainstream elementary schools (approximately two-thirds of the Deaf students in the study) went on to the same secondary schools as their hearing counterparts, despite having lower scores. According to the study, the Deaf students attending specialised schools were far more likely to attend lower-level post-primary educational institutions and rarely went on to post-secondary education or vocational training.

1 Deaf is used to mean a person with hearing loss significant enough to negatively impact their education (Jarvis et al., 2002).



While it is widely reported that Deaf students in mainstream education are at a significant  advantage  to  their  counterparts  in  specialist  schools,  questions  remain about the suitability  of  this  approach,  even  in  the  developed  economies  in  which they are prevalent (Kreimeyer, 2000 ; Silvestri &amp; Hartman, 2022). Owing to the challenges with background noise, low level conversations and challenges following the instructions  of  teachers,  'instructions  in  mainstream  settings  are  not  always  communicatively accessible' to the Deaf (van der Straaten et al., 2021, p. 407). Inclusion in mainstream classrooms is largely facilitated by the use of cochlear implants (CI) by deaf students (Muller et al., 2023). Notwithstanding this drive, Deaf students with CI's have a number of challenges related to their use including challenges in acquiring the nuances of spoken language and a lack of sign language; this leaves them at a disadvantage academically and socially, as they do not have a natural place in either the hearing or the Deaf communities (Archbold, 2015 ; Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2015 ; Hilviu et al., 2021 ; Šantić &amp; Bonetti, 2023). Wearers of hearing aids are also at a disadvantage in mainstream education, as they are often thought to be performing relatively well, compared to CI users, and are thus offered less support (Marschark et al., 2015). In many countries it has been recognised that Deaf students, even those with some degree of oral language, benefit academically and socially from a bilin -gual-bicultural  approach,  which  incorporates  sign  language  and  Deaf  culture  into mainstream education (Skerrit, 2020 ; Dammeyer &amp; Marschark, 2016). This approach better aligns with the ideal inclusion model as it meets the needs of the Deaf rather than requiring them to adapt to the provisions available to the regular stream of students in the system. Despite the benefits of this approach, it has not been widely adopted (World Federation of the Deaf, 2016) owing to disagreements about the most suitable  approach  to  educating  Deaf  individuals  and  concerns  about  the  potential harm that bilingualism can cause to the Deaf individual's ability to acquire spoken language (Jones, 2021 ;  Pontecorvo, 2023). The challenge is made even more significant, as less than 4% of children acquire sign language from birth as a result of exposure to Deaf parents who sign (Mitchell &amp; Karchmer, 2004). The implication of this is that over 95% of Deaf/hard of hearing (D/hh) children are born to parents who are hearing and whose primary means of communication is a spoken language (Archbold, 2015 ; Terry, 2023). These parents face the daunting challenge of being unprepared for the birth of the D/hh child, which includes the lack of proficiency in a language that is accessible to their child (Humphries et al., 2019).

The bilingual-bicultural model of inclusive education may not be feasible in the developing world, as there are challenges that are unique to the developing world given their smaller economies, approach to education and attitudes to the Deaf. There are questions about the acceptability of this approach, given that there are technical, financial and socio-cultural challenges that attend this approach to equitable educa -tion (Cooper, 2019 ;  Punch &amp; Hyde, 2011). Some of the issues include the cost of assistive devices, maintenance and rehabilitation costs as well as a shortage of trained surgeons to implant CIs and audiologists to support the Deaf in their use (Magro et al.,  2018 ;  Jesuyajolu et al., 2023). It must be noted that some studies have shown that CIs are cost-effective in low- and middle-income countries (Emmett et al., 2016, 2019 ; Emmett et al., 2015). However, that does not appear to be the reality on the ground, as, globally, less than 1% of people who could potentially benefit from the use of a CI have access to one (Bodington et al., 2021 ). Another reason why the bilingual-bicultural model may not work in the Global South is that the typical model of inclusive education is predicated on the assumption that the student has spoken language, or at least, the ability to read the main language of their country, which is not always the case for the Deaf (Counselman, Meltzer &amp; Marquart, 2020 ; Tang, 2024 ;  Alanazi, 2021 ; Gagnon et al., 2021 ; Tsach &amp; Most, 2016). This is because reading proficiency is achieved through phonological awareness, phonics, word recogni -tion, vocabulary acquisition and fluency, skills that are not accessible to many Deaf persons (Hartman et al., 2019 ; Runnion &amp; Gray, 2019 ; Wass et al., 2019). It should be noted however, that some profoundly Deaf learners have been able to develop their reading skills through the acquisition of sign language as their first language (Verlinden et al., 2005 ; Howerton-Fox &amp; Falk, 2019).



Even if assistive  technologies  were  widely  accessible  in  the  Global  South,  the bilingual-bicultural approach is not culturally responsive to all Deaf communities, in  particular  those  communities  that  would  prefer  to  not  use  a  spoken  language. Having a culturally appropriate solution to the education of the Deaf is critical, as some within the Deaf community view the use of assistive devices (such as cochlear implants and hearing aids) as an attack on Deaf culture and are openly hostile to the use of the technology and to those who use it (Cooper, 2019 ; Sparrow, 2010 ; Coy, 2022). Such an approach must give prominence to the use of sign language in Deaf education, given it is the native language of the Deaf and they should not be forced to  rely  on  spoken  language  as  their  only  means  to  achieving  equity  in  education. The challenges for Deaf education are different in the Global North and south (the developing world). However, there is compelling evidence, from the Global North, that allowing Deaf learners to use sign language as they engage in the teaching and learning process, has tremendous benefits for their overall development (Dammeyer &amp; Marschark, 2016 ; Nielsen, 2016 ; Goodwin &amp; Lillo-Martin, 2019 ; Hoffmeister et al.,  2022)  and  that  depriving  them  of  an  accessible  language  is  certainly  harmful (Secora &amp; Smith, 2021 ; Hall et al., 2019).

In summary, inclusive Deaf education is inadequate in both the Global North and south. In most inclusive classrooms in the Global North, sign language is not always accommodated (Powell et al., 2014 ; Constantinou et al., 2020) or attempts to incorporate sign language is done through teachers who aren't trained, or with the use of interpreters who are not teachers, which means that students often miss out on important information (Ngobeni et al., 2020 ; Jones et al., 2021 ; Kelly et al., 2022b). In the Global South, assistive technologies are not readily available, nor are trained teachers who sign. This leaves a space for the use of technology to fill the gaps evident in the mainstream classrooms of both domains - the need for increased use of sign language in  bilingual-bicultural  Deaf  education  in  the  north  and  the  need  for  a  system  for facilitating sign language use in the south, where the vast majority of the Deaf have no access to the language of instruction. While there have been some calls for the inclusion of technology to enhance access to education for the Deaf (Constantinou et al., 2020 ; Lynch et al., 2024) - including recent calls for systems incorporating, or developed by, artificial intelligence (Sreemathy et al., 2023 ; Levesque et al., 2023) - the existing literature lacks a proposal for a comprehensive solution to the myriad challenges outlined.



This paper proposes a novel approach to the provision of education for the Deaf as stakeholders seek to have equitable Deaf education world-wide. The solution is applicable to the Global South, given that the majority of the challenges, and potential users, exist there. Additionally, it has been designed for potential future use, with appropriate modifications, in the Global North, as the challenges are not unique to the developing world. A framework and approach are proposed that employ the extensive use of AI solutions to facilitate the education of the Deaf in mainstream classrooms, even if they have no access to spoken language and their teacher is unable to sign. The solution is envisioned in such a way as to be culturally appropriate and tailored to the Deaf, while not being disruptive to the hearing students or the teacher.

The research is guided by the following research questions:

1. What are the main components needed for a closed-loop system that foster inclusive Deaf education in the Global South?
2. What are the most important concerns of the Deaf community as it relates to the development and use of such a system.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 explores the state of Deaf education in both Global North and south contexts, common barriers to inclusive Deaf education and how AI can play a pivotal role in fostering inclusive Deaf education. The guiding theoretical framework is presented in Sect. 3, which is followed by a detailed description of the proposed solution, its value, and potential limitations. A focus group, exploring the reaction of members of the Deaf community in Jamaica to the proposed solution, is described in Sect. 5. The results, analysis and discussion follow and the paper is concluded in Sect. 8.

## Issues Impacting Deaf Education

## An Overview of Deaf Education

Equity in education for the disabled is a global imperative, which is supported by a  variety  of  international  organisations  (Barton  &amp;  Armstrong, 2007 ;  UNESCO, 2005 ). After several international declarations, including most recently, the Agenda for  Sustainable  Development  education  goal  (SDG  4),  most  Deaf  students  in  the Global North are educated in inclusive classrooms. This, however, is not universally accepted as producing the most beneficial outcomes for those in the disabled com -munity (Venail et al., 2010 ;  Sarant et al., 2015 ;  Nunes et al., 2001 ;  Krijger et al., 2020). Nonetheless, the drive to have Deaf students in inclusive education continues unabated because it is believed that inclusion is the pathway to equitable education (UNESCO, 2001 ; OECD, 2003).



The widespread implantation of the cochlear implant has been credited with bolstering  the  effort  to  move,  almost  exclusively,  toward  inclusive  schooling  in  the developed world (Archbold, 2015 ;  Marschark et al., 2019 ;  Duarte et al., 2016 ). A CI is a surgically implanted device that by-passes the normal acoustic pathways to provide the Deaf user with a modified sense of sound; a CI does not help the Deaf to recover lost hearing. Thus, while there are noted benefits to the education of the Deaf, the overall impact is mixed. Early implantation, coupled with speech and lan -guage training specific to the access to speech sounds that the CI provides, is shown to confer educational and employment benefits to Deaf students with CIs compared to those without them (Venail et al., 2010 ; Sarant et al., 2015 ; Spencer et al., 2012 ; Huber, 2018 ; Alasim, 2021 ). However, there are also challenges, including: difficulty in acquiring the finer points of spoken language, social and a loss of educational sup -port as they grow older (Archbold, 2015 ; Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2015).

In many developed countries, the majority of Deaf children access inclusive education. They are supported by personal assistive devices, such as hearing aids, induction loops, frequency modulation (FM) systems, and cochlear implants, as well as classroom teachers who sign, interpreters or itinerant teachers of the Deaf (Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2012). The use of assistive technology for Deaf education has become so normalised, only 1-2% of Deaf people across the globe access education through sign language (World Federation of the Deaf, 2016). This is unfortunate, as many Deaf people, particularly those in the developed world, function as bimodal bilinguals; this ability to utilise both oral and sign language is known to impact the way they learn (Knoors &amp; Marschark, 2012). Deaf children need to be exposed to sign language at an early age, given that delayed language acquisition leads to language delay and lower levels  of  academic  attainment  (Dammeyer &amp; Marschark, 2016 ;  Magnuson, 2000 ). A lack of support for bilingualism has implications for relationships with the Deaf community, which can leave the Deaf at risk of isolation and a lack of identity, especially in CI users who may have challenges with oral language (Bat-Chava &amp; Deignan, 2001 ; Wheeler et al., 2007 ; Punch &amp; Hyde, 2011).

An understanding of the value of bilingualism has led to support for bilingualbicultural education in countries such as Sweden, Norway and Denmark (Swanwick, 2014 ;  Dammeyer  &amp;  Marschark, 2016),  the  United  Kingdom  (Powers,  2002),  the United States (Humphries, 2013), Hong Kong (Tang, 2017 ) and Zimbabwe (Sibanda &amp; Tlale,  2019).  These  programmes  include  all  stakeholders:  family,  teachers  and administrators.

## Education of the Deaf in the Global South

The developed world has, generally speaking, subscribed to a model of equity in education for the Deaf referred to as inclusion. This is feasible because of the availability of assistive technology, which is readily available in these contexts. Assistive technologies are not as readily available in the developing world, which has a profound negative impact on the educational and overall life opportunities of the Deaf in developing countries (de Witte et al., 2019 ; Matter et al., 2017 ; McPherson, 2014). Thus, even though developing countries, such Brazil, Chile, Jamaica, Nigeria and South Africa (Tanure Alves et al., 2021 ; Rosas et al., 2023 ; McArthur, Ewen-Smith &amp;



Scott, 2021 ; Jesuyajolu et al., 2023 ; Walton &amp; Engelbrecht, 2022), are moving in that direction, a number of issues, including culture, lack of infrastructure and financial resources to support inclusion act as barriers (Lee &amp; Low, 2013 ; Leyser et al., 2011 ; Kisanga, 2022 ; Ngobeni et al., 2020 ; Kelly et al., 2022b).

Some developing countries attempt to offer inclusive education. However, what often  occurs  is  simply  the  application  of  an  operational  definition  of  inclusion achieved by providing the Deaf a place in mainstream schools. Without the required technical and physical infrastructure specialist teachers and learning resources, this approach is of little value to the Deaf (Singal, 2008 ; Ngcobo &amp; Muthukrishna, 2011 ; Sharma &amp; Das, 2015 ; Wapling, 2016). Often, the Deaf are not well catered for in mainstream schools, in part because they are not well understood, teachers do not have specialist training, and sign language is not the language of instruction (Arm -strong, 2005 ; Kelly et al., 2022).

## Barriers to Inclusive Deaf Education in the Global South

While countries have the best of intentions as it concerns equitable education, there is not always the effort required to follow through on this promise. Budgetary alloca -tions for educating the Deaf have been cut in developing and developed countries (Mertzani, 2015 ; National Deaf Children's Society, 2019 ; Strauss, 2019). This limits what can be done to provide the required accommodations for the Deaf learner, whether in a mainstream school or a Deaf only specialist school.

Without a coherent policy and overarching legislative framework covering Deaf education in many developing countries, there is a significant impediment to equi -table education (Mutasa, 2000 ). Without a systematic effort to provide support for the Deaf learner, all attempts to move to inclusive education will end in failure and be to the detriment of the Deaf (Cheong et al., 2019 ). Barriers exist, even where resources are available, they may not be accessible. The cost of assistive technology is either too high, skilled doctors are not available, or the Deaf community is unaware of the availability or benefits of assistive devices (McPherson, 2014).

Inaccessibility is expressed in other ways also. At times, projectors, and tablets, are provided to support Deaf education. For the Deaf student without assistive devices, reading from a projector, lip-reading, and working with an interpreter may cause cognitive overload, given the attempt to integrate the information from all three sources (Domagała-Zyśk, 2013). Much of the information displayed using these visual aids is presented as text. This assumes that the Deaf student is fluent in the language of instruction, which is not always the case.

The Deaf have historically  been  seen  as  being  disabled  and  without  prospects for a productive life (Hensley, 2015). Often, they are treated as though they need to be cured of a terrible affliction (Humphries et al., 2012 ; Sparrow, 2005). There is a significant backlash against those attitudes, as many Deaf persons are quite proud of their Deafness. These members of the community view Deafness as part of their cultural identity and sign language as a linguistic marker of this identity (Chapman &amp; Dammeyer, 2017 ; Foley &amp; Beth, 2012 ; Goggin et al., 2003). To these individuals, the implantation of cochlear implants in young Deaf children is seen as a form of cultural erasure or ethnocide (Sparrow, 2010 ).  As a result of these and other concerns, many culturally Deaf individuals refuse to use assistive devices and reject the model of inclusive education championed in the developed world. They prefer, instead, a model that allows them to express themselves culturally, where sign language is the language of instruction (Chapman &amp; Dammeyer, 2017). This antagonism has led to some members of the community ostracising users of assistive devices, often because they are not fluent in sign language and are perceived to have rejected Deaf culture (Leigh, 2009).



A significant barrier to inclusive education for the Deaf is the lack of trained teach -ers  of  the  Deaf  or  even  trained  teachers  who  sign  (Gamal,  2022 ;  De  Meulder  &amp; Haualand, 2021). Where teachers do not have these specialist skills, they believe that teaching a Deaf child is difficult and should only be done by those with the particular skill sets (Andrews et al., 2021 ; Walton &amp; Engelbrecht, 2022 ). A number of solu -tions have been proposed to deal with the shortage of teachers who sign, including: training more teachers to sign (World Federation of the Deaf, 2018 , Alawajee, 2022), providing support for in-service teachers (Kelly et al., 2022a, b) and hiring more sign language interpreters to support learning (De Meulder &amp; Haualand, 2021 ). Each approach has its own challenges. Budgets for hiring additional, sign language trained, teachers are constrained (Makoelle &amp; Burmistrova, 2020) The use of sign language interpreters to facilitate communication has been shown to limit direct teacher communication with the Deaf student (Cawthon, 2001). It has even been suggested that the use of an interpreter in class goes against the spirit of inclusion by isolating the Deaf student from direct contact with the teacher (Murray et al., 2018).

Thus, the goal of inclusive education for the Deaf in the Global South - the provision of accessible education in mainstream classrooms - is often reduced to minimal representations, where teachers either, use sign to support their speech, which is not direct communication what is said, and is not accessible to all Deaf learners (De Meulder et al., 2019 ); or they use just enough sign language to get by, but not enough to ensure equity for the Deaf learner (Swanwick Goodchild &amp; Adami, 2022 ); or teachers may pick up a few key phrases, which can do more harm than good (Scott &amp; Henner, 2021).

## The Role of AI in Educating the Deaf

For more than three decades, artificial intelligence has been touted as a disruptor of traditional approaches to education (Schank &amp; Edelson, 1989 ; McArthur, 1995 ; Seldon &amp; Abidoye, 2018 ; Williamson &amp; Eynon, 2020 ). Important applications of AI, such as speech recognition and synthesis (Verma et al., 2021) performance monitoring (Kumar, 2019) adaptive learning (De Melo, 2014), interactive learning environments (Ijaz, 2017) and intelligent tutoring systems (Weitekamp, 2020) all hold great promise for easing the burden on teachers, enhancing student engagement and improving learning outcomes. As computing technology and computing power have evolved, there has been an explosion in the number of AI-driven applications devel -oped for  education.  Most  recently,  there  has  been  an  explosion  of  interest  in AIbased text generation technologies (technically, generative language models), such as ChatGPT (OpenAI, 2023 ). Despite initial concern, educators are finding that, when properly used, these technologies can be extremely useful in enhancing the teaching and learning experience (Zhai, 2022).



Beyond the contribution of AI to education, generally, there are specific communi -cations technologies that have been improved by modern AI techniques, which have particular implications for Deaf education. Consider gesture recognition and analysis and machine translation, which all play a role in improving access for learners who do not benefit from traditional approaches to teaching and learning. For inclusive education, the Deaf have particular needs that AI can help to address. Firstly, in a traditional classroom, it is unlikely that the teacher will have the ability to sign. Here, tools developed using AI and deep learning can be used to allow the Deaf student to communicate his ideas to a teacher that does not sign and for the teacher to communicate seamlessly with the Deaf student. With the modern AI techniques and access to high performance computing, the role of AI in enabling equitable education for the Deaf has never been more evident or more accessible. Given the issues of equity in education of the Deaf, and the issues of transfer of this concept from the developed world, it is clear that AI is required to address the cultural issues that present themselves  when  Deaf  students  are  included  in  mainstream  classrooms  (Mohammed &amp; Watson, 2019 ).  Here, one must consider issues of fairness in AI. The lack of representative data in training and inaccessible interfaces are issues that impact the usefulness of AI for the Deaf (Kaffle et al., 2019 ). Bragg et al., have considered the 'bias, privacy, accuracy, and over-promotion' issues related to the promise and implementation of AI technologies for people with disabilities (2023, p. 1). Their analysis of the FATE (Fairness, Accountability, Transparency and Ethics) of AI data use, as it relates to the sign language translation, highlights the fact that it could exacerbate some of the traditional discrimination and oppression of the Deaf (Bragg et al., 2021 ). Additionally, there is concern that the advent of sign language translation sys -tems has led to the ambition of replacing human signers with AI-based avatars before these systems are good enough to effectively replace human signers (Al-Khazraji et al., 2018 , Kaffle et al., 2019).

There has long been a recognition of the potential for AI to positively impact Deaf education  (Parton,  2006).  Parton's  review  explored  research  into  computer-aided instruction, sign language recognition, sign language synthesis and signing avatars. Almost 20 years later, very few of these technologies have made their way into class -rooms and there is no coherent approach to using AI to enhance the teaching and learning  experience  for  the  Deaf  community. This  gap  has  been  highlighted  by  a recent review of technologies for inclusive Deaf education in which the AI-based studies reviewed featured applications for one-way translation of speech-to-sign or sign-to-speech, as well as speech-to-text and speech synthesis solutions (RodríguezCorrea et al., 2023 ). The contention of this paper is that this is insufficient, and that an AI-based system that simultaneously allows for signing by the Deaf student and speaking by the non-signing teacher is required for inclusive Deaf education in the

Global South.



## Previous Solutions for Deaf Education

Previous work aimed at accommodating Deaf students in classrooms with hearing students have proposed solutions that can broadly be grouped into three categories: systems that convert speech to text (Iglesias et al., 2014 ;  Butler, Trager &amp; Behn., 2019 ); systems that convert speech into a transcript that drives a signing avatar (Bou -zid &amp; Jemni, 2020 ; Adamo-Villani &amp; Anasingaraju, 2017 ); and systems that convert sign to text (Reis et al., 2015 ; AlShammari et al., 2018 ; Bragg et al., 2019).

Iglesias et al. (2014 ) propose a speech-to-text system under the APEINTA project. The system produced real-time captions from the speech of the teacher, which was either projected or sent to a mobile device for the Deaf student to read. The recognition system utilised a commercial automatic speech recognition (ASR) system Dragon Naturally Speaking - to produce captions for Spanish students. Butler, Trager &amp; Behn ( 2019 ) implemented a captioning system, driven by ASR, as a means of pro -viding university students with multimodal access to spoken language. The students also had access to sign language interpreters. The researchers reported good reviews for the captioning system, despite the errors of the ASR system.

Sign language avatar systems have been making significant strides over the past 40 years, due in large part to increasing computing power and, more recently, deep learning algorithms (Aziz &amp; Othman, 2023 ; Rastgoo et al., 2023). Dhanjal and Singh (2022 ) developed a system that automatically converts speech to Indian Sign Lan -guage (ISL). The system combines a multi-language ASR with machine translation techniques,  transforming  the ASR  transcript  into  HamNoSys  sign  language  nota -tion (Hanke, 2004 ). A signing avatar was then developed and deployed for testing. The resulting accuracies and acceptability ratings were favourable and the authors pointed to the value of the tool for educational as well as social settings. The reviews by Bouzid &amp; Jemni ( 2020) point to a number of speech-to-avatar as well as text-toavatar systems, using a variety of technologies, including machine learning. Many of these systems were tested in the classroom with favourable results.

An  emerging  trend  is  the  use  of  avatars  for  communication  with  the  Deaf  in mixed-reality (MR) classrooms (Miller et al., 2017 ; Adamo-Villani &amp; Anasingaraju, 2017 ). Yang, Mousas &amp; Adamo ( 2022 ) built an MR application for use in teaching K-1 Math lessons. They used motion capture technology (OptiTrack, StretchSense, and FaceWare software) to create an avatar (using Autodesk's Motion Builder) of a professional deaf signer going through the lessons. Participants in the study (eight, hearing adults) found the appearance acceptable and the signing function, this despite the high cognitive demand of interacting with the system. The major challenge with the study was that it was not done with Deaf children in a real classroom. The study by Imashev et al. (2024) sought to overcome this with a study of 21 Deaf children in an inclusive classroom using MR to teach lessons on animals.

As with sign language production system, sign language recognition has been rev -olutionised since the introduction of deep learning - see Núñez-Marcos et al. (2023) for a review. However, very few applications have been developed directly for inclusive education and fewer have been tested in the classroom environment.

Papadimitriou et al. (2023) developed a web-based, Greek sign language recognition system for use in education. They employed a 3D convolutional neural network



(CNN) for isolated word recognition as well as MobileNet, a 2D-CNN for continuous fingerspelling. A bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) encoder was used to combine the separate streams. The system was tested with 23 users, a mixture of university students and system developers. Quantitative analysis of the accuracy showed  that  the  system  worked  well  for  multi-signer  models,  but  degraded  with signer-independent recognition. The user experience was reported to be positive.

There are three major drawbacks with the speech to text approach. Firstly, these systems assume that the Deaf have a command of written language, which most do not (Volpato et al., 2018). Secondly, there are most often errors in the transcribed text (Marschark et al., 2005 ; Marchut, 2017). Finally, switching between sources of information (a screen and the instructor, for instance) increases the cognitive load and makes learning more challenging (Kushalnagar, 2016). Speech to avatar systems go  beyond  producing  a  written  transcript,  using  that  transcript  to  drive  an  avatar that signs. This type of system allows the Deaf to understand what was said by the teacher, but it does not allow the Deaf to sign and be understood. Similarly, sign to text systems perform gesture recognition to produce text from the student. However, if the instructor does not sign, the communication with the student is limited.

Each of these AI-based technologies is useful by themselves, but do not address, at the same time, the needs of the Deaf in an inclusive way. For instance, a sign-tospeech system will allow for the Deaf student to communicate their thoughts to the teacher, but the teacher who does not sign, will not be able to respond directly. This is where a sign language interpreter would be called upon to convey information to the student. However, recent advances in sign language avatars (see, Bouzid &amp; Jemni, 2020 and Atasoy, 2023 , for reviews) promise to address this deficit.

Despite the existence of a number of enabling technologies, there is still a need for a solution that allows for the hearing teacher and the Deaf student to communicate seamlessly. Given the myriad challenges in increasing the number of Deaf teachers, hearing teachers that sign, or sign language interpreters, in mainstream classrooms, the most practical approach is to develop closed-loop AI-based systems that will be deployed alongside the hearing teacher that will allow the teacher to speak and the student to sign and to have them understand each other using their preferred mode of communication. No such system currently exists; the system being proposed in this paper is meant to fill this gap. The following section discusses the theoretical frame -work that guides the development of the solution.

## Theoretical Framework

This section explores the theoretical framework that informs the development of the proposed inclusive solution that aims to facilitate communication and learning for Deaf students in classrooms with hearing students and a hearing teacher, that does not sign, where no interpreter is present. One theoretical framework that informs the development of the inclusive solution is Positioning Theory. Positioning Theory facilitates an exploration of language, including sign languages and other sign systems, as dynamic, multifaceted discourses while recognising how people actively choose and change their roles in conversations, making language a lively and complex interac- tion. It also posits that individuals construct their identities and perspectives within social contexts, including their positioning within social contexts, their relationships with others and interactions with various discourses and power structures. Positioning Theory emphasises how individuals adopt certain positions or roles in discourse. The positions are seen as fluid and changeable over time, depending on the context and the discourses. The value of Positioning Theory to the proposed system lies in the insight it provides into how individuals navigate social interactions and construct meaning in specific contexts. Specific to the development and deployment of a sign -ing avatar for Deaf students in mainstream or inclusive education settings, Positioning Theory informs this research in promoting: (1) an understanding of identity and the positioning of Deaf students in the classroom, (2) the empowering of agency on the part of Deaf students, (3) navigation of the power dynamics of social interactions with the Deaf students and their hearing teaching and student counterparts and (4) facilitating a more inclusive education experience for the Deaf.



In addition to Positioning Theory, the theoretical framework also integrates aspects of Universal Design for Learning (UDL), Cultural Responsiveness, Vygotsky's Poly -glossia, Sociocultural Interactions, and the Matching Person and Technology Model.

UDL  is  a  framework  that  emphasizes  designing  curriculum  and  instructional materials to accommodate the widest range of learners possible without the need for adaptation or retrofitting (Hitchcock et al., 2002 ). Applying UDL principles to the development of technology for deaf students involves ensuring multiple means of representation, engagement, and expression. For instance, the technology will support various modes of communication, such as sign language and speech, to cater to diverse learning preferences and abilities and to reduce the burden on the school and classroom teacher, who would have to hire and accommodate a sign language interpreter to support the Deaf learners.

Combining cultural responsiveness with UDL - as proposed by Silvesti and Hart -man (2022) - allows for the recognition and valuing of the various cultural backgrounds, identities, and experiences of students and teachers. For Deaf students, their cultural identity often revolves around sign language and Deaf culture (Andersson &amp; Adams Lyngbäck, 2022). Therefore, the technology should not only support sign language but should also incorporate features that reflect the linguistic and cultural nuances of the deaf community. This aspect of the solution should be highlighted during training of classroom teachers, so that they can provide access to resources and content that are culturally relevant and affirming for deaf students.

Vygotsky's concept of polyglossia emphasises the coexistence of multiple languages and forms of communication within a social context (Vygotsky, 1987). In the context of developing technology for deaf students, polyglossia underscores the importance of supporting both sign language and spoken language as complementary means of communication. Even though the majority of Deaf students in the Global South, who this solution is initially aimed at, do not use spoken language, those that do will benefit from the multimodal input. The technology should facilitate dynamic interactions between sign language and spoken language, enabling seamless translation and comprehension for both the deaf student and the teacher.

Sociocultural interactions (Vygotsky, 1987) refer to the social exchanges and collaborative learning experiences that occur within a cultural context. For deaf students in mainstream classrooms, effective communication and interaction with peers and teachers are crucial for academic and social development. The technology will allow for multiple Deaf students to be accommodated in a mainstream classroom, which should promote meaningful socio-cultural interactions by fostering inclusive communication environments where Deaf students can actively participate and engage with their peers in academic discussions, group activities, and social interactions. As currently envisioned, the tool does not allow for interactions of the Deaf with their hearing counterparts, which is a limitation that can be addressed once the first version is deployed.



The Matching Person and Technology (MPT) Model emphasises the importance of aligning technological solutions with the unique needs, preferences, and abilities of individual users (Martinez, 2018). When designing technology for Deaf students, it is essential to consider factors such as language proficiency, communication pref -erences, sensory capabilities, and educational goals. The technology should be customisable and adaptable to accommodate the diverse needs and language preferences of Deaf students, empowering them to fully participate and succeed in mainstream educational settings.

By  integrating  the  principles  of  the  various  theoretical  models,  a  tool  should emerge that considers every aspect of inclusive Deaf education. This comprehensive approach to the development of technology for Deaf students in mainstream classrooms, it is thought that the barriers of sign language communication can be removed and that a more inclusive learning environment can be fostered, to enhance educational opportunities and empower Deaf students to thrive academically and socially.

## A Proposal for AI-Enabled Inclusive Deaf Education

Given the reluctance of some in the Deaf community to consider inclusive education that  is  not  culturally  sensitive  or  accessible,  an  equitable  solution  has  to  be  envisioned. The solution must allow the Deaf person to engage in learning using their native language and not require them to learn to lip-read, or to demand that they have a command of the majority language of their country.

The proposed approach addresses shortcomings in these systems, by facilitating real-time two-way communication between Deaf students and non-signing teachers. In order to achieve this, the approach combines speech and language technologies, computer vision, machine translation and 3-d avatars. The most significant differ -ence between the proposed approach and others is that none of those currently in existence advance a closed loop system (see Sect. 2.4, for a discussion). That is, they do not allow for real time communication between the hearing and the Deaf. This innovation will make the proposed system the first of its kind. Acceptance of the system, by both the Deaf and the hearing, is more likely, as it allows them to interact using the mode of communication with which each is most comfortable. One major drawback would be the realism of the signs produced by the avatar (Coy, 2022). This system would have to be able to interpret the non-verbal features of communication that impact meaning and convey such meaning in the signs produced by the avatar. Machine translation between spoken and sign language is another potential challenge of the project. Unlike machine translation between spoken languages, the machine translation from sign language to spoken language would need to interpret the nonmanual markers of the sign utterances of students and convey the appropriate meaning in the translated utterance.



The proposed system will allow for the integration of Deaf students in main-stream classrooms with a teacher that is not competent in sign language. This will allow for improved education and increased employment opportunities for Deaf students, who currently  have  few  prospects  for  intellectual  development  or  social  advancement. Also, exposing hearing individuals to the Deaf could change attitudes to the Deaf. It is anticipated that the main output of this work will be a system that allows a Deaf child to be able to attend the school of her choice all the way through to university without the need for expensive training for teachers and support staff or the require -ment for Deaf students to adapt to  learning  in  English. This  is  true  equity. What currently obtains in much of the developing world is that the Deaf attend specialist schools with little technological innovation, no curriculum specific to Deaf educa -tion, and no structured pathway to higher education. The proposed system will offer the Deaf a choice of schools, where they can learn in their own language and will have access to the type of education that is suitable for their chosen career path.

The proposed system would make it feasible for the voice of the Deaf to be heard and  for  their  linguistic  and  cultural  positioning  in  the  classroom  to  be  validated. When a human interpreter is in the classroom, the Deaf student has to rely on them to ask for clarification, request feedback, and express a breakdown in comprehension. In these instances, the Deaf students may fail to experience self-agency. Positioning Theory acknowledges the influence of power dynamics on social interactions and therefore the deployment of a signing avatar empowers the Deaf student to activlely  and  instantly  enagage  in  communication  and  learning  despite  the  absence  of the ideal- a signing educator. In the context of hearing teachers and peers interacting during teaching and learning activities and especially those featuring collaborative processes, the use of a signing avatar helps to mitigate the power imbalance by facilitaing equitable access to communication and the learning resources. Deaf students would now have to opportunity for fuller participation in classroom discourse and the dependency syndrome would be stymied. By leveraging Positioning Theory through the use of the signing avatar, this solution promotes inclusive practices and accommodates this crucial diverse need that revolves around the language deprivation issue that defines communication experiences of the Deaf. Indeed the application of Positioning Theory to the development and deployment of the signing avatar for Deaf students in school placements where their teachers and peers are both hearing and do not sign, heralds the value of recognizing and validating students' identities, empowering their sense of agency, navigating the power dynamics during classroom discourse and promoting inclusive education practices.

## System Structure

The objective of this research is to design and implement a system that will facilitate real-time, direct communication between the Deaf and hearing in the classroom.



In  order  for  the  Deaf  to  understand  the  hearing,  the  system  will  accept  real-time, continuous conversational speech as input and produce sign language, signed by an animated avatar. When the Deaf person signs, it is recognised by the system which produces an output of spoken English so that the hearing individual can understand what was signed. Figure 1 outlines the main steps involved in the proposed solution, which can be deployed directly in the Global South. Given the modularity of the system, specific modifications could make it usable in the Global North. An ASR system capable of accurate real-time speech recognition will be developed. Such a system is possible, given recent advances in end-to-end, low-latency ASR systems that work in real-time, even for low-resource languages (Kannan et al., 2019).

A machine translation system capable of translating spoken language to sign lan -guage and a computer animated avatar that signs are the other novel components of the Speech to Sign Language section of the project. Recent advances have been made in the development of speech to sign language avatar systems for real-time speech to  signing  avatar  systems  for  use  in  real  world  applications  (Kumar  et  al.,  2020; Waldow &amp; Fuhrmann, 2020). For the Sign Language to Speech section, existing (or novel) gesture recognition and machine translation approaches will be incorporated.



Fig. 1 An overview of process flow of the proposed closed-loop solution using, as an example, Jamai -can Sign Language (JSL) and Jamaican Standard English (JSE). The modularity of the solution will allow for any pair of languages to be used



Figure 1 indicates the use of Jamaican Sign Language (JSL) and Jamaican Standard English (JSE) as the target languages, however, the system is meant to be modular and any pair of languages can be used, depending on the domain in which the tool is to be deployed.

Sign language recognition is a maturing field, with a range of techniques being developed, from generative hidden Markov models, to the more recent deep learning gesture recognition techniques (Bragg et al., 2019 ; Pu et al., 2019 ; Bantupalli &amp; Xie 2019). Novel sign language translators are being explored that treat the recognition and translation of sign language as an end-to-end process, accomplished using deep learning approaches (Bantupalli &amp; Ying, 2018 ; Camgoz et al., 2019 ; Guo et al., 2019 ; Yutong, 2022). Modern end-to-end synthesis approaches make the development of realistic synthetic voices accessible, even for low-resource languages; these techniques will be employed to model speech (Fu et al., 2020 ; Zhang &amp; Lin, 2020 ; Mohammed &amp; Coy, 2021).

## A Deaf Perspective on the Solution

## Participants

In an effort to gauge the willingness of members to accept an AI-based closed-loop solution to the challenges of educating the Deaf in Jamaica, a focus group was held in March 2020. The session was conducted at the weekly Deaf Club meeting in Kingston, Jamaica with 14 of the leaders in the Deaf community (five females and nine males, all of whom were in the age range 21-60), one hearing individual - a graduate student, who is also a trained interpreter - and an interpreter selected by the members of the Deaf community. Six of the participants were tertiary educated - one was enrolled in university at the time of the meeting. The others were educated to the secondary level. None of the participants had an assistive device.

## Sampling

Convenience sampling was employed, as the focus group was held during the height of  the  COVID-19  pandemic.  Recruiting  for  the  focus  group  was  done  indirectly, through the Jamaican Association for the Deaf (JAD). Owing to the closed nature of the Deaf community in Jamaica, the JAD was approached to act as a go-between and to seek an invitation for the researcher to attend a meeting of the Deaf Club to present the idea of the closed-loop system to the attendees and collect their feedback. Thus, the sample consisted of the members of the club who attended the meeting on that night. Additional sessions were planned with other members of the club, but the country went into a series of restrictions,  including  lock  downs,  which  prevented meetings of the Deaf Club. Given the exploratory nature of the study, the small sample size was deemed to be justified, as there was no intention to generalise the result -ing responses to the wider Deaf community.

1. Did you use technology for education and was your experience with technology a positive one?
2. How would you feel about having a device in the classroom that would replace an interpreter?
3. Is there a general reluctance among the Deaf community to use technology in the classroom?
4. How would you feel about a software tool that allows for two-way communication between a hearing teacher and a deaf student in a mainstream classroom?

## Methodology

The session was facilitated by the first author with the assistance of the interpreter. The facilitator stood at the front of the room and spoke orally, while the interpreter signed the presentation of the facilitator to the Deaf participants. When the Deaf participants signed their feedback, questions and comments, the interpreter spoke orally, so that the facilitator could hear and respond. The facilitator's responses were then signed to the Deaf participants. Video and audio recordings of the session were made and transcriptions were produced after the session for future analysis. The session was divided into two parts; it began with engagement on the use of technology for education and the barriers to technology use in Deaf education (Coy, 2022). Next, the participants' views on the use of technology for Deaf education were elicited. As a part of this discussion, the facilitator introduced the notion of the closed-loop system described in Sect. 4 and then played a video of a signing avatar 2 for the participants, as an example of what a signing avatar could do. The format of the focus group session was to have an open discussion with the participants and to only intervene when clarification was requested. Guiding questions were used to stimulate discussion (see Table 1).

The resulting exchanges were analysed for themes and sentiment. ChatGPT-3.5 (OpenAI, 2023)  was  employed  for  the  thematic  analysis  -  the  results  of  which were verified by all three authors - while sentiment analysis was performed using a transformer-based sentiment analysis toolkit, the pysentimiento toolbox (Pérez et al., 2021). For the thematic analysis, a transcript of the session was generated. The authors worked independently to identify the key themes by reading through the transcript and extracting sections that followed a coherent theme. The separate analyses were shared and overlapping themes identified. Where themes could be merged, a decision was taken to do so. Additionally, the sections of the transcript that contained the  utterances  of  the  Deaf  participants  were  passed  to  ChatGPT3.5  and  a  prompt to generate a thematic analysis was given. The resulting themes aligned with those determined by the independent analyses of the authors.

## Results

## Thematic Analysis

Five main themes were extracted from the transcript. The themes were: avatar effec -tiveness  and  realism,  resource  and  cost  concerns,  language  and  cultural  considerations, real-time translation challenges and community involvement and feedback.

## Avatar Effectiveness and Realism

The  participants  had  two  major  concerns  regarding  the  potential  effectiveness and realism of the avatar. Firstly, they mentioned that it was not human-like in its appearance.

… and also the color of the complexion is just poor. And it seems like it' s a robot for real. It' s not human-like. I wasn't impressed of the [sic] complexion of the person when the person was signing. [Participant 3]

Secondly, the Deaf participants of the focus group had real concerns about the potential of the avatar to effectively represent their language. Here, the participants had two  primary  concerns,  the  multimodal  nature  of  signing  and  whether  the  avatar would be effective in situations where a Deaf relay interpreter would be required. The participants made the point that accurate signing involves facial expressions as well as gestures.

That's not effective. Let me explain why I say that. For example , prefix like prefix are some other words where you can add… UN.

Deaf relay interpreters (DRIs) are experienced Deaf interpreters that provide broker -ing services to other Deaf individuals (Adam et al., 2014). Their roles vary, but they often provide support for Deaf signers with mental challenges, home signs or vision challenges. In some instances, they work alongside hearing interpreters to assist the Deaf where unfamiliar concepts are being discussed, and where the sign being used by the hearing interpreter needs clarification. Given that the contexts within which signing avatars will be used (education, healthcare, transportation, etc.) may require clarification of some concepts, a system that employs an avatar for communication with the Deaf should consider how these clarifications will be provided.

… I have two things. It comes with interpreters and DRI. Deaf relay interpret -ers. For interpreter purposes , you have to sign naturally and sometimes persons don't understand. So that's why we need a deaf relay interpreter in between the hearing  interpreter  and  the  deaf  person.…  Possibly  a  deaf  individual  won't understand. For example , a deaf English word comes down to different words. A deaf person may understand that. For example , idiom.… How will the system incorporate those idioms , those prefix and so forth into the sentence , into Jamaican Sign Language? Because in English it is very easy. [Participant 4]



## Language and Cultural Considerations

The  participants  expressed  concerns  about  the  avatar's  ability  to  understand  and translate multiple languages and the importance of cultural and linguistic sensitivity. This concern arose from a discussion of the assumption that the teacher in the classroom would be using Jamaican Standard English for instruction.

So my concern , suppose the teacher is speaking patois with your program or whatever , you are able to understand clearly and translate it to JSL. [Partici -pant 4]

So will your program be able to understand the three languages , English , patois , and JSL? [Participant 5]

This thought led to the questioning of the motives for developing the avatar, as it related to Deaf culture. One participant asked if the avatar was being developed to replace interpreters - a common concern among the Deaf (European Union of the Deaf, 2023 ; World Federation of the Deaf, 2023, Kipp et al., 2011).

I'm just curious , what's the reason behind the development of that avatar? Do you have a problem with interpreters or what's the reason behind the develop -ment of the avatar? [Participant 12]

Another significant concern expressed, was that hearing parents of Deaf children may not find it necessary to learn sign language, if they think that a signing avatar can be used for real-time communication. This approach, they thought, would lead to hearing parents not interacting directly with their Deaf children.

And  for  young  children , I  think  this  system  can  be  a  bit  negative  for  them because their parents don't learn to use sign language to communicate with them one-on-one. [Participant 3]

## Resource and Cost Concerns

The participants wanted to know if the system would come at a cost to them. They recognised that there would be a cost to develop the system and to provide the classroom-based infrastructure  required  for  successful  deployment. Their  concern  was that the Deaf students, or their parents would have to pay a premium for access to the system. There was fear that, as they had experienced before, the Deaf community would be used as a way to get project funding, which was misappropriated and the community received no benefit from the project. Participants were keen to ensure that cost would not be a barrier to access for Deaf children.



… will that app be free?… , when you develop an idea for the deaf community , it' s  fantastic.  I  like  it. So , they go out , they start project and benefit from us , but we as deaf people don't benefit at all. So , they use the deaf community as a name to get funds for themselves. So… And that is not right. So , the deaf community , most times , they write to our views. I understand. So , before you finalise the development , make sure that the deaf club can have full access. [Participant 1]

A discussion ensued about the comparative benefit of spending on system development as opposed to interpreter training. ## Real-time Translation Challenges

The technical challenges were raised by the participants; they had concerns regarding real-time translation and its impact on classroom interactions and learning experiences. The proposed approach consists of a number of integrated sub-systems working together to facilitate classroom-based communication between Deaf students and hearing  teachers.  It  was  pointed  out  that  each  of  the  sub-systems  would  have  an associated time lag. When combined, they feared, the total system lag would hinder real-time participation of the Deaf student.

When the teacher is talking in… English , then translate[s] to Jamaican sign language… So it will be five minutes lagging , ten  minutes , one minute , 15  s because that would be a problem because when the teacher talks , the hearing person , they get it quick quick but for the deaf persons to benefit from what the teacher says. It depends on the waiting time for that , for the voice to be trans -lated to text and then to sign language , so what's the lag time? [Participant 3]



Participants pointed to other fora, such as plays, where interpreters were used. ## Sentiment Analysis

Sentiment  analysis  was  performed  using  the pysentimiento toolbox  (Pérez  et  al., 2021). The sentiment analysis tool is trained with the SemEval 2017 corpus (Rosen -thal et al., 2019 ) using a pretrained RoBERTa model, BERTweet, which is trained on 850 million English tweets (Nguyen et al., 2020). The text of the participants' statements  was  tokenised  into  units  of  topical  coherence,  to  ensure  that  part  of  a thought was not separated into unrelated chunks. These were then input to the sentiment analyser, which output one of three labels, 'POS', 'NEU' and 'NEG', indicat -ing whether the input was positive, neutral or negative in sentiment. Along with the label, a percentage certainty was output by the tool. If the percentage certainty fell below 80%, the response was manually examined and the label corrected, if it was erroneous.



Figure  2 shows  the  result  of  the  sentiment  analysis.  Almost  50%  (49.61)  of the  statements  were  neutral,  while  45.76%  and  4.6%  were  negative  and  positive, respectively.

## Discussion

## Thematic Analysis

The focus group results raised many issues related to the design of a closed-loop system for  inclusive  education  of  the  Deaf.  One  of  the  most  striking  is  the  issue of cultural responsiveness. The appearance of the avatar proved to be an important concern for the participants. This is a common concern for the Deaf when they are introduced to the use of avatars for signing (Quandt et al., 2022) - Kipp et al. (2011) found that the Deaf thought naturalness to be the second most important feature of an avatar. It has been shown that the Deaf showed a preference for natural-looking avatars over more cartoonish anime-style avatars (Brock et al., 2018). However, there seem to be limits to this preference. Adamo-Villani et al. ( 2015) found that a realistic, human-like avatar was less acceptable to users than a stylised one - as pointed out by Quandt et al. (2022 ) this preference may be due to the uncanny valley effect (Mori et al., 2012 ; Shin et al., 2019 ; Wolfe et al., 2021 ). Users can, therefore, be significantly impacted by the appearance of an avatar; if the impression is unfavourable, then the interest in the avatar will be low, even if the signing is intelligible (Adamo-Villani et al., 2016). This may even lead to an overall rejection of the avatar (Naert et al., 2020). This speaks to the need for cultural responsiveness in the design. The requirement that the avatar be representative of the population resonates with the issue of representation and potential bias in AI-based systems for the Deaf (Kaffle et al., 2019).

These concerns aren't simply cosmetic, they speak to the very heart of Deaf culture.  Many  Deaf  individuals  define  themselves  by  their  language  (Andersson  &amp; Adams Lyngbäck, 2022 ) thus any inappropriate representation of it is an affront to them. This is where UDL comes into play in the design process. As posited by Sil -vesti and Hartman (2022) a culturally responsive design of the proposed closed-loop system must respect the sociocultural backgrounds, identities (linguistic and cultural) and preferences of the Deaf student, if a desirable outcome is to be achieved.

The issue of Deaf Relay Interpreters, to the best of the authors' knowledge, is an area that has not been discussed in the literature. It is possible that this has been overlooked because the focus has been on generating an avatar to produce accurate, natural-looking signs and less on the deployment of that avatar in a real-world application. As the technology improves and more avatar-led communications systems are developed, attention should be given to how the role of the Deaf Relay Interpreter will be replicated, as stated in World Federation of the Deaf and the World Associa -tion of Sign Language Interpreters statement 'Translators and interpreters take time to prepare in order to fully understand a message and to give consideration to their target audience. An automated translation will not be able to take other sociolinguistic and sociocultural factors into account in order to ensure that the intent of the message sender, and the goals of the message receiver, are achieved.' (World Federation of the Deaf, 2023 , p. 1). This is where an AI-based system could be developed to model the appropriate sociolinguistic and sociocultural elements of deaf relay interpreting in order to enhance the utility of the proposed system for a wider range of Deaf learners.

When the  participants  pointed  out  the  need  for  supporting  bilingual  Jamaican speakers, it brought up the relevance of Vygotsky's concept of polyglossia in the system design in an unexpected way. The idea of support for multiple languages, in the context of inclusive Deaf education, is usually focused on providing language support for the Deaf who have access to both spoken and signed language. The fact that Deaf individuals are aware of the language needs of hearing teachers of the Deaf is a revelation. The Jamaican context is one in which most speakers are either monolingual speakers of the local English-based creole (referred to as Jamaican by linguists, but colloquially called Patois) or bilingual Jamaican/English speakers (Farquharson, 2013 ). While English is the official language of instruction, it has been found that most teachers selectively use Jamaican in the classroom (Nero, 2022). This raises the issue of the ASR system being able to effectively recognise what the teacher says - the hearing students would understand, but the avatar would not be able to sign it. This, the Deaf participants, linked to the culture of the teacher and the apparent effort to stifle the culture, by limiting the teacher to speaking one language over the other. This issue is distinctly a problem in the Global South, as creoles were developed in post-colonial societies, which is also where their use is still mostly seen as problematic (Krämer et al., 2022). Thus, the MPT aspect of the framework becomes relevant to both the hearing and Deaf users of the system - an aspect of the design that can be facilitated by the use of AI-based technological advancements in speech and language technologies.



Participants were in general agreement that it would be better for all teachers in mainstream classrooms to be taught sign language, so as to accommodate Deaf learners - this is echoed by some in the literature (Skyer, 2020). The rationale given was that there would then be enough teachers with the language skill to help the Deaf directly in the classroom and there would be no need for a signing avatar. The primary concern being expressed here, seems to be the fear of cultural marginalisation, which developers of signing avatars have to take seriously and address with the community (Wolfe et al., 2021). However, even the staunchest advocate for signing (even Deaf-only) teachers of the Deaf recognise that the concept is somewhat idealistic (Scott et al., 2023). While the reception to signing avatars may not be ideal, based on the view that signing humans, the logistics and cost implications (Gamal, 2022 ; De Meulder &amp; Haualand, 2021) suggest that the best option for inclusive Deaf education in the Global South is to have a system that allows for the use of signing avatars.

Despite the potential value of having a signing avatar, participants were sceptical. In particular, they felt that hearing parents of Deaf children may not find it necessary to learn sign language, if they think that a signing avatar can be used for real-time communication. This would be quote harmful, given the importance of early sign language learning to the overall development of the Deaf individual (Lieberman et al., 2024). This is a similar concern to that expressed about the rise in popularity of the cochlear implant (Wolfe et al., 2021). This cannot be ignored, but it should not be a barrier to technological innovation in inclusive education. One approach being taken, is to provide early interventions and support for hearing parents of Deaf children to learn sign language (Humphries et al., 2019 ; Terry &amp; Rance, 2023 ; Lieberman et al., 2024).

The  cost  implication  of  inclusive  education  is  significant  for  countries  in  the Global South (Coy, 2022). These concerns came out strongly in the focus group session. So much so, that past experiences of exploitation of the Deaf came up. It was indicated that, in the past, the Deaf community has been taken advantage of by individuals who got project funding and did not deliver, while engaging the deaf, raising their hopes, using them to gather information and ultimately, wasting their time. This non-tangible cost was seen as significant and weighed heavily on their minds. The cost to use the system was also a question that was raised - one participant suggested it might be better to spend the money that might be used for system development, on training new interpreters. The cost of assistive technology to facilitate inclusive education has always been a concern (Borg &amp; Östergren, 2015) especially  in  the Global South (Lynch et al., 2024). This is an important consideration for developers and policy makers who would have to ensure that any system developed using the proposed framework, is accessible, in cost terms, to the Deaf.

The focus group participants  were  sceptical  of  the  speed  and  efficiency  of  the proposed closed-loop system. While advances have been made in the various com- ponents of the proposed system (Kannan et al., 2019 ; Kumar et al., 2020 ; Waldow &amp; Fuhrmann, 2020 ; Wolfe et al., 2022) it is certain that much work will have to be done to ensure a seamless experience for all users.



Community involvement was highlighted as being important - the Deaf participants wanted to ensure that they were integrally involved in the development of the proposed system. Previous work aimed at the community did not include them in the design process, which led to a feeling of distrust of any solution claiming to be for the Deaf (Coy, 2022). This is not a new phenomenon, as other researchers have found the same limitation in systems aimed at inclusive education (Gersten and Edyburn, 2007 ; Stinson, 2010). The partnership model should see Deaf individuals brought into the process at the level of project conception, as their unique perspective can only make intended systems better. Wolfe et al., (2021) go even further, to encourage hearing researchers  to  get  involved  in  the  Deaf  community,  immersing  themselves  in  the language and culture, as much as possible, so that they gain perspective on the needs, concerns and expectations of the Deaf. Abstract: In this study, a complete fuzzy-based framework was developed for balanced allocation of resources in seamless schools in line with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 4). The study simplifies the uncertainty and complexity of educational data by combining Fuzzy Rule-Based Systems (FRBS), Fuzzy Cognitive Maps (FCM) and Fuzzy Multi-Criteria Decision-Making (MCDM) methods. The framework uses enrolment, access and quality as the three factors to prioritise schools for targeted interventions and allocation of better resources. The approach leverages the composition of expert-driven rules, causal modelling, and quantitative ranking to identify which schools need the most attention and allocate resources accordingly in real-time. The results from the case study showed that schools that continuously scored poorly in every criterion received the highest priority while schools performing moderately or better received an equitable allocation of resources. The results highlight the need for data-informed leadership in tackling education disparities into manifest, practical recommendations for policy makers. Future lines of research pointed out are the addition of other criteria, the prediction by means of machine learning and the broadening the framework into other contexts. This research adds a scalable, sustained model for equitable educational development around the globe.

## 1 Introduction

## 1.1 SDG 4 and Its Challenges

Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4) - 'ensure inclusive and equitable quality education and promote lifelong learning opportunities for all' - aims to achieve this by 2030 [1,2,3,4]. Education is a universal human right and also the foundation for sustainable development, shaping social, economic and cultural progress worldwide. Despite recent strides toward universal access to education around the globe, an equity challenge persists which threatens the breadth of sustainability in the achievement of SDG 4, because many children are still not receiving quality, inclusive, and accessible educational opportunities.

Global Differences in Learning Outcomes: Unequal access to education is the central challenge. Globally, 258 million children and youth remain out of school [5,6,7], with a large majority of them living in areas affected by conflicts or underprivileged places. Another area of immediate concern is gender equality; girls in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia face hurdles in their very right to education such as early marriages, poverty, and maintaining male-centered cultural traditions.



Quality of Education: Another challenge is assurance of quality education. Although enrollment rates have improved, UNESCO [8] finds that more than 617 million children and adolescents worldwide are unable to meet minimum proficiency levels in reading and mathematics, often due to poor infrastructure, insufficient teaching resources, inadequate implementation of school programs, and insufficiently trained teachers.

Digital Divide and Technological Challenges: The COVID-19 pandemic has exposed the digital divide and worsened inequity in education. According to UNICEF [5], two-thirds of school-age children around the world do not have internet access at homes, limiting their ability to participate in online learning. This information gap14 hampers technology inclusion in education, hindering progress toward SDG 4.

Learning for Life and Skills Gap: In the pursuit of equitable education, lifelong learning opportunities are important; however, ensuring adults and youth perform and obtain relevant skills in the job market continues to be a challenge. According to a 2022 report by the International Labour Organization (ILO) [9], there is a growing skills mismatch, where educational systems fall short of meeting labor market demands, especially within developing economies.

Financial and Policy Constraints: Financial constraints continue to be one of the major barriers, with UNESCO [1] reporting that $148 billion per year are needed to achieve SDG 4. Fragmented policies and lack of political will with poor data-driven decision-making further exacerbate the problem.

Marginalized Communities Inclusion: Marginalized groups like the disabled, refugees, and ethnic minorities experience further obstacles to accessing education. According to the World Bank [10], children with disabilities were more likely to be out of school than children without disabilities, at a rate 2.5 times higher, highlighting one of many areas where more inclusive education is needed.

## 4 Case Study

Title: Addressing Equitable Access to Education in Underserved Areas Using Fuzzy-Based Techniques.

## Background and Context

Promotion of quality education for all is a key Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 4), it highlights the importance of inclusivity and lifelong learning opportunities for all. But it is a goal that remains a steep challenge in underserved areas, where gaps in access, infrastructure and education quality still exist. Transportation challenges, insufficient teacher-student ratios and low enrolment rates further widen the educational gap, with vulnerable communities at a disadvantage.

Traditional paradigms for tackling these problems tend to be formalized and deterministic models which are unable to account for the ambiguity and uncertainty present in educational data. For instance:

- -Accessibility metrics can vary based on local conditions such as seasonal road blockages or public transport availability.
- -Quality of education, assessed by teacher qualifications and school infrastructure, is subjective and varies between regions.
- -Enrolment rates are influenced by socio-economic factors that are difficult to quantify precisely.

## Objective of the Case Study

This case study showcases the different strategies that can be utilized to address such challenges of fuzzy mathematics and AI based techniques:

- -Fuzzifying fuzzy metrics (example: accessibility, quality, enrollment), which deal with uncertainty
- -Vests the causal relationships among these critical factors over time with Fuzzy Cognitive Maps (FCM) to simulate themselves.
- -Fuzzy MCDM for intervention prioritization and resource allocation.
- -Commenting down to FRBS in dynamic decision making.

## Study Area and Data

The case study investigates five schools located in a rural area with a persistent unequal distribution of resources. Three critical factors that directly affect education outcomes are data collected on

- -Accessibility: Measured on a scale of 0 to 100, indicating the ease of transportation and connectivity to schools.
- -Quality: Rated on a scale of 0 to 100, according to student-teacher ratios, school infrastructure and resources available.
- -Enrollment: Measured on a scale of 0 to 100, reflecting the percentage of school-aged children currently enrolled.

- -Rule 4: IF Accessibility is Low AND Quality is High, THEN Priority is Medium.
- -Rule 5: IF Accessibility is Medium AND Enrolment is High, THEN Priority is Medium.

## 5 Results and Discussion

## 5.1 Insights from the Fuzzy Models

In this study, the developed fuzzy-based approach provided valuable insights into the challenges of equitable allocation of resources for underserved schools. The facet of managing complexity and uncertainty in real-life educational data was well handled when approaching them through fuzzy models. The Fuzzy Rule-Based System (FRBS) showed the capacity to differentiate subtle changes in terms of accessibility, quality, and enrolment. By adopting expert-driven fuzzy rules, it enabled decision-making in real-time and identification of schools in urgent need of interventions. For example, according to the provided metrics, schools performing poorly across all categories were universally prioritized while schools that were balanced were treated evenly.

The Fuzzy Cognitive Map (FCM) emphasized the causal relationships between pairs of criteria and also the dependencies among them. For example, the availability of actual accessibility improvements was found to have a benefit cascading through to enrolment and quality. By applying a core set of rubrics iteratively with several stakeholders, we were able to identify the relationship in-between condition outcome metrics, ultimately uncovering the systemic connections between process and outcome metrics. This was crucial in properly prioritizing resources for the schools since the steady-state values derived for each criterion had to relate to realistic conditions for the schools.

Fuzzy MCDM methods, especially Fuzzy TOPSIS, helped in the process of ranking these schools where based on the closeness to the ideal solutions refined the prioritization process. This approach successfully simulated the dilution of competing criteria and built a calculated closeness coefficient for every school. The results were consistent with the nuanced findings of the other models, underscoring the robustness of the combined fuzzy approach.

## 5.2 Policy Implications

This study has important implications for policy making along the framework of Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4), which advocates quality education for all. Herein, we provide a fuzzy-clustering-based framework which serves as a scalable and flexible decision-making tool for policymakers. This not only allows the allocation of resources to be based on data but also helps in eliminating the ambiguity and subjectivity that is usually present when it comes to Education data.

One of the core take home messages: not all resources are created equal, distribute and prioritize based on evidence, not uniform distribution. In contrast, note how all schools identified as School D across all four criteria (excluding School C, the outlier, which exhibits a more mixed performance across all categories) receive the highest share of resources demonstrating the need for targeted and systemic school improvement in these schools. Thus helping interventions to target the areas it is needed the most, maximizing their effect. Finally, setting minimum thresholds for resource allocation, as illustrated in the threshold-based strategy, ensure that no school left behind, consistent with the equity principles of the SDG 4.

The other important implication is that the fuzzy framework can be adapted to other contexts and/or more localized areas. The models are flexible and can be adjusted to reflect local priorities, such as weighting enrolment in populous areas more heavily than in rural areas with fewer infrastructure options or vice versa when weighting quality. This ensures that the framework is equipped to provide guidance to policymakers across a range of educational contexts.

In conclusion, causal analysis is introduced via FCM that highlights the importance of holistic policy-making. Policymakers are able to design interventions that address root causes rather than symptoms. For example, improving access to education through better transportation or digital connectivity may increase enrolment and quality indirectly, creating a multiplier effect.

This fuzzy-based methodology not only satisfies the immediate goal of resource allocation, but also offers a practical and future-oriented route to sustainable educational development. It provides policymakers with actionable insights, promotes equitable practices of accountability, and supports the achievement of the overall targets of SDG 4.

## 6 Conclusion

## 6.1 Summary of Findings

In this study we examined a fuzzy-based approach to equitable resource allocation among underserved schools, relevant with the objectives of Sustainable Development Goal 4 (SDG 4). Combining FRBS, FCM, and Fuzzy MCDM offered an effective means to deal with the intrinsic complications and uncertainties present in educational information. Using this framework, the standing of every school with respect to Accessibility, Quality, and Enrolment was computed for resource allocation.

Schools that performed poorly on all criteria, like School D, were prioritized for resource allocation. Fuzzy TOPSIS provided closeness coefficients for each school, facilitating an objective ranking that resulted in efficient and fair resource allocation. The weighted resource allocation strategy, for example, showed that needs based on criteria could be addressed, where accessibility improvements could be prioritized in places that were particularly poorly connected. The integrated use of FCM pointed out the interconnections between criteria, indicating that specific actions could cascade to create positive changes throughout the STIs.

The fuzzy-based approach was therefore capable of taking into account various factors in the resource allocation process which made it flexible and scalable according to the education standards. This helped not only to make time-dynamic decisions, but it also applied subjective insight of experts and eliminated the possibility that a school id left out, thanks to the implementation of minimum thresholds for allocation algorithms.

## 6.2 Future Research Directions

Adding Additional Criteria: Future studies may extend upon these results to develop an enhanced ranking based on even more criteria. Aspects like socioeconomic status, cultural dynamics, infrastructure development, and community engagement might give a deeper understanding of the phenomenon, even though the present study created awareness towards accessibility, quality, and enrolment. For example, schools serving economically disadvantaged neighbourhoods might need extra weight for these underlying inequities. Likewise, integrating local cultural contexts may help to ensure resource allocation reflects the unique challenges that diverse communities face. More inclusive and context-sensitive fuzzy models would allow the set of used criteria to be broadened, which would improve their effectiveness and effectiveness.

Fuzzy Logic and Machine Learning Integration: One potential pathway is through the combination of machine learning methods with fuzzy models to improve team-oriented approaches. For instance, in many cases, machine learning algorithms have become highly effective in discovering patterns, predicting trends and providing insights from massive datasets, and fuzzy systems can manage the uncertainties and approximations in the data. As an example, predictive models may predict future enrolment rates, or identify accessibility bottlenecks that would be incorporated into the decision-making process of all fuzzy models. Such a hybrid model may yield more flexible and reliable frameworks for the optimal allocation of resources to increasingly complex and dynamic education problems.

Application in Diverse Contexts: The fuzzy-based framework suggested in this study is generic and can be adapted and validated through different contexts, for example in urban versus rural schools or in areas with contrasting educational policies and cultural dynamics. By comparing multiple countries or regions, the studies can shed light on the generalisability and adaptability of the framework. For example, resource allocation priorities might vary between densely populated urban regions and remote rural territories. Adapting the fuzzy models to these variety of contexts would allow them to remain relevant and effective, making them a tool that can be utilized for global educational equity.

Longitudinal Impact Studies: Another major direction should be longitudinal studies to try to capture the long-term consequences of the resource allocation decisions obtained from fuzzy models. Long-term tracking of outcomes would enable researchers to assess what works in helping children thrive, whether through improved student performance or increased enrolment or quality of teaching. Such feedback loop could enable fine-tuning of the auto-modelling thus preventing them from drifting apart with their goal. Longitudinal data could also provide policy makers with evidence that would help them understand the effectiveness of their resource allocation strategies over time.



Incorporating Real-Time Data: Future research can also consider real-time data with fuzzy models. Technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT) and analytics are leading the way towards more real time monitoring capabilities within our schools. IoT-enabled systems, for instance, could monitor classroom utilization, resources usage, or even student attendance. So obtaining such data would serve as inputs to fuzzy models by which we can create dynamic/rubber-stamp models which would optimize resources by making it demand-based rather than a one-size fits all approach. This would allow the framework to respond more dynamically in the event of major shocks or crises like a natural disaster or a pandemic [34].

Interdisciplinary Collaborations: There are many areas such as education, mathematics, computer science, sociology, and public policy which could have synergy in this framework as fuzzy-based educational frameworks. Bringing together multidisciplinary teams with various knowledge sets would help enhance the models and guarantee their relevance for real challenges. For example, educators could offer perspectives on the reality of schools' logistical constraints, while sociologists could draw attention to the societal consequences of resource allocation. Thus, further research using this fuzzy modelling framework should be further enhanced by collaboration to make the fuzzy models more usable to have a greater impact and gain wider acceptance.

These future directions indicate the possibilities to extend and improve this fuzzy-based framework discussed in this study. At the same time, by tackling these challenges researchers can help make equitable, efficient and sustainable practices of allocating educational resources a worldwide phenomenon.

## RELATED RESEARCH REVIEW

## Problems in the Allocation of Rural Education Resources

With the implementation of the rural revitalization strategy, the challenge of how to optimize the allocation of rural education resources has become a hot topic of academic concern. In rural areas, the shortage and uneven distribution of educational resources have become increasingly prominent, especially in remote areas, where the lack of educational resources has led to low education quality. Studies have shown that the problem of allocation of rural education resources is reflected not only in insufficient capital investment but also in the uneven distribution of educational resources in different regions, with the gap between urban and rural areas being particularly significant. In particular, in some western and mountainous areas (Zhang et al., 2023), the construction of educational facilities lags behind, and high-quality educational resources are difficult to penetrate into the grassroots, resulting in an imbalance in urban and rural education development.

Some studies have pointed out that although the national and local governments have achieved certain results in increasing investment in education funds, owing to the low level of informatization, educational resources are still unevenly distributed. To improve this situation, many scholars have proposed solutions to optimize the allocation of educational resources through digitization and informatization. By using technologies such as big data and cloud computing, educational management departments can more accurately grasp the educational resource needs of each region, thereby formulating a more scientific resource allocation strategy. These solutions provide a new perspective for solving the problem of educational resource allocation, and they also highlight the huge potential of modern technology in the allocation of educational resources (Wang et al., 2023).

The introduction of digital education has brought revolutionary changes to the allocation of educational resources, especially in the rational allocation of rural educational resources. Researchers believe that with the help of information technology, especially big data analysis, resource gaps can be effectively identified and filled. This approach not only improves the accuracy of resource allocation but also reduces the waste of resources caused by information asymmetry, helping to solve the problems that have long plagued rural education (Xue et al., 2021).

## Application of Deep Learning in Educational Resource Optimization

In recent years, deep learning, as an important branch of artificial intelligence, has been widely used in  many fields,  and  its  potential  in  optimizing  educational  resources  has  gradually  been tapped. Deep learning can help identify problems in the allocation of educational resources through deep mining and analysis of massive data, thereby providing strong data support for educational decision-making. Many studies have shown that the application of deep learning technology in the allocation of educational resources, especially in demand forecasting and resource allocation optimization, has significant advantages. In some studies, deep learning is used to process and analyze large amounts of educational data to help educational managers better understand resource use and demand changes. By establishing a deep learning model, educational management departments can accurately predict the future demand for educational resources in a certain area and then allocate resources in advance (Wang et al., 2024). This process not only improves the efficiency of resource allocation but also avoids resource waste and shortages, ensuring that educational resources are used reasonably. At the same time, deep learning is also widely used in education quality assessment. In rural areas, because of the weak educational infrastructure and differences in teaching quality, deep learning is used to analyze students' learning behavior, classroom performance, and changes in grades. By analyzing students' learning under different teaching modes, deep learning can provide teachers with personalized teaching strategies and help optimize teaching methods. A further application is to use deep learning technology to track the quality of education in real time during the education evaluation process, promptly identify teaching problems, and make adjustments, thereby improving the quality of education (Xiao et al., 2022).

The advantage of deep learning is also reflected in its powerful self-learning ability. Based on a large amount of teaching data, the deep learning model can be continuously optimized and adjusted, gradually improving the prediction accuracy of resource allocation and educational effects. The self-iteration and optimization characteristics of this technology enable it to make more flexible and accurate decisions on resource allocation, providing an efficient and intelligent tool for educational decision-makers (Guo et al., 2020).

## Challenges and Solutions in the Allocation of Rural Education Resources

Although modern technology, especially deep learning, has shown its potential in optimizing the allocation of educational resources in some studies, in practical applications, the allocation of rural educational resources still faces many challenges. One of the most prominent challenges is the lack of data and the low quality of data. In some remote areas, owing to weak technical infrastructure, collecting and processing educational data are still difficult. As a result, the allocation of educational resources in many places still relies on traditional manual methods and cannot achieve precise resource allocation. Another major challenge is the limitation of technical infrastructure. Even though advanced technologies such as deep learning can theoretically solve the problems in the allocation of educational resources, in many rural areas, information construction is still lagging behind. The lack of high-speed Internet and smart terminal devices makes it difficult to popularize the application of educational technology. For some areas with weak infrastructure, the challenge of how to improve the popularity of educational technology and provide the necessary hardware support for the application of technology is still an urgent problem to be solved (Ohta et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024).

In response to these challenges, researchers have proposed a number of solutions. On one hand, through policy guidance, local governments can be encouraged to increase investment in rural education informatization, improve the coverage of Internet infrastructure, and gradually establish a complete education data platform. On the other hand, edge computing and other technologies can be used to process and analyze data in areas with limited resources, reduce dependence on efficient Internet, and provide support for deep learning applications in remote areas. In addition, strengthening data sharing and cooperation is also an effective solution. Integrating education data from different regions and departments enables a national education data sharing platform to be established, thereby improving the transparency and fairness of resource allocation (Lu et al., 2022; Liu, 2024).

With the development of technology, the application prospects of deep learning in the allocation of educational resources are broad. Although it faces certain challenges, with the support of policies, the improvement of infrastructure and the advancement of technology, the application of deep learning in the allocation of rural educational resources will gradually take root and become an important tool for optimizing educational resources and improving teaching quality (Wang, 2022).

## Neural Network Structure Design

## EXPERIMENTAL EVALUATION

In  this  section  I  evaluate  the  effectiveness  of  the  proposed  deep  learning-based  educational resource  optimization  model  through  experiments.  The  experimental  design  focused  on  the performance of the model in different educational scenarios to verify its advantages in resource allocation, educational equity, and teaching quality improvement. The main task of the experiment was to compare the differences between the optimization algorithm based on deep learning and traditional optimization methods (e.g., linear programming and genetic algorithms) to evaluate the effectiveness and advantages of the new model.

## Experimental Design

The experimental data set included multidimensional data, such as educational resources, student needs, and educational quality, in various regions to ensure that the actual needs of different regions can be fully reflected. I divided the data into training sets, validation sets, and test sets, and I ensured the quality and consistency of the data through standardization. During the experiment, I trained the model with the training set and selected the best model structure and parameter combination through cross-validation and adjustment of hyperparameters.

The evaluation indicators covered resource allocation accuracy, educational fairness, training time,  computing efficiency,  and  improvement  of  teaching  quality.  Through  these  indicators,  I comprehensively evaluated the performance of the model in practical applications, especially its stability and efficiency when processing large-scale data sets. In addition, I compared it with traditional optimization methods to analyze the advantages and limitations of deep learning models in resource optimization.

During the performance evaluation phase, I comprehensively evaluated the model based on the following evaluation indicators: resource allocation accuracy, educational equity, and teaching quality improvement.

For resource allocation accuracy, I measured the accuracy of the model in allocating resources to various regions and schools. By calculating the error in resource allocation, I evaluated the deviation between the resource allocation predicted by the model and the actual demand.

For educational equity, I calculated the balance of resource allocation between regions and schools to evaluate the performance of the model in improving educational equity. I used indicators such as standard deviation or Gini coefficient to measure the fairness of resource allocation.

For teaching quality improvement, I evaluated the improvement of students' academic performance and teaching quality after resource optimization. I verified the effectiveness of the model by changes in academic performance and improvements in teaching outcomes.

## Discussion

My multidimensional comparative experiments on the education resource optimization model based on deep learning, linear programming, and genetic algorithms, yielded results that show that the deep learning model has significant advantages. In terms of resource allocation accuracy-whether it  is  the number of teachers, teaching facilities, book resources, or education funding-the mean error of the deep learning model is much lower than that of traditional methods, and therefore, it can accurately match the actual needs of various regions.

From the perspective of educational equity, in the comparison of the standard deviation of resource allocation in the school dimension, the deep learning model has a lower mean, making the resource allocation among schools more balanced and effectively reducing the differences in education quality. In terms of improving teaching quality, the deep learning model is significantly ahead of the linear programming method in terms of improving the average scores of Chinese, mathematics, and English; improving the excellent rate and pass rate; improving the teaching results rate; and improving student satisfaction. This series of experimental results fully verifies the effectiveness and superiority of the deep learning model in the optimal allocation of educational resources. In practical applications, the model can help the education department to reasonably allocate resources, promote educational equity, and improve the overall quality of education. Future research can further explore the in-depth application of the model in different educational scenarios, optimize the model parameters, and improve its generalization ability to better serve the development of education.

## CONCLUSION

In this paper I examined the problem of optimizing the allocation of rural education resources by deep learning under the background of rural revitalization. By constructing an optimization model for the allocation of education resources based on deep learning, I comprehensively analyzed the current status of rural education resources and proposed and verified an effective optimization scheme. In the data collection and analysis stage, I collected, preprocessed, and analyzed data on education resources, student needs and education quality, providing a solid data foundation for subsequent modeling. The established mathematical optimization model abstracts the problem of education resource allocation by reasonably setting the objective function and constraints, and it also uses a multi-objective optimization method to comprehensively consider multiple objectives such as resource utilization efficiency and education equity. The optimization algorithm design based on deep learning adopts a multilayer perceptron neural network structure, combined with the mean square error loss function and the Adam optimization algorithm. Through training and evaluation, the performance and generalization ability of the model are effectively improved. The experimental results show that compared with traditional linear programming and genetic algorithms, the model based on deep learning has obvious advantages in resource allocation accuracy and that it can more accurately meet the actual needs of various regions for teachers, teaching facilities, book resources, and education funds. In terms of education equity, the deep learning model makes the resource allocation among schools more balanced and significantly reduces the differences in education quality. In terms of improving teaching quality, the model is significantly ahead in multiple teaching quality evaluation indicators, which has effectively promoted the improvement of education quality. Future research can further expand the application of the model in different educational scenarios, continuously optimize model parameters, and improve its generalization ability to better serve rural education and provide stronger talent support and intellectual guarantee for rural revitalization.
