## AsiRoµ : A Multi-purpose Robotic Assistant for Educational Inclusion of Children with Multiple Disabilities

Jonnathan Andrade-Altamirano 1 , Ana Parra-Astudillo 1 , Vladimir Robles-Bykbaev 1( B ) , Nidia Almeida-Solíz 1,2 , Sofía Bravo-Buri 1 , and Efren Lema-Condo 1

Abstract. Nowadays, artificial intelligence is a tool widely used in several areas such as medicine, personal assistance, regular/special education, leisure, among many others. Additive manufacturing development allows designing and making innovative low-cost robotic assistants for educational inclusion processes in developing countries. There are not enough assistive technologies for special education centers in Ecuador due to the lack of resources. For these reasons, in this paper, we describe the robot AsiRoµ (multi-purpose robotic assistant, for its Spanish name). AsiRoµ is based on the open-source 3D printed robot Inmmov. It implements the following functionalities: hand gestures recognition, automatic speech recognition and text to speech function through the IBM Watson Cloud services, and gesture imitation. The robot aims to conduct a dialogue with the children, motivate them to carry out the exercises/rehabilitation activities, and motivate and engage them in the therapy sessions (for children with disabilities). To determine the children's acceptance level, we carried out a pilot experiment with two groups of children. The first group consisted of 7 children with multiple disabilities (intellectual disability, Joubert syndrome, Down Syndrome, Autism, etc.), whereas in the second participated 30 children aged between 4 and 6 years (without disabilities). Each child interacted with the robot, and a group of experts evaluated the children's perceptions.

Keywords: Robotic assistant · Equalization · Microcontroller · processing · Segmentation · Computer Vision

## 1 Introduction

Talking about education is exciting, describing learning theories, sharing experiences on didactic processes or methodological strategies applied in the classroom, enriches the teaching work, even more so when there is commitment, vocation and love for teaching. However, the least addressed topic in these meetings is the applicability of such strategies

with students who have Special Educational Needs (SEN); educational inclusion still generates some fear, perhaps due to the lack of training received as teachers, the lack of resources or strategies to be used or simply because of the social prejudices that exist.

Knowing each student allows proposing plans that contain strategies that promote equal opportunities based on their individual needs and interests, improving the classroom environment, providing an active role to each student in order to optimize their learning and achieve true educational inclusion.

The Ministry of Education, in the Pedagogical Strategies Work Guide for Special Educational Needs, proposes intervention mechanisms based on diversified strategies such as the setting and adaptation of the classroom, maintaining channels of communication with families and therapists.

The methodology that rules the teaching-learning process at the levels of Initial Education and High School is the game, which is a strategy that allows the integration of students with and without SEN associated or not with disabilities. At these levels of education, inclusion processes are carried out in a very natural way, since in early childhood, children accept their peers without prejudice and their characteristic of solidarity favors the support in the acquisition of skills according to their abilities. When continuing in the Basic Education levels, these processes are broken and the methodologies used vary according to the strategies that each teacher considers optimal for the development of their classes [1].

It is at these levels where the continuity of inclusive strategies should be proposed, through collaborative work that facilitates the management of various levels within the classroom, each student has an active role: investigating and experimenting with the guidance of their teacher who facilitates and raises situations or topics that help them to discover and learn in an active way.

Therefore, it is important to highlight the importance of continuous training in inclusion and new methodological strategies that allow the development of meaningful learning.

## 2 Related Work

This section the importance of the use of robotic assistants and inclusive software developed to support children of different ages and with different needs.

It has been shown that the work of children with Down Syndrome with humanoid robots is very important for their educational development and social skills, is what Lehmann et al. proved, by conducting an experiment with two robots, the first robot has a humanoid form (Kaspar) and has the size of a child, while the second has the shape of a small car, the experiment was conducted for three months with a girl with Down Syndrome; one of the activities that was performed with the girl was the ability to mimic a complete sequence of movements. After 3 months it was concluded that the girl preferred to work with the humanoid-shaped robot [2].

As autism is a neurological disorder that manifests itself in the first years of life and alters the normal functioning of the brain, it causes some inconveniences in children such as not being able to interact and communicate easily with the environment around them, pronouncing unintelligible words in a low voice, responding incorrectly to any question and not presenting fluency in their dialogue, these drawbacks were obtained in the research conducted by [3], developed for three months with a large population of children with autism between 4 to 11 years old.

With the results obtained from this research, a computer game has been developed, which is based on the use of pictograms that appear randomly on the screen, the children must pronounce the name of each object and then form sentences, which has caused children with autism to be able to have a fluent dialogue [3].

Likewise, an interdisciplinary team developed ' An intelligent system based on ontologies and ICT tools to support the diagnosis and intervention of children with autism ', designed to improve the social skills of people with autism, which adapts to the needs and abilities of each user in order to provide a personalized therapy. For this research a robotic assistant was built in the shape of a stuffed animal and was evaluated with 30 children who expressed their agreement with its appearance, movements and functionalities [4].

## 3 Proposed Method

## 3.1 Robotic Assistant

There is a need to develop a low-cost robotic assistant to interact with children with and without disabilities, capable of perform voice recognition and sign recognition using artificial intelligence techniques and with a friendly design.

Figure 1 shows the architecture diagram of the robotic assistant proposed in this article. Therapists, teachers, and psychologists are represented at the top of the figure, who will be in charge of working with the robotic assistant in their classrooms and in their different work sessions.

The end users and the visual, auditory, motor and intellectual stimuli that the tool will allow to develop with the use of the proposed modules are shown below.

The implementation of the robotic assistant was based on the INMOOV animatronic model, its website presents open access resources (http://inmoov.fr/), further, an additive manufacturing through 3D printing was used for the construction of the head and all its internal parts, whose links will move thanks to the actuators that form the head.

The robotic assistant has four degrees of freedom distributed in the eyes, jaw and trachea, for which the tool is composed of the electronic components shown in Fig. 2, the motion control of the actuators is performed with the Arduino MEGA 2560 microcontroller together with a 16-channel, 12-bit PWM controller PCA9685.

The actuators used for the movement of the joints are detailed below. Two DS929V micro-Servos are used for the movement of the eye mechanisms, a Metal Gear MG995 Servo is used for the movement of the jaw mechanism, and a Mega Giant HS-805BB Servo is used for the movement of the head mechanism.

## 3.2 Gesture Recognition System

Displayed One of the main points for the robotic assistant to perform interaction is the artificial vision system, which consists of two parts: object detection and object identification. In order to perform the gesture detection process, first, it is necessary to work on the lighting of the scene, just as with the human eye, it is possible to have a better projection of the area that is being observed according to the amount of light that is available at that moment.

The histogram equalization technique has been used to improve the illumination, which alters each pixel of the image, returning a new image with a better contrast, to move on to a second point such as segmentation, which consists of taking a scene in the RGB color space (called like that because of the composition of its three channels corresponding to red, green and blue) and creating a binary image where the object is separated from the rest of the scene based on its color intensity, being white the space occupied by the object and black the color of the rest of the scene, it is possible that after performing a segmentation there may be pixels left over that can be considered as noise, which can be eliminated by applying blur and threshold filters.

This process allows the detection of objects based on their color, in this case the pixels that represent the skin are taken. The second part is the application of object identification techniques, in this case the Sklearn MLPClassifier python library (Multi-layer Perceptron Classifier) has been used, algorithm that relies on an underlying neural network to perform classification tasks, which optimizes the loss function using stochastic gradient descent [5].

It has been proposed to differentiate a gesture to initialize the interaction with the robotic assistant, another gesture to identify a greeting, a gesture to move to the next question and a gesture to try again, 500 images for each gesture of its binary form (black and white) have been sampled to create the classifier, the samples have been taken from both the right and the left hand. The Hu invariant moments have been extracted from each sample, a technique first proposed by Ming-Kuei Hu in [6], which can recognize objects no matter how different their size, position and scale are, minimizing the loss of information, also considered as the weighted average of the pixels of an image.

For the construction of the network, the 7 invariant moments of Hu are used in the input layer, which represent each image with a gesture, in the binary color space, moreover, an element for the processing of the output layer is used, which indicates the number by which the gesture will be represented, as shown in Fig. 3. Although it is intended to identify only 4 gestures, the network has been trained with 7 different gestures, to avoid false positives with gestures that may appear similar in different positions or distances of the hand with respect to the camera, once the network is trained, it returns an accuracy of 0. 92% of effectiveness.

## 3.3 Systems Integration

The integration with the robotic assistant consists in that once the child puts his hand in front of the robot, the robot recognizes the gesture and asks a question, together with the jaw gesticulation to simulate the movement of speech, then he has a between 10 to 20 s to make a short recording depending on the case in which the child can answer, the audio file is captured and through the IBM Watson Speech To Text API the audio is processed to convert it into text, this enters an algorithm where keywords are searched to validate that the answer is correct, if the answer is correct the robotic assistant will play an audio saying 'correct, now you can perform a gesture to move to the next question', at that moment the gesture that corresponds to the next question must be done, and the robotic assistant will ask another question, in case the answers are considered incorrect the assistant will reproduce the following sentence 'incorrect, could you make the gesture to try again', at that moment the child must make the gesture that corresponds to try again (Fig. 4).

## 4 Pilot Experiment and Preliminary Results

In order to determine the real feasibility of using the robotic assistant as an educational support tool, an experimentation plan was carried out with a group of 30 children without disabilities and 6 children with multiple disabilities. The first group of 30 children are students from the 'Eugenio Espejo' Educational Unit, between the ages of 4 and 6 years (mean = 4.6, and SD = 0.844). A total of 18 boys and 12 girls participated in the experiment.

An interaction process was carried out to measure the children's perception of the robotic assistant AsiRoµ , by using the assistant's gesture recognition and automatic speech recognition system. Then, they were asked to fill out a survey consisting of 13 questions related to the appearance and functionalities of the robot, as well as the possibility of having the assistant at home, and its fluency in speech and movements (mouth). Also, in order to protect the children's privacy, only 2 demographic questions (age and gender) were considered.

Each survey question was designed on a Likert scale where 1 represents 'totally disagree' and 5 represents 'totally agree'. The survey was validated with the internal consistency model, Cronbach's alpha, obtaining a result of 0.86. Thus, it can be affirmed that the reliability used to measure the perception of the 30 children is appropriate.

As can be seen in Fig. 5 (top plot), 60% of the children consider that they 'strongly agree' and 'agree' that the size and shape of the robot are appropriate. Along the same line, 76% and 83% of the children liked the colors of the robot and the base that holds the robot's head, respectively. It is important to mention that the speed of the robot's mouth movements should be improved, since 50% of the children consider them as 'totally adequate' and 'adequate'. As for the robot's voice, 83% considered it as 'very pleasant' and 'pleasant'.

On the other hand, the lower plot of Fig. 5 shows the children's perception of the robot's functionalities. 97% of the children consider the activities provided by the robot as 'very interesting' and 'interesting', while 70% think that they would 'totally agree' and 'agree' to have the robot at home. One aspect that stands out is that children prefer to talk to the robot (73%) compared to interacting through hand gestures (50%).

On the other hand, we worked (virtually) with 7 children (2 girls and 5 boys) with disabilities (4 with Down Syndrome, 1 with intellectual disability and autism, 1 with deep bilateral anacusis, and 1 with chronic encephalopathy). ## 5 Conclusions

Through the analysis conducted from the application of the surveys, it is concluded that the robotic assistant AsiRoµ produced a great impact on children between 4 to 6 years old with and without disabilities, which can be verified with the value of Cronbach's coefficient, which is 0.86, showing that the survey reflects consistency between the items.

Likewise, the perception of the children with respect to the size, color, voice and shape of the robotic assistant has a positive acceptance, since it is in scales of 'Totally agree' and 'Strongly agree', 'Very pleasant' and 'Pleasant', in addition the results indicate that the children prefer to interact with the assistant verbally than using manual signs, as indicated in the section of the experimentation plan and preliminary results.

In the case of children with intellectual disabilities, it has been concluded that due to their difficulties in the motor area, it is difficult for them to imitate the requested gestures, they tried, but most of them required physical support from the person who accompanied them. In terms of listening to the assistant, it was easier for them to understand the question, although they were given verbal support by repeating the question. It is worth mentioning that the questions were repeated a second time and this time the children understood the instructions a little better, and even tried to make the gestures they liked or found easier on their own. This experience is encouraging because it opens the door to future improvements by adapting gestures and providing the opportunity to work with a robotic assistant during therapies.

Due to these results, it can be concluded that an interactive tool has been created, which will favor children in the first educational stage with or without SEN, in line with the purpose of the GIIATa Research Group and the UNESCO Chair on Support Technologies for Educational Inclusion of the Politecnica Salesiana University, Campus Cuenca.

Abstract -Inclusion in education is critical to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 4, which ensures equitable education for all. This study explores the application of decision tree algorithms to improve adaptive education, promoting inclusion and diversity in higher education. Decision trees were used to classify students into predefined categories based on various attributes such as gender, age, learning styles, and specific needs (for example, ADHD). The algorithm was trained on an experimental dataset and demonstrated high accuracy (95.94%) in classification. By dynamically adjusting educational content and accessibility options, this approach aims to provide personalized learning experiences tailored to individual needs. The findings highlight the potential of AI-driven adaptive learning systems to improve educational outcomes and foster an inclusive environment by accommodating various learning preferences and requirements. This research contributes to a greater understanding of how technology can be used to address educational inequities and suggests future directions to improve adaptive learning frameworks.

Index Terms -adaptive education, Smart Learning Platforms, educational innovation, higher education, decision trees, inclusion and diversity.

## I. INTRODUCTION

Inclusion in the context of education promotes the achievement of Sustainable Development Goal 4, 'Quality of Education', which ensures inclusive and equitable quality education and promotes opportunities for lifelong learning for all [1]. From this stance, every effort to grant equitable access to education will allow learners to reach their full potential and develop opportunities to improve their livelihoods regardless of their physical, mental, socioeconomic, cultural, or linguistic abilities or contexts [2]. Inclusion also broadens the possibilities of understanding diversity as a powerful source of innovation, in which intersectionality, the combination of needs of traditionally marginalized or neglected populations [3], represents a myriad of opportunities to create different and more effective paths. Especially in the context of digital learning or e-learning, technological development opens a substantial number of possibilities regarding inclusion and accessibility. Inclusive education is not only about assisting people in need. It is about creating environments that promote and perform through the adaptability and adequacy of different types of responses according to what learners need. For example, adaptive evaluation [4] allows learners to achieve intermediate goals at their own pace while building the capacities and abilities needed to reach the main learning outcomes. Another good example of inclusive learning is the use of recommendation systems on e-learning platforms, which allow learners to follow personalized paths tailored to their interests and abilities [5]. Furthermore, Bardach et al. [6] note that automated feedback provides instant information on learners' performance, enhancing self-efficacy and classroom readiness, pointing out the relevant role of feedback and reflection during the learning process. All of these approaches are significantly improved when artificial intelligence (AI) is applied to determine the type of response, the adaptability paths, and, even more, the paths a learner should follow during a formative experience. Applying AI to adaptive e-learning has many benefits both for the learner and the administrator of the digital ecosystem, such as personalization, real-time monitoring and feedback, and process optimization. These capacities improve the learning experience [7] and promote inclusion and access to learners with neurodiversity. Furthermore, AI allows direct efforts to detect patterns and profiling strategies according to the diversity of learners [8] at each stage of the learning process, but is especially relevant in the early stages. In higher education, it becomes pertinent to broaden the possibilities for students to reach their highest potential by recognizing their demands toward a more effective and personalized education adapted to the diversity of students' learning styles and rhythms.

## III. METHOD

This study adopted an experimental approach to evaluate the effectiveness of decision tree algorithms in promoting inclusion and diversity within adaptive education systems. The research design incorporated data collection, the development of an experimental model, and the validation of results in a real educational context.

The study involved a diverse sample of 244 higher education students from one of Mexico's largest private universities. Participants were selected to represent a variety of sociodemographic characteristics, including gender, age range, learning preferences according to the VARK model, and specific learning needs such as dyslexia or Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). Participation was voluntary and anonymous, ensuring confidentiality and ethical adherence in data handling.

Data collection was conducted through a survey designed to gather detailed information on each student's individual characteristics. The survey included questions addressing demographic variables such as gender, age range, and access to high-speed internet. It also captured information related to learning preferences and specific needs that might require accommodation. The responses collected formed a dataset that represents a diverse student population.

The research procedure began with the administration of the survey, allowing the collection of baseline data prior to the interaction of students with the adaptive learning platform. The data collected was processed and encoded into a structured tabular format suitable for algorithmic analysis. Using this dataset, a decision tree algorithm was trained to classify students into one of 14 predefined categories, which were designed based on key characteristics such as learning styles, internet access, and the presence of special needs. These classifications were then used to dynamically tailor educational content and accessibility options within the platform. The model training used 70% of the dataset, reserving 30% for testing and validation purposes. The results demonstrated a classification accuracy greater than 95%, confirming the reliability of the model for deployment in adaptive learning environments.

Data analysis involved quantitative techniques to assess the accuracy and effectiveness of the model. Metrics such as precision and sensitivity were applied to assess the performance of the algorithm. In addition, descriptive analyzes were performed to identify trends and patterns within student profiles, providing information on the diversity of the participant population.

One of the key strengths of this methodology lies in its ability to dynamically adapt learning materials and accessibility features to the specific needs of individual students. However, the study acknowledges certain limitations. The sample consisted predominantly of students aged 18 to 25, restricting the generalizability of the findings to broader or more diverse populations. In addition, the classification accuracy was heavily dependent on the completeness and precision of the input data, highlighting the need for robust data collection mechanisms to minimize potential misclassifications. These limitations underscore the importance of extending future research to include more heterogeneous samples and exploring complementary approaches that enhance data reliability and algorithmic robustness.

## IV. RESULTS

## A. Entry Variables

To implement the classification algorithm, a set of foundational classes was defined, each designed to account for specific characteristics and values that distinguish them from each other [18]. The initial variables focused on demographic differentiators, which included students' gender and age range. Gender was categorized as 'male', 'female', 'non-binary', and 'prefer not to disclose'. The age ranges were grouped into four categories: under 18, between 18 and 25, between 25 and 60, and above 60. The second set of variables was related to the environment and contextual background of the students. These variables included categorical indicators such as whether a student belonged to an ethnic group with indigenous Mexican roots and whether they had access to high-speed broadband internet. The final set of variables was primarily related to the students' learning capabilities. These included how students process information, based on the VARK learning style model, and variables identifying sensory limitations or specific learning needs that may require additional attention, such as ADHD.

## B. Description of the Data Obtained

The data collection process involved the participation of 244 higher education students from Tecnologico de Monterrey. The students answered a poll that included their answers concerning the characteristics mentioned in the previous subsection. In the sample of students surveyed, the majority (98%) were found to be in the age range of 18 to 25 years. The remaining responses represented students younger than 18. For this study, data from students under 18 years of age were excluded from the data set. The gender identity of the students in the student sample was predominantly male, with 70% of the students polled. Meanwhile, 29% identified as female, and the remaining percent decided not to mention their gender. Regarding ethnicity, ninety-nine percent mentioned that they do not come from native roots, while only three students disclosed that they were from such lineages, representing Mixteco, Tlahuica, and Yaqui cultures.

## C. Classification Algorithm

A decision tree machine learning algorithm was selected to be used as a classifier for this study. This algorithm was selected over other machine learning tools, such as neural networks, due to its principal use in tabular data while providing faster performance compared to similar tools [19]. This algorithm creates a tree-like graph trained on previously classified data that generates a structure of nodes and branches that the algorithm transits according to different values in a received input until it reaches a final node that provides the appropriate classification [20]. A working decision tree was built in Python, using the Sci-kit Learn machine learning library [21].

## V. DISCUSSION

The results of this study highlight the potential of decision tree algorithms to promote inclusive and adaptive learning in higher education contexts, as evidenced by the algorithm's high accuracy of 95.94%. This accuracy suggests that decision trees can effectively classify and customize learning experiences for diverse students when trained with well-defined student profiles. This aligns with the study's aim of promoting inclusion and diversity through adaptive technology. The use of decision tree algorithms in adaptive learning platforms significantly contributes to educational innovation's practical and theoretical dimensions. On a practical level, the ability of the decision tree to accommodate specific learning needs - such as ADHD, dyslexia, and autism, as well as different learning styles - demonstrates how educational content can be dynamically tailored to individual profiles. This adaptability is essential for creating inclusive learning environments that cater to neurodiverse populations and varying sensory needs, as evidenced by the student classifications shown in Figure 3. Theoretically, these findings support the extension of adaptive learning theories by integrating AI methods, further advancing personalised educational approaches in response to cognitive diversity. The successful classification of students into 14 categories based on accessibility and learning needs validates the algorithm's ability to distinguish between different profiles accurately. The incorporation of accessibility tools such as UserWay to adapt web content based on specific needs not only improves usability for students with neurodiversity but also supports the equitable engagement of diverse user groups. This alignment with user needs suggests that decision trees are effective for classification and highly applicable for realtime adaptive learning interventions in digital educational platforms. The limitations of this study are mainly related to the diversity of the sample. While the current dataset provides valuable insights, extending this model to more diverse student populations would increase the robustness of the results. In addition, the accuracy of the decision tree classifications is highly dependent on the completeness and accuracy of the student input data. Incomplete or inaccurate data submissions could lead to misclassifications, thereby compromising the quality of personalisation and potentially limiting the effectiveness of adaptive responses. Future iterations of this model should consider safeguards or alternative algorithms to minimise these risks.

## VI. CONCLUSIONS

This study explored implementing decision trees to foster inclusion and diversity within adaptive education systems. Decision trees were chosen over other machine learning methods due to their efficiency in processing structured data and their ability to provide rapid classification results. This approach contributes to the current understanding of adaptive education by offering a method that customizes learning experiences and optimizes the content delivery process based on individual learner profiles. While the decision tree algorithm demonstrated high accuracy, the study acknowledges potential limitations. The profiles of the students surveyed were mostly focused on only a few of the 14 classes created, so it will still be necessary to test the decision tree algorithm by applying the experiment to other sets of students with greater heterogeneity. Furthermore, the classification process relies heavily on the accuracy and completeness of input data from students. Incomplete or inaccurate data could lead to misclassification and subsequently affect the personalization of learning experiences. This study advances the current views on educational inclusion and diversity by demonstrating how AIdriven adaptive education systems can effectively cater to varied learning needs. The integration of accessibility tools such as UserWay further enhances the inclusivity of the platform, accommodating students with special needs by dynamically adjusting web content to meet their specific requirements. Implementing decision trees within adaptive education systems presents a promising approach to fostering inclusion and diversity. Future research can continue to enhance the effectiveness and accessibility of educational technologies, ultimately contributing to more equitable and personalized learning environments for all students. Future studies could extend the application of this decision tree model to other educational settings or more extensive, more diverse student populations to validate its generalisability. Additional research is recommended to explore how decision tree-based adaptive learning can be enhanced with real-time feedback mechanisms that can further refine content personalisation based on ongoing student performance. Integrating other machine learning models, such as neural networks in conjunction with decision trees, may offer improved accuracy and adaptability in adaptive learning systems, promoting more accurate, responsive, and inclusive educational environments.

## I. INTRODUCTION

In  Japan,  there  has  been  a  significant  increase  in  the number  of  students  enrolled  in  special  education  classes, which  has  doubled  over  the  past  decade.  According  to  a survey  conducted  by  the  Ministry  of  Education,  Culture, Sports, Science, and Technology in December 2022, approximately 8.8% of children, equating to about 540,000 students in elementary schools nationwide, exhibit characteristics that necessitate special support. These characteristics include Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) [1], Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) [2, 3], Learning Disability (LD) [4], Specific Learning Disability (SLD),  and  Developmental  Coordination  Disorder  (DCD). Such  a  scenario  underscores  the  need  for  tailored  support throughout  the  various  stages  of  a  child's  life,  from  early childhood to adulthood. Furthermore, the Ministry's Course of Study for Special Needs Schools (2017 edition) emphasizes  the  importance  of  fostering  an  environment where  families,  schools,  and  support  institutions  work  in collaboration.

Schools are advised to consider the developmental stages of  children  and  students,  enhance  their  language  activities, and  work  toward  establishing  effective  study  habits  in collaboration  with  families.  The  'Vision  of  Special  Needs Education Towards the Establishment of an Inclusive Education System for a Symbiotic Society (Subcommittee on Elementary  and  Secondary  Education  in  2012)'  by  the Central Council for Education highlights the importance of close cooperation between schools and families in supporting children with disabilities. It suggests that schools and families of such children should regularly exchange information about the child's progress and clearly define their respective roles. In line with this, the Triangle Project was launched in 2018, aiming  to  further  promote  cooperation  among  families, education,  and  welfare  sectors,  ensuring  that  children  with disabilities and their parents receive seamless support in the community [5].

When  preparing  individual  educational  support  plans, schools should collaborate with relevant organizations, including  medical,  welfare,  health,  labor  institutions,  and private organizations. Emphasizing the importance of consistent support, the policy states, 'It is essential to provide continuous support for students with disabilities, from early childhood through post-graduation, encompassing school life, family life, and community involvement.' Therefore, schools must  develop  individualized  educational  support  plans  in collaboration with the student and their parents or guardians. The transition period is particularly critical, with guidelines suggesting that schools should appropriately share information about the student with their next higher education institution or other relevant entities, provided that they have obtained  the  consent  of  the  individual.  Globally,  various educational services utilize  ICT,  examples  include  systems for  sharing  knowledge  between  teachers  and  students  [6], knowledge  bases  for  effective  teaching  and  learning  in special education [7], and tools supporting personal learning environments in higher education [8].

Supporting children with developmental disabilities involves unique challenges due to the evolving nature of their conditions and the highly individualized nature of their needs. This complexity makes it difficult to accurately assess and address each child's specific situation. When offering support to these children, one of the primary challenges is that their conditions vary significantly as they develop, and capturing the  state  of  their  condition  can  be  challenging  due  to  its extreme variability from one individual to another.

Consequently, it's vital for teachers, guardians, and supporters  to  dynamically  and  closely  share  information tailored to the individual characteristics of each  child. Accumulating such information is essential to understand the behavioral  characteristics  of  the  children  and  to  provide support  that  is  tailored  to  their  individual  needs. In  Japan,  the  Ministry  of  Education,  Culture,  Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) and the Ministry of Health, Labour  and  Welfare  (MHLW)  are  actively  working  to promote cooperation among family, education, and welfare sectors.  This  collaboration  aims  to  support  children  with disabilities  and  developmental  disabilities,  as  well  as  their families. In Fukui Prefecture, a system has been developed and tested in a Proof of Concept (PoC). This system facilitates the  connection  among  schools,  families,  and  specialized institutions,  enabling  the  instant  and  dynamic  sharing  of information about children's behavior. It also provides educational support services tailored to the individual characteristics of each child.

In  previous  studies  [9,  10],  we  developed  a  system  that facilitates  immediate  and  dynamic  information  sharing  on children's  behavior  among  schools,  parents,  and  support organizations, thereby streamlining the support process. This system is designed with a human-centered approach and was developed using a spiral model. During the Proof of Concept (PoC)  phase,  we  have  been  continuously  enhancing  the system  by  adding  new  features  and  upgrading  versions  to meet evolving needs.

On the other hand, addressing the long working hours of teachers  in  the  field  is  equally  essential.  It  is  necessary  to utilize digital technology to enhance  the efficiency of administrative tasks and reduce teachers' workload.

According to the Survey of Teachers' Working Conditions in 2023 released by the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science  and  Technology  (MEXT),  77.1%  of  junior  high school teachers and 64.5% of elementary school teachers are reported to exceed the government's 45-hour per month limit for overtime work. MEXT acknowledges that, although there has been a decrease in working hours compared to the past, teachers  still  face  long  working  hours.  In  light  of  this,  the demonstration experiments conducted so far have highlighted the  need  for  functions  that  streamline  the  preparation  of documents related to individual educational support, communication with parents, and the transition of support to new  teachers,  support  staff,  and  successors  based  on  the individual characteristics of students.

In  this  study,  we  propose  a  novel  approach  that  utilizes Large Language Models (LLMs) [11] and RetrievalAugmented  Generation  (RAG)  [12,  13].  This  approach leverages the support information and behavioral history of students, which are accumulated in the Individualized Educational Support System, to generate personalized support recommendations.

Our study investigates how this automated text generation feature  can  alleviate  the  workload  of  busy  teachers  and support staff. Additionally, we present survey results regarding the educational support needs of supporters.

In related research, a study has been reported that developed a RAG system using pregnancy-related guidelines from  India,  focusing  on  medical  education  in  low-  and middle-income countries [14]. Additionally, as an application case in the field of education, research has been reported on the development of an AI tutor system that utilizes RAG to construct a  response  system  for  student  inquiries  [15]. However,  upon  investigation,  no  prior  research  applying RAG or LLMs to support for developmental disabilities was found.  In  this  respect,  this  study  can  be  considered  a pioneering effort.

The  use  of  LLMs  is  in  its  nascent  stages,  with  its application advancing across numerous fields. Within educational settings, the benefits of utilizing LLMs have been highlighted; however, concerns such as uncritically accepting generated  responses  have  also  been  noted.  In  2023,  the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) in Japan developed interim guidelines for  the  use  of  LLMs  in  primary  and  secondary  education. According to these guidelines, LLMs should be used merely as a starting point, and ultimately, it is essential for teachers and supporters to review and finalize the content themselves. Upon interviewing teachers and experts, it was suggested that it  is  more  accurate  and  efficient  for  the  person  who  was responsible before the transition to use the system, rather than having  the  new  person  in  charge  do  so.  By  conducting experimental demonstrations through this research, we have been able to gain important insights for the future introduction and social implementation of such technologies in educational settings.

Furthermore, the guidelines state that tools such as OpenAI ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot, Google Bard, Google Gemini, and  Anthropic  Claude  may  not  always  meet  the  necessary security requirements, thus necessitating caution not to input sensitive information when entering prompts. Consequently, as  demonstrated  in  this  research,  employing  local  (offline) LLMs and RAG to avoid external data exposure is preferable.

## III. METHODS

Large Language Models (LLMs) [11] have been trained using a vast corpus. They learn grammar and knowledge from a tremendous amount of text and can perform various natural language processing tasks, not limited to specific tasks but including translation, summarization, and questionanswering.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [12, 13] involves searching for information relevant to a question from external sources before LLMs perform question-answering. RAG has been  proven  to  significantly  improve  answer  accuracy, particularly in knowledge-intensive tasks, and to reduce the frequency of incorrect responses.

First, the target external information is converted into text and split into appropriate units (Fig. 3). In this study, a single unit is defined as the comments stored in PicotTo retrieved on a daily basis. These split texts are then transformed into vectors using a text embedding model. When a user poses a question,  it  is  also  converted  into  a  vector  using  a  similar document embedding model. The inner product between the vectors  of  this  query  and  the  split  external  information  is calculated to determine their similarity. Documents with high similarity, along with the query, are used as inputs to perform question-answering with the LLMs (Fig. 4).

In  PicotTo,  comments  shared  among  supporters  and comments  directed  towards  the  children  are  stored.  These comments are segmented by date and by supporter and saved in  a  vector  database.  In  this  study,  data  from  a  six-month period pertaining to a specific child were utilized.

Due to the potential inclusion of personal information in the  data  used  for  supporting  children  with  developmental disabilities,  services  such  as  the  OpenAI  API  cannot  be employed for LLMs or embedding models. Therefore, it is necessary to utilize  LLMs and embedding models that can operate  in  a  local  environment.  Several  LLMs  capable  of local operation have been released, among which Llama2 is widely known [16]. In this study, we use models that have been  fine-tuned  with  Llama2,  including  Xwin-LM-70BV0.1-GPTQ,  Xwin-LM-13B-V0.2,  Swallow-70B-instructGPTQ,  and  Swallow-13B-instruct-hf.  These  models  have demonstrated  improved  capabilities  in  generating  Japanese text due to the fine-tuning. Xwin-LM-70B-V0.1-GPTQ and Swallow-70B-instruct-GPTQ  are  models  that  have  been subjected  to  a  quantization  technique  called  GPTQ  [17], applied  respectively  to  Xwin-LM-70B-V0.1  and  Swallow70B-instruct-hf. GPTQ is a quantization technology that can compress the bit-width per model weight to approximately 4 bits  while retaining the performance of the original model. This  allows  the  models  to  be  used  in  environments  with limited  computational  resources.  For  the  text  embedding model, 'multilingual-e5-large' is utilized.

## VII. CONCLUSIONS

This study developed two systems to facilitate information coordination among supporters of children with developmental disabilities and provide personalized support. The  first  system  utilizes  Retrieval-Augmented  Generation (RAG) for a question-answering system based on individual student information, while the second system employs Large Language Models (LLMs) for summarizing individual student information. Experiments with the questionanswering  system,  using  sample  questions  and  multiple models, confirmed the possibility of providing answers based on  content  specific  to  each  child.  Evaluations  by  actual supporters  confirmed  the  effectiveness  of  the  summary system in providing individualized support.

The  survey  results  indicated  a  need  for  a  function  that utilizes  AI  to  automatically  summarize  behavioral  history, particularly for skilled teachers and supporters who have been involved in the Proof of Concept (PoC) to date. There was significant anticipation for the application  of  RAG  to independently segregate and utilize data, and to facilitate the transfer  of  information  to  subsequent  entities.  Moreover, there were high expectations for such technology to reduce the  workload  involved  in  preparing  individual  educational support  plans.  In  Japan,  where  individualized  educational support plans and instructional plans are mandatory, streamlining  this  part  of  the  workload  could  significantly alleviate the burden on teachers. The survey also revealed a strong demand for system usage and a keen interest among teachers  regarding  its  utilization  and  operation.  The  future operation mode of the system is an area of focus, necessitating ongoing adaptations to reflect teachers' needs and  discussions  with  the  Board  of  Education  and  other stakeholders.

A limitation of  this  study  is  the  difficulty  in  conducting experiments with multiple children and students. This constraint is primarily due to two important factors. The first is  the  need  for  ethical  approval  and  adequate  consent  for experiments  involving  data  on  children  and  students.  The second is the challenge of efficiently collecting and recording information  on  children  and  students,  due  to  the  lack  of widespread  systems,  making  it  difficult  to  obtain  diverse experimental  data.  An  additional  critical  limitation  of  this study  arises  from  the  propensity  of  LLMs  to  produce hallucinations [18], which are instances where the generated text  includes  inaccurate  information  not  grounded  in  user inputs  or  existing  facts.  This  phenomenon  significantly undermines  the  reliability of text generation, posing  a substantial  challenge  to  achieving  completely  accurate  and trustworthy outputs.

As a future research direction, it is proposed to construct a system  that  not  only  incorporates  PicotTo's  comments  but also utilizes documents recording the actual support provided, enabling more detailed information sharing.

Currently, it's crucial for the social implementation of the functions highlighted in this report to validate their effectiveness  in  facilitating  effective  student  support  while alleviating the workload of teachers and parents. Gaining an understanding of the use of LLMs in educational and welfare support  is  fundamental.  We  believe  that  incorporating  an automatic sentence generation function using LLMs into an ICT-based  individualized  education  support  system  will significantly contribute to realizing more effective education in today's fast-paced society. We aim to develop this system for broader social implementation, fostering a collaborative environment between the educational field and the system. This  collaboration  is  key  to  achieving  a  society  where  no student  or  parent  is  left  behind,  ultimately  benefiting  all involved.

## 1   Introduction

The term 'Special Educational Needs and Disabilities' (SEND), refer to students who have learning problems or disabilities that make it harder for them to learn than most of their peers. This  may include physical, development disabilities, behavioural, emotional and communication disorders and learning deficiencies (Kryszewska, 2017).  Universities and colleges in the UK welcome almost 30,000 disabled students each year (UCAS, 2018). As of today, there are only 9 EU countries,  including  France  and  the  United  Kingdom,  who  have  implemented  policy plans to help SEND students in higher education (Limbach-Reich &amp; Powell, 2016). Some of these include free transport to and from universities, special software to aid learning and teaching and other simple assistance to students with specific impairment. However, there is a lack of support and social inclusion for students having a learning disability. There are a number of other concerns such as poor quality, wrong  career  advice  or  lack  or  guidance  for  students  with  a  learning  disability (Disability Rights UK, 2017). Research shows that the dropout from education in the EU for the disabled is at 31.5%, much higher compared to only 12.3% for non- disabled  students  (Limbach-Reich  &amp;  Powell,  2016).  Supporting  young  students who require special educational needs in pursuing higher education is an ambitious and necessary step that needs to be adopted by tertiary education providers worldwide. MALSEND project aims at building an artificial intelligence platform using machine learning algorithms to identify any patterns in the type of learning disability of a student and their chosen subject at university level. The platform will analyse large datasets from UK universities in the previous years and will be trained to discover any trends in subject areas and performance among autistic students, dyslexic students or students having attention deficit hyperactive disorder (ADHD), among others. The system might also help us identify other trends among certain learning disabilities and subject performance based on big datasets which would be impossible for humans to detect otherwise.

## 1.1 Statement of the Problem

Disability Rights UK states that there are a number of issues they found out during their events, steering groups and participation in various meetings and seminars, among which were the issues of having poor quality or wrong career advice for students with a learning disability, bad choice of subjects that do not match these students' aspirations and lack of guidance for students with specific learning disabilities (Disability Rights UK, 2017). There is a lack of support and social inclusion for students having a learning disability to go into higher education, participate in various domains and contribute to the economy of a country (Kim, Shin, Yu, &amp; Kim, 2016) . This might include having a regular income, having a good quality of life  and having access to the overall social and educational system. Pathways to tertiary education for SEND students depend on the type of learning disability, financial resources and self-motivation of students after leaving secondary schools. To reduce dropout and encourage those with learning disabilities, research must be carried out to see what are the best options for these students in terms of subject areas, performance and their potential contribution to the society. However, there are no models that can detect any trends or patterns in the learning disability of a student, their  performance and success in specific areas. If such a  model can be implemented, then the probability of having more students to enter tertiary education might rise and hopefully reduce the dropout rate since the latter will know that similar students suffering from the same learning disability managed to complete their degree. Having such a model can also help students to have a voice and help organisations in policy making around SEND students.

## 1.3 Purpose of Inquiry and Inquiry Questions

This conceptual paper is proposed to identify and recognise factors that affect the learning and performance of higher education students suffering from a learning disability through an analysis of students' records from previous years. Therefore, the research questions for this study focuses on the following; 1) Can a machine learning platform be used to identify any repeated patterns of data among a learning disability, subject area and performance of students at tertiary education level? and 2) Can the platform be trained with more datasets to then predict the subject area a SEND student is most likely to succeed based on his learning disability?

The next section of this paper discusses the related work.

## 2 Related Work

## 2.1. Machine Learning

Machine Learning (ML) is an application of artificial intelligence  (AI)  whereby programs uses statistical models to give computer systems the ability to learn without being fully programmed (Jordan &amp; Mitchell, 2015). The adoption of machinelearning methods can be found throughout different areas to improve marketing, decision-making in health care, manufacturing, education and also applied for financial analysis. For example, machine learning is being used to predict mortality rate associated with certain type of diseases, predict effectiveness of surgical procedures, to help physicians make better decision and to discover relationship among clinical and diagnosis data. Unsupervised algorithms attempt to overcome limitations of supervised learning algorithms by automatically identifying patterns and dependencies in the data. Unsupervised learning can be beneficial for this study as it allows the algorithms to look back for patterns that have not been previously considered. Useful new information can therefore be extracted from the observed data to building predictors. In statistical unsupervised learning pattern recognition, the data can be identified by finding clusters, for example by using K-means algorithms or Adaptive Resonance Theory (ART) adapted algorithms, dimensionality reduction or Principle Component Analysis (PCA) to reduce the number of random variables under consideration by defining a set of principle variables (Wagstaff, Cardie, Rogers, &amp; Schroedl, 2008). It is important to understand the relation between identified clusters and competitive learning algorithms can provide efficient solutions to problems.

## 2.2 Prediction of student's performance

Previous work has been done to predict the performance of students as shown by a few  studies  (Cortez  &amp;  Silva,  2008)(Thiede  et  al.,  2015)(Chamorro-Premuzic  &amp; Furnham, 2003). Different machine learning techniques, such as matrix factorization  (Thai-Nghe,  Horváth,  &amp;  Schmidt-Thieme,  2011)  or    collaborative  filtering (Toscher &amp; Jahrer, 2010) have been used to predict students' grades. The right research  questions  are  important  to  understand  the  existing  studies  of  predicting SEND students' area of expertise. Current studies make use of Cumulative Grade Point Average (CGPA), assignment mark, quizzes, lab work, class test and attendance to predict performance of students in general (Mohamed Shahiri, Husain, &amp; Abdul Rashid, 2015). Other researchers considered gender, age, family background, disability,  extra-curricular  activities,  social  interaction  and  psychometric  factors (Mohamed Shahiri et al., 2015) to see how these affect the student's performance. The proposed study, however, is looking at identifying relationships between identified factors and SEND students' areas of expertise by analysing at least 15,000 student records. To the best of the authors' knowledge, there are no studies that tried to identify how a specific learning disability can be associated with particular subject areas and this is an identified gap in the literature.

## C. Analysis

## I. Dimensionality reduction

Using Scikit-Learn (software machine library for Python) (Géron, 2017), the data will first be fed to a dimensionality reduction algorithm to reduce the number of variables and simplify the data without losing much information. There are group of algorithms that can be used to remove unneeded data, outliers, and other nonuseful data. Dimensionality reduction will be made to free storage space on our server and improve the performance of our machine learning system. It will also help the researchers to visualize the data (Hurwitz &amp; Kirsch, 2018). An anomaly detection algorithm can also automatically remove outliers from the datasets.

## II. K-Means Clustering

Clustering (k-means) and visualisations algorithms can then be applied to the dataset to identify clusters and unsuspected patterns.  Finally, another method of unsupervised learning, known as association rule learning algorithm will be used to discover interesting relations among other attributes.

## Limitations

Thre are a few identified limitations for this pilot study which can affect the results. For example, since the sample data is taken from only 2 universities in the first phase, the results does not represent the demographics of the population. Moreover, the fact that some universities provide more specific courses than other universities, the results may show certain patterns and high correlations among a specific learning disability and a course. Hence, there is a need to carry out the second part of the project to test any hypothesis and conclude any findings. More importantly, there are other factors such as social, economic factors or family background that can also affect the results of this study.

## Discussion and Conclusion

We expect the machine learning platform to generate new knowledge by identifying patterns that could help solve some of the social challenges such as high dropout rates from education (31.5% compared to 12.3% for non-disabled students), low employability (only 6% of adults with a learning disability in England are in paid work) or depression among SEND students in the future. The findings will create new research questions and help bring other universities together through collective intelligence to find similar patterns regarding other health conditions (visual or hearing impairment, epilepsy). The results in wider applicability could also be used to support career advisers in schools, colleges and communities by providing course suggestions tools.  Finally, the findings could assist the government and other institutions for the development of policies, curriculum and practices to help those students to find a direction in regards to their career.

As to the best of our knowledge, no such system has yet been implemented to help SEND students in the UK find out what are the subject areas chosen by other past students who had a similar learning disability as them, their success/failure in particular areas and their future career paths. The findings of this study might open new opportunities and act as a guide to those having a learning disability and entering higher education. It might also help in the investigation of the performance of students with a specific learning disability based on identified patterns.

Future AI and machine learning prediction models can provide an extra set of eyes and ears for SEND students, therapists, teachers as well as parents. Further analysis of this data can lead to pinpointing social success factors and assessing a student's strengths and weaknesses. Moreover, educators would be better able to understand the students' learning and emotional development, slowly introducing them to more complex and varied social environments over time.

## II. STATE OF ART

## A. Learning Object (LO)

The IEEE defines Learning Objects as 'any entity, digital or  non-digital,  which  can  be  used,  re-used  or  referenced during  technology  supported  learning'.  As  examples  of Learning Objects include: multimedia and instructional content, learning objectives, instructional software and software  tools,  persons,  organizations,  or  events  referenced during technology supported learning [6].

Learning objects serve as an instructional design component in e-learning for the development and delivery of educational content [7].

For Collis [8] the technology involving learning objects can  be  considered at four levels. In this research,  we considered the technology related to the repository in which the  objects  are  collected,  including  database  technology and/or learning content management technology [9].

Furthermore,  is  important  to  highlight  that  repositories and reuse of learning objects in different learning environments  have  a  description  of  standardized  content, called metadata.

The IEEE organizations [9] and Instructional Management  System  (IMS)  [10]  consider  the  following characteristics  as  common  to  learning  objects:  reusability, portability, modularity, metadata, and interactivity.

A  learning  object  must  have  its  granularity  proper  in order to provide reuse, must be developed for use on various platforms (through this, respecting the feature of portability), and be produced in independent and not sequential modules, and finally being used in conjunction with other resources.

The  most  common  metadata  standards  are  Learning Object  Metadata  (LOM)  of  the  IEEE  and  the  Sharable Content Object Reference Model (SCORM).

## B. Accessible Learning Object (ALO)

According to the IMS Global Learning Consortium [10] accessibility is defined as a set of efforts in order to facilitate access  to  the  media  and  social,  educational  and  cultural characteristics  to  reduce  the  effect  of  a  limitation  of  the environment and greater equality for students with disabilities.  For  the  World  Wide  Web  Consortium  (W3C) [11], accessibility is an extension of sites, products, services or information, making them available to the greatest number and variety of people regardless of their physical and motor, cultural and social limitations and perceptions. In this sense, both have worked to put guidelines and standards in place to assist in ensuring interoperability and accessibility.

Considering the above concepts, it is understood that an Accessible Learning Object is a Learning Object with accessibility  requirements  and  social  inclusion  premises  of People with Disabilities (PWD).  For  this reason, it is possible  to  provide  autonomy  in  access  to  information  and learning through  digital resources. In this research, the concept of Accessible Learning Objects was adopted according to [2] as any digital material (images, videos, web pages, animations or simulations), since they provide information for the construction of knowledge, attends standards and guidelines of accessibility, and was structured so that they can be reused.

Batanero  [12]  explores  in  your  paper  the  relationship between  accessible learning objects and  accessible  web content.  Macedo  [13]  presents  a  significant  contribution  to teams or developers working on the development of Accessible Learning Object.

## C. Multi-agent Systems (MAS)

There are several multi-agent-based e-learning applications  in  the  literature  [14].  In  this  way,  the  authors state  that  artifacts  represent  resources  and  tools  and  can  be dynamically created and managed by agents to support their respective activities.

According  to  Russel  [15],  an  agent  is  all  that  can  be considered  capable  of  perceiving  its  environment  through sensors  and  acting  on  this  environment  through  actuators. For Wooldridge [16] an agent is a computer system, situated in some  environment, which is capable of performing autonomous action in this environment in order to meet the objectives for which it was designed.

A  Multi-Agent  System  is  a  distributed  system  where  a group of autonomous agents reaches their goals in a reactive, proactive  and  social  way.  According  to  [17],  an  MAS  is  a system  composed  of  cooperative  or  competitive  agents, which interact with each other to achieve a common goal or individual.

The agent is a computer system positioned in an environment that can act with autonomy flexible to reach the objectives  for  which  it  was  designed  [18].  For  the  author, some properties must be observed in an agent. In accordance with  [19],  we  highlight  features  as  situatedness,  autonomy, pro-activeness, adaptiveness, responsiveness, mobility.

As  described  in  [20],  there  are  several  types  of  agents, and they can be: software or hardware, stationary or mobile, persistent or temporary, reactive, or cognitive. In this sense, the  design  of  a  multi-agent  system  should  consider  several aspects  as  type  or  problem,  the  autonomy  of  the  agent, knowledge representation, communication protocols, definition of system organization, data modeling, cooperation, and conflict resolution.

## B. Analyzer Agent

This  Agent  entitled  'Analyzer  Agent'  has  a  role  to perceive  the  environment,  and  uses  all  of  the  history  and knowledge to perform its function. Therefore, the agent only to accomplish an action from a perception.

First,  the  agent  receives  and  analyzes  the  data  provided by the professor in relation to the course, discipline, content, and disability (physical, auditory, cognitive, visual or multiple) of the student. After, the agent recommends ALO the  most  appropriate  resource.  Followed,  the  agent  sends  a message  with  the  requirements  for  the  recommendation agent.

## C. Recommender Agent

This Agent entitled 'Agent Recommender' has a role to recommend for professors the Accessible Learning Objects. After  receiving  the  message  of  the  analyzer  agent,  the recommendation agent confirms receipt. Then, it communicates  with  the  data  agent  to  obtain  a  response  in relation to the request. The data agent runs the search in the repository by means of the metadata and sends the results to the Recommendation Agent.

Recommendation  Agent  will  provide  to  the  Human Agent (professor) a list containing one (1) or more ALO in accordance with its request or, if not any ALO be found, it will issue a warning message (Fig. 4). The items are a list by type of disability and content when found.

## D. Data Agent

This  Agent  entitled  'Data  Agent'  is  responsible  for recover accessible learning objects in repository ROAA. The access occurs  through  the  metadata, data are collected,  and sent to Recommender Agent. The agent organizes, classifies the items, and sends the results obtained for the human agent. If  there  are  no  results  a  message  is  sent  stating  "that  no objects were found in accordance with the requested requirements".

According to this  approach,  an  agent is  capable  to  play the  role  of  people  and  can  acquire  new  knowledge  by  the interaction  with  professors  and  other  agents,  raising  the potential  their  understanding  and  performance.  The  ALO metadata permits the identification of what educational topic is related to the requirements.

In this way, the agents can find out what is the content or disability  associated  with  the user  experience  shown  by  the professor,  and  then  to  show  complementary  information  or recommend other objects.

SIMROAA  makes  possible the recommendation of Accessible Learning Objects automatically through the environment Web.

Thus,  we  adopt  the  model  cited  by Molina,  Koutrika,  and  Parameswaran  [26].  The  authors affirm that the Communities of Practice (COP) are experiencing a flood of data with the  increasing  volume  of digital objects. The article reports a recommendation mechanism using COP's in the format of learning objects, in communities of virtual practices, through a network or circle of trust.

Training  professors  to  teach  computer  science  through sustained  support  by  a  community  of  practice  [31],  [32], [33], is a challenge to be overcome in computing due to the technical training of professors [1]. Equally important, reflect in the affirmation of your professional self-identity in the classroom [34].

Thus, we  accomplish  prototyping and simulation to improve  the  trust  indexes  necessary  to  guarantee  a  precise recommendation, considering the architecture shown in Fig.5. In SIMROAA,  the  agent  of  recommendation is responsible for filtering the relationships of trust and classifying the relevant learning objects  according  to  a human  agent,  in  our  case  the  professor.  In  this  way,  the model was contextualized in the Communities of Practice in the  field  of  teaching  and  learning,  where  trust  relationships are  essential  for  the  development  of  collaboration  between professors.  The  proposed  model  used  the  trust  construct among  professors  as  a  metric  for  the  recommendation  of learning objects.

According to [27] COP  is a combination of three elements: the domain of knowledge, which defines a set of issues; the group of people who care about this domain; and shared practice for the internalization of knowledge. That is, if  there  is  trust  among  its  members,  then  the  communities produce  faster  and  more  prosperous  results.  Table  1  shows the trust-based recommendation model.

The  interactions  at  the  COP  are  shown  in  Table  1  by crossing  the  components  of  the  model  such  as  the  Human Agent (professors), Recommendation Agent, Learning Object and the interactive environment that is the COP.

It is important to emphasize that establishing trust among the  members  (professors)  of  a  COP  is  an  accepted  social process as a prerequisite for cooperation. For [28] levels of trust among members influence the sharing of resources and the exchange of useful knowledge.

In  this  framework,  the  recommendation  agents  interact by presenting accessible learning objects relevant to human agents.  Suggestions  from  the  recommendation  agents  are related to the various decision-making processes.

Professors  indicate the  confidence  index  of  the  learning objects.  This  indication  is  marked  in  the  user  interface, where  a  Likert  scale  of  7  points  represents  the  confidence degree. Graphically it is shown the scale as 7 stars above the professor  avatar.  This  confidence  index  is  used  to  indicate the  reliability  degree  between  people  and  their  confidence circles.

The inclusion of the index (icon favorite) is justified by the fact that professors are more likely to use the accessible learning  objects  produced  by  their  trust  network.  Thus,  the recommendation mechanism performs clustering of the trusted  network,  removing  nodes  with  indexes  below  the scale means from the graph. After the procedure, the trusted items  were  identified,  and  the  agent  then  sorts  the  ALO, classifying them according to the user context information.

Recommendation agent clusters the network to detect the hidden  structures  and  group  the  trusted  members.  In  this context, people are represented by vertices and the trust are the weighted edges that connect the members of the network.

Each  edge  of  the  network  has  a  weight  relative  to  a weighted graph G = {V, E}, where the confidence index of the vertex V'1 with the vertex V'2 expresses the weight. The confidence  network  is  formed  by  the  way  made  to  the weighted graph where the vertices are removed, whose edges have  a  weight  less  than  0.4  or  unreliable  within  the  Likert scale of 1 to 7. Table 2 shows the scheme.

Thus, we obtain a sub graph G '= {V', E '} representing the  relations  dependable  as  a  function  of  a  specific  human agent. The direct connections are important, in this example represents the confidence in only 1 degree in the graph G '.

In the graph G', the data agent queries accessible learning objects  produced  by  members  of  the  trusted  network,  and route  the  information  to  the  recommendation  agent  that  to accomplish the classification and recommendation.

The information used refers to the user login (professor), registered by the analyzer agent. The recommendation agent, after consulting the data agent in the repository, presents the resulting accessible learning objects according to the professor profile and informed requirements.

ALO resulting from the query are classified according to the weight assigned to it (considering the author-professor). In this way, the degrees of confidence of the weighted edges adjacent to the professor  who  produced  the  ALO  are summed.  Considering  the  process,  a  weight  is  assigned  to each learning object stored in the repository. The weight is used as the ordering criterion when presented to the human agent.

In  this  way,  we  obtained  a  functional  recommendation system, using intelligent agents and COP, where the recommendation is based on trust. It's important to create an instrument to identify factors related to  professors  and classes, in order to plan actions for training and definitions of teaching strategies [37].

## V. CONCLUSION

This  paper  presents  the  development  of  the  SIMROAA that  is  a  recommendation  system  designed  to  attend  the Model proposed by Mourão e Netto [2]. The objective of this proposal  is  to  provide  the  professors  of  the  computing nucleus of the State University of Amazonas an educational solution  to  the  recommendation  of  accessible  educational resources.  And,  in  the  future,  allow  the  repository  to  be available and can be shared by other universities, professors, and courses.

Models and support mechanisms were analyzed to make this recommendation process. The viability of the methodology was obtained through tests with agents, prototypes, and simulations. Besides, most professors considered  motivating  to  learn  through  learning  objects,  as well as considered that the objects presented a new reality.

Recommendation systems provide access to the elements and provide distinct strategies for recommending objects. In this  educational  context,  its  use  prevents  professors  from spending a lot of time locating accessible learning objects to attend  their  goals.  The  use  of  COP  allowed  validating  the trust, which is a fundamental element for the construction of relations between the members of a community. The inclusion of the trust construct to the metrics guarantees the degree of trust of the neighbors identified in the professor's network of relationships. Thus, the mechanism also depends on the  social  system  to  indicate  the  degree  of  trust  and  the technological agents accomplish clustering tasks of confidence circles and the association for inference of accessible learning objects.

The results  obtained  by  this  work  contribute  to  support cooperation and collaboration in teaching and learning processes. This way, encourage the professors to use educational tools that collaborate with improvements in the teaching  and  learning  process,  stimulating  the  adoption  of inclusive education.

As  future  work,  we  propose  that  this  recommendation system  after  consolidated  will  become  public  and  can  be used  by  other  higher  education  institutions  and  professors. Also, explore and incorporate data mining techniques within ROAA. And finally, the development of a chatterbot, where the users will interact with the application.

## II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

Games can provide  a  better  and  more  efficient  learning environment as they introduce a 'fun' element to education which  makes  the  whole  experience  more  appealing  for students.  Gamification  of  education  is  not  only  there  to engage users but also to develop cognitive abilities as well as problem solving skills. The lack of enjoyment in education leads to failure in learning [1]. When students are engaged, their motivation to learn will increase because they become more  focused  [2].  Students  thrive  on  dynamic  learning experiences.  Boredom  is  still  a  challenge  in  traditional education  settings.  The  view  of  combining  gaming  with education is not a new one, however recently, there has been a steady increase in 'gamifying' education and creating more interactable content to motivate learners and increase engagement [3].

A study conducted by [4] which investigated how the role of modern games in children's development concluded that games with enough dynamic content can enable children to develop physically their motor  skills and intellectually through problem solving skills. There is a strong association between engaging in computer-based learning activities and cognitive development in children. Research conducted by [5] found  the  evidence  that  specially  designed  games  do  have benefits in increasing coordinated motor skills. Authors in [6] found  strong  evidence  in  measurable  changes  of  neural processing through the use of gaming. It is important to note that enhanced cognitive performance is not a product of all the  digital  games  that  are  available,  but  the  essence  of increasing cognitive processing through games is a possibility, if designed correctly. There are several ways to assess  the  usability  and  efficacy  of  educational  games. Human-Computer Interaction (HCI) techniques, (e.g. semiotic inspection), allow computer scientists and educational  game  developers  to  evaluate  such  games  and efficacy on children development [7].

The  authors  in  [8]  designed  an  interesting  and  novel interface integrating a commercial headband with one single dry electrode to get beta frequency from the frontal lobe to measure the concentration level. They integrated the virtual environment  with  the  leap  motion  technology  for  gesture recognition. They aimed at using this novel interface developed in Unity for children with cerebral palsy. Despite the  idea  being  interesting,  the  researchers  did  not  use  any machine learning concepts for gesture recognition with the leap motion sensor, nor to automatically classify brainwave activity  from  the  EEG  sensor.  Instead,  this  work  opted  for basic functionalities provided by both commercial sensors as tools, and thus analysis of the performance of concentration levels of the users or the efficacy of the gesture recognition in specific tasks was not possible. However, they evaluate the acceptance of their novel virtual environment as a potential user-friendly interface that can be used to measure concentration level.

Different approaches have been developed using EEG data to classify emotions [9], immersive 3D games [10], control games  [11],  [12],  and  analysis  of  brainwave  patterns  of concentration, mood, etc. while patients play games [13]. But none of them have combined an intelligent game based on response  of  EEG,  natural  user  interface  and  emotional reactions together to keep the user's concentration during the interaction. Furthermore, none of them have monitored and analyzed long-term data to monitor the progress of the user over time after multiple training sessions. This work is going beyond state-of-the-art by developing an intelligent system that will  be able to adapt  to the  users  by  generating appropriate  stimuli  to  keep  their  concentration  during  the training sessions based on the classification of  EEG brainwave  patterns.  In  addition,  the  future  implementation shown in Fig. 2 will make our approach a potential tool for neurocognitive training of children with attention deficit.

## III. AI-BASED INTELLIGENT GAME DEVELOPMENT

The idea of an AI-based interactive game is conceptualized here for an intervention context, with a number of potential applications  in  the  education  system,  such  as  cognitive educational  therapy  (e.g.  facilitation  of  coping  skills),  and health  promotion  settings  (e.g.  training  of  health-related skills).  Current  empirical  evidence  suggests  that  the  use  of smart  technology  in  therapeutic  and  educational  context  is likely  to  enhance  the  outcomes  of  cognitive-behavioral interventions. The primary question of this work is: How can one  endow  an  intelligent  game  with  advanced  perception, learning  and  interaction  capabilities  in  order  to  stimulate children with learning difficulties to enhance their cognitive and motor functions without stressing, but engaging them ? In order to answer this question, our strategy is focused on: (i) the  development  of  an  AI-based  game  which  adopts  an approach to automatically classify mental states to measure concentration  levels  through  EEG  data;  (ii)  control  and interaction via gesture recognition using hand motion patterns  within  a  game  environment;  (iii)  focusing  the neurocognitive  training  using  user-friendly  interfaces  for tasks such as reasoning, hand-eye coordination, and memory to  improve  the  attention  deficit  of  children  with  learning difficulties; (iv) real-time, autonomous adaption by the game when  concentration  drops.  Once  the  concentration  level  is classified during the user interaction, the intelligent system can  make  decision  on  whether  new  stimulus  (visual  or auditory)  is  needed  to  keep  the  user  attention  to  avoid concentration loss.

## A. EEG Data Processing and Classification

Our  EEG  classification  technique  is  based  on  previous observations with mental state [14] and emotional state [15]. In these works, the temporal brainwave signals are processed, and mathematical features are extracted. This is performed through  a  sliding  window  technique,  where  windows  of length 1s with an overlap of 0.5s capture short segments of brain  activity,  and  the  temporal  data  is  then  converted  to singular  data  points  covering  the  window  through  various algorithms. The 988 features derived from the 1s windows as well as the 0.25s and 0.5s windows by offset include Fourier Transforms,  Shannon  Entropy,  and  Log-energy  Entropy among  many  others 1 .  An  extensive  set  of  features  beyond those  usually  observed  in  a  clinical  setup  [17],  [18]  are extracted due to the more cost-effective nature of the Muse headband  sensor  in  comparison  to  expensive  but  higher quality clinical EEGs.

Following  this,  the  features  are  organized  into  a  matrix, and since temporal segments are now  represented as numerical  features,  machine  learning  algorithms  can  learn useful pattern recognition rules from the data. In this study, we  consider  the  ground-truth  dataset;  that is, the data collected  during  the  calibration  phase  at  the  start  of  the experiment. We benchmark various classical machine learning algorithms on the ternary problem of concentrating/relaxed/neutral and compare the predictions to the collected data in order to discern how classifiable the data is prior to future exploration into the unknown data, which is the  data  recorded  while  the  child  is  interacting  with  the educational  game.  Training  is  performed  on  the  basis  of 10-fold cross validation technique in order to prevent overfitting.

## B. Interfaces Development Using UNITY Game Engine

Unity is a 2D and 3D game development engine created by Unity  Technologies,  allowing  the  development  of  a  wide range of games on multiple platforms. It has been extensively used  in  industry  and  academia  since  it  has  a  number  of features that make it particularly appealing including relevant standpoints,  such  as  inexpensive,  easy  to  use,  has  a  fairly shallow  learning  curve,  accommodates  multiple  scripting languages,  and  ships  with  intuitive  prototyping  tools.  Our games  are  based  on  educational  games  reported  in  the state-of-the-art,  focused  on  perception,  memory,  reasoning and  attention.  We  designed  a  spelling  game,  where  the interface randomly selects images of animals and their sound, separated  into  categories  of  easy,  medium  and  complex spelling. Once  children  interact  with the interface  and progress  on  each  phase,  the  difficulty  is  increased.  The interface generates random cubes with alphabetic letters on the  ground,  and  the  user  has  to  pick-up  the  letters  in  the correct order corresponding the animal's name and throw it into a box until the word is complete. If the player succeeds, then a new image of animal is randomly given, otherwise, the game warns the user about the mistake and prompts the user to try again. ## C. Gesture Recognition

Our  gesture  recognition  module  is  based  on  the  Leap Motion Controller (LMC) Software Development Kit (SDK) functions. The LMC is primarily designed for hand gesture and finger position detection in interactive software applications.  It  consists  of  three  infrared  emitters  and  two CCD cameras to track the hand. It provides preprocessed data through their Application Programming Interface (API). The data provided is given as follows (Fig. 5): the position of the palm  frame-by  frame,  its  normal  and  velocity;  the  hand direction; fingertips position and velocity; and arm direction. Currently  we  are  using  the  leap-motion  API  functions  for gesture recognition instead of implementing our own classifier,  since  it  works  very  well  for  gestures  such  as opening  and  closing  the  hand  for  simple  tasks  (e.g.  pinch, pick-up  and  release  an  object,  and  also  static  gesture  like pointing,  two-fingers,  three-fingers  and  so  on).  The  hand pose  provided  by  the  API  can  be  directly  mapped  as  the controller (e.g. palm and finger position and orientation) in the game interface to move an object forward, backward, left, right and rotate it based on the hand orientation.

The  Unity  Game  Engine  and  LMC  SDK  are  easily compatible  with  one  another,  making  game  design  and implementation integration easier, since the LMC functions are embedded within the Unity development interface.

## IV. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND DATA ACQUISITION

In order to gather data for the experiment, the commercial Muse headband (EEG) sensor is used as can be observed in Fig.  6.  The  subject  group  was  made  up  of  a  total  of  30 children from 3 different public primary schools in Cambé , a municipality within the State of Paraná  in Southern Brazil. All of the children's legal parents or guardians were informed in detail of the project characteristics and written consent was given. Special monitoring roles were created for the trial in order to ensure compliance of ethical requirements, confidentiality and protection of personal data. No trials were performed without previous approval by the ethical committee and data protection authorities in Brazil. The pilot trials were conducted in accordance with the highest ethical standards  from  the  UK  and  Brazil.  To  ensure  that  the information is easy to understand, all written information that was given to the involved people was approved by experts on 'Easy to Read' guidelines. All  trials  were  conducted  after receiving the signed 'Informed Consent Form'. The sample size of 30 children is selected for preliminary experimentation,  with  positive  results  presenting  assurance and reason for collecting a much larger dataset in the future.

The subject group of 30 children were aged from six to ten years  old,  24  were  male  and  6  were  female.  The  children considered  in  this  experiment  all  had  forms  of  disability including Intellectual Disability  (ID),  Autism  Spectrum Disorder (ASD) and Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) which were widespread within the subject group, oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) and Hydrocephaly were also  present  within  the  subject  group.  One  of  the  subjects suffered  with  Cerebral  Palsy.  Finally,  one  subject  with Down's Syndrome could not concentrate for a long enough period for data to be recorded, but through informal observation was seen to enjoy the Leap Motion game. Due to this, another child was added to the subject group to retain the planned  30  (the  above  ethical  consent  was  obtained). For each of the children, up to two minutes of data is recorded during the  interaction  with  games  for  each  of  the  three  states  of interest by the experimental setup shown in Fig. 8. For states of concentration, several of the subjects were not recorded for the full two minutes due to issues arising with attention for the  duration  of  the  recording.  This  leads  to  a  slight  class imbalance of 820 data objects for concentrating and 830 for both relaxed and neutral data. Although this slight imbalance is  present,  it  was  noted  that  Precision  and  Recall  strongly supported the observed accuracy of the algorithms, detailed further in Section V. For  the neutral state, no stimuli were present. The data was recorded based on observation, if the subject were to lose concentration  on  the  task,  then  the  recording  would  be stopped. This was done in order to prevent contamination of the calibration data.

## V. PRELIMINARY RESULTS

At this stage, the results are related to the performance of classification  algorithms  given  the  EEG  data.  The  results from the single algorithm benchmarking experiment can be observed in Table I. Random Forest and Logistic Regression hyperparameters are manually optimized in Fig. 10 and Fig. 11. For the three performance measures, Logistic Regression seemed to be the best with an overall classification accuracy of  96.24%  given  three  classes: concentrated , relaxed and neutral . The Logistic Regression model took 130 seconds to execute  whereas  the  Random  Forest  model  took  only  19 seconds, and thus the reduction of 0.34% overall score may be worth the reduction in computational complexity in future when  the  algorithm  is  required  to  run  on  standard  office machines.  In Table II, it can be observed that  when combining  the  two  best  models,  a  higher  classification accuracy is achieved for the dataset. This is observed through both  two  voting  methods  and  stacking,  where  the  voting method achieves the highest classification accuracy for the dataset. It must be noted that the models are complex and take a  lengthy  amount  of  time,  around  10  to  30  minutes,  on  a high-end computer (Intel core i7@3.7GHz). When the two models  vote  on  class  both  by  average  and  maximum probability, scores of 97% are achieved.

## VI. DISCUSSION AND FUTURE WORK

In this work, we successfully benchmarked various classical machine learning techniques on the dataset acquired in  primary  schools.  In  future,  we  plan  on  benchmarking  a deep  neural  network  through  the  same  technique  as  [16]. Additionally, the complexity of the model was high due to the consideration  of  988  numerical  features,  in  future  we  will attempt to reduce the complexity of the dataset by performing and benchmarking dimensionality reduction algorithms. This will  aim to improve the speed of classification without the loss of accuracy and will aid in real-time classification.

Through the derivation of a less complex model which can classify in real time, education can then be customized for the individual based on brain activity. The educational game will consider the predictions by the model as additional input to the  child's  physical  activity,  allowing  for  the  activities  to react in real time to a drop-in concentration, in order to raise the concentration level and improve the educational experience.

We will also use our educational framework as a long-term form of cognitive behavioral therapy for certain conditions. To do this, we plan on repeating the experiment in this study for  a  group  of  children  who  do  not  suffer  with  intellectual disability  and  generating  a  signature  to  describe  the  wave activity (see Fig. 2). Over a period of months of using our proposed framework, the children with intellectual disabilities will periodically have a similarity measure generated  between  their  own  signature  and  the  signature generated  from  the  non-intellectually  disabled.  The  goal therein is to aim to increase the similarity metric over time through engaging educational activities, with real-time classification guiding the quality of individual sessions.

The  planned  activities  outlined  in  this  section  are  now enabled due to the strong classification ability of the models derived from this study which pave the way for more in-depth studies and the development of the educational framework.

In  regard  to  evaluation  of  usability  and  acceptance  of children with our developed games, where they have to wear the headband for BCI and also interact with a NUI via LMC, we applied  a  questionnaire  to  record  the  opinions  of  users after the experiments were all complete as shown below:

For a) 83.3% of the responses stated it was as easy; 10% as medium and 6.7% difficult; b) 86.7% of the children pointed that the leap motion controller is easier to use than mouse/pad; c) 83.3% of children chose the aircraft game as their favorite and the other 16.7% is split into the other games; d) 93.3% of the children answered no, it is not difficult to wear the EEG sensor;  e)  96.7%  of  the  children  answered  yes;  f)  100% agreed;  g)  83.3%  answered  the  NUI  (using  the  hand  to control the game); h) in this question, it is interesting to point out that 53.3% mentioned that after long time playing with leap motion, sometimes they got tired to keep the hand up to control the aircraft; i) 100% yes.

It is important to mention here that all children, with their disabilities,  some  of  them  even  with  motor  disability,  got well-engaged with the game interfaces. Most of them wanted to continue playing after their sessions. The educational staff observed and pointed out that even the most difficult students in terms of attention deficit (specifically ODD) were focused on the games interface. They noticed that with only a few instructions  the  children  were  able  to  play  the  games,  and through intuition they realized how to solve specific problems,  by  observing  us  (instructors  demonstrating  only once how to play in a short of period of time). Since most of the  children  in  these  schools  we  visited  were  children belonging to low-income families, some of them had never played a similar type of game before. Most of them showed high anxiety to participate on these experiments, since they were told a few days before the experiments, and they were very motivated and anxious waiting for the day.

The  next  step  is  to  check  the  confidence  given  by  the concentration level classification to trigger the game to adapt to the user and change the stimuli in case the concentration level  drops  below  a  certain  threshold.  This  functionality  is implemented, waiting for the real-time classification of the EEG data, that is currently being developed. Following this step, we will implement the approach mentioned in Fig. 2, as a final version of our artificially intelligent game to monitor the  progress  of  children  over  time  at  schools.  With  all validation done so far, we can affirm that this approach has potential  to  have  strong  societal  impact  in  children  with attention deficit towards improving their cognitive abilities.

## VII. CONCLUSION

This work introduces a new approach for neurocognitive training towards improving children's cognition and concentration by using intelligent games. We combined BCI to measure concentration levels and engagement of children during the interaction with the game and a NUI controlled by hand  gestures.  Data  acquisition  of  brainwave  patterns  of different groups of children with disability or disorder (e.g. ID, ODD, ASD, ADHD) were acquired, so that we can check their patterns of concentration level when performing multiple  tasks.  Preliminary  results  on  children  brainwaves classification  of  concentration  show  that  our  framework achieved 96% accuracy. Questionnaires pointed out positive feedback on the use of the game and acceptance of children to this technology in this kind of intervention. Future work will address our system fully integrated, enabling the game ability to learn the user concentration pattern and make decisions to generate new  stimuli when  the user's attention drops, effectively enabling the real-time individual personalization of education.

Abstract. To foster inclusive education, accessible educational materials must be tailored to meet the diverse needs of students, especially students with disabilities. Images in educational materials play an important role in understanding textbook exercises but can also distract such students and make them more challenging. Therefore, we need an adaptive system that identifies non-essential images to reduce cognitive load and distractions for students with disabilities. Accordingly, this work proposes a computational framework to categorize textbook exercise images, facilitating their inclusion in accessible textbooks and enhancing learning for visually impaired and neurodevelopmental disorders students. Using three French textbook exercise datasets of 652 (text, image) pairs, we compared monomodal (text-only) and multimodal (text and image) classification approaches. We found that text-based models, particularly CamemBERT, excel in classifying images, achieving an accuracy rate of 85.25% on French text data. We also use Local Interpretable Modelagnostic Explanation for model interpretability and conduct qualitative analyses to deepen insights into model performance. This work is only a very first step towards an automatic translation of inclusive textbooks. Moreover, this paper revealed that this first step is already very challenging. We hope to draw more researchers' attention to this problem.

## 1 Introduction

As per the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, inclusive education asserts that it is the right of every student, not merely a privilege [21, 13], underscoring the importance of ensuring that educational opportunities are accessible to all individuals, irrespective of their abilities or disabilities [19]. The right to education is universal, transcending limitations imposed by physical or cognitive disabilities. However, conventional educational resources, mainly textbooks, are not inherently designed to cater to the diverse needs of all learners, especially those with disabilities [19, 33]. Inclusive education addresses this disparity by ensuring that educational materials are accessible and accommodating to all students, regardless of their disabilities.

Students benefiting from inclusive education include those with visual impairments and Neuro-Developmental Disorders (NDDs). Visual impairments range from difficulties with images, blurry vision, and partial sight to complete blindness, while NDDs affect brain function, impacting physical, social, academic, and occupational functioning [22]. Common NDDs in childhood include Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), Autism Spectrum Disorders (ASD), and Developmental Coordination Disorder (DCD) [5, 6]. Children with visual impairment and NDDs often face academic challenges that do not reflect their true abilities due to visual and fine motor skill difficulties [18, 2]. Similarly, visually impaired students encounter obstacles in accessing irrelevant visual materials and materials without suitable adaptations. Therefore, classrooms should consider these students' visual, reading, and gaze coordination difficulties when utilizing textbooks to promote inclusive education.

Recognizing their needs lays the foundation for building a computational system that automatically adapts textbooks, addressing reading, motor coordination, and attention difficulties to foster inclusivity in classrooms by tailoring educational materials for children with disabilities. Some non-profit organizations, such as Le Cartable Fantastique [16], have started creating adapted digital textbooks for children with DCD. However, manually transforming materials poses challenges due to diverse textbooks, frequent updates, and resource-intensive processes. The optimal solution would be for publishers to create inherently tailored textbooks for pupils with disabilities, which is currently unattainable. Therefore, automating textbook adaptation using Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine Learning (ML) is essential for improving accessibility.

A significant challenge in creating tailored textbooks is the manual annotation of images for diverse students. Professional educators and annotators currently spend considerable time and effort to classify images in textbooks, a process that is both labour-intensive and prone to errors [16]. This highlights the importance of automating image classification to ensure consistency and efficiency. Textbook exercise image classification is crucial to address this challenge, especially for the visually impaired and NDD students. By accurately identifying and categorizing images into different classes, we can remove those that are not required to understand the text. This approach ensures that essential visual information is preserved while managing non-essential images to reduce cognitive load and distractions. To address this, we propose a novel image classification framework for textbook exercise images. This framework categorizes images into Essential , Informative , and Decorative classes with expert help. It aids textbook adaptation by determining image inclusion in the user interface and allowing for customization based on user needs and preferences. Images can remain unaltered, be placed at the end of exercises, or be substituted with alternative text. Excluding Decorative images ensures document clarity and accessibility [28], particularly for students with distraction disabilities like NDD.

The main contributions of this work are: (1) the development of a novel computational framework for classifying textbook illustrations into distinct categories; (2) an empirical evaluation of monomodal and multimodal approaches for classifying (text, image) pairs; and (3) a thorough interpretability analysis using Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) [27].

## 2 Related Work

The work presented in this article is related to different fields: Natural Language Processing (NLP) applied to textbooks and text-image similarity and interaction. Limited research exists on NLP applied to textbooks, with some studies focusing on question generation and interactive content integration [3, 7, 1]. Interest in adaptive textbooks is increasing, yet manual concept indexing remains challenging. Recent advancements introduce ML methods like FACE [4] for automatic concept extraction, enhancing adaptive textbook technologies. The proliferation of MOOCs presents challenges with unstructured data, but ML frameworks for concept extraction show promise in addressing these issues [11]. Moreover, the research explores ML methods to automate the labour-intensive process of labelling educational data, particularly in Japanese schools [32]. While educational image classification primarily focuses on image type identification, recent work like [20] introduces datasets for illustration classification and [31] summarizes progress in chart classification. Additionally, [8] proposes a novel semi-supervised image classification method using curriculum learning, MMCL, outperforming five state-of-the-art methods on eight image datasets.

Similar to our goal of adapting textbooks for children with disabilities, recent studies have focused on modelling and extracting content from textbooks [16] or classifying exercises based on their adaptation for children with DCD [15]. However, these works focus on layout and textual content, not the textbook's images. To our knowledge, there is a gap in research regarding image classification for textbook adaptation to promote inclusivity. Two recent papers explore the relationship between text and image in image-text retrieval and classification [25, 23]. In [23], authors present a classification framework analyzing semantic relationships between images and textual descriptions, defining eight classes based on cross-modal mutual information, semantic correlation, and status metrics. While they utilize publicly available datasets, they mainly comprise single-sentence captions or labels, differing from the context-rich text in our exercises. Several efforts, such as the Web Form Accessibility Framework for the Visually Impaired (WAFI) [9], have been made to improve website accessibility for visually impaired individuals. W3C Web Accessibility Initiative 1 provides guidelines for replacing images with alternative text on web pages. Although not designed for educational contexts, these guidelines can be adapted to ensure essential visual information is retained, enhancing comprehension for all.

No prior research explores the relationship between images and text in educational materials. While strides have been made in adapting textbooks for children with disabilities, a gap remains in understanding how images contribute to inclusive learning. Existing studies focus on layout, textual content, and semantic relationships between images and text but often overlook the nuanced interaction between images and more extensive textual content in educational exercises. To address these challenges, we propose a computational framework for multimodal classification of textbook illustrations for adaptive learning environments.

## 3 Problem and Data Challenges

Images in textbooks serve diverse roles, making the complex task of annotating them essential for automated adaptation to meet diverse needs. This process involves classifying images into categories such as Essential , Informative , or Decorative , based on their educational roles. Differentiating between Essential and Informative images involves understanding their educational value and context. An Informative image is not crucial for completing an activity but serves an informative purpose, such as providing clues for solving an exercise or depicting a concept unfamiliar to students. The Essential and Informative categories aim to provide subtle adaptations for diverse visual impairments, ensuring optimal accessibility to educational materials.

The challenge also lies in the variability of content and layouts across textbooks, which makes standardization difficult. Data challenges include dealing with inconsistent image quality and varying levels of detail, which complicate automated classification. Accurate manual annotation requires significant expertise for consistent classification but is time-consuming and resource-intensive, especially for large datasets. This process presents scalability issues and cannot efficiently handle high volumes of images. Variability in image types and content further complicates manual annotation, necessitating adaptable methods. Ensuring consistent quality across datasets is challenging due to potential errors and inconsistencies. Automated systems can address these issues by providing initial classifications that experts can review, thereby enhancing efficiency and consistency. Automation is crucial for managing large datasets and improving accessibility, as ML algorithms can classify images based on visual and contextual features, reducing manual effort and supporting the creation of inclusive educational materials.

## 5 Approaches

For the textbook illustration classification, we utilized various modalities, including both multimodal approaches, which integrate images and text, and monomodal approaches that consider text or images independently. The workflow for the final approaches used in this work is shown in Fig. 1.

## 5.1 Multimodal Approaches

For the multimodal approach, we used the CLIP model [26], which excels in bridging the semantic gap between images and textual descriptions through zeroshot transfer, natural language supervision, and multimodal learning. Our text data is extracted from French textbooks, so we translated the text into English using the open-source offline translation library Argos Translate 4 with Open- NMT [14] to align with CLIP's requirement for English text input. We chose the RN101 variant in CLIP, which performed better on our dataset. This variant has a 512-dimensional embedding, processes 224 × 224 images with a backbone of (3, 4, 23, 3) layers, and includes a text transformer with 12 heads, 512 width, and eight layers, enhancing multimodal comprehension.

4 https://github.com/argosopentech/argos-translate

Fig. 1: Workflow for Multimodal Textbook Illustration Classification



We utilized the CLIP model to compute the cosine similarity between images and texts, establishing their relationship. For example, the cosine similarity between text and image features in Table 1 is 0.42, 0.48, and 0.41 for the Essential , Informative , and Decorative classes. Since some of our textual data exceeded CLIP's default token length of 77, we used truncation and segmentation for text data. Truncation shortens texts to the default length, while segmentation divides texts into segments of the default length. We calculated average, maximum, and truncated cosine similarity scores for text-image pairs. Descriptive statistics reveal that the Essential class has the highest mean average similarity score (0.42), slightly above Decorative (0.419), with Informative showing lower central tendencies. For maximum similarity scores, Informative leads with a mean of 0.438, followed by Decorative (0.431) and Essential (0.422). The Informative class also has the highest truncated similarity score (0.433). Despite Informative showing higher scores, the close mean values across classes indicate that CLIP-based similarity alone may not fully capture distinctions. To improve classification, we used the RN101 variant of CLIP to extract 512-size feature vectors and applied a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) as shown in Fig. 1.

## 5.2 Monomodal Approaches

Text-based features encoding: We strategically used domain-specific language models for text feature extraction: BERT for English text [12] and CamemBERT for its French counterpart [17]. These state-of-the-art language models provided comprehensive text understanding and nuanced insights into linguistic complexity. We followed the same procedure for classification as we did for our multimodal approach. We extracted features using bert-base-uncased for English and camembert-base for French. Larger models diminished performance, likely due to overfitting and increased computational demands. We then tokenized English text using bert-base-uncased and French text using CamembertTokenizer , processed tokens through all layers of BERT and CamemBERT, and extracted features from the last hidden layer. Adaptive average pooling was used across the sequence length dimension for fixed-size representation. The textual embeddings obtained through these models are then fed to a MLP for classification.

To further enhance our exercise representation, we follow the work of [15] and fine-tune CamemBERT's language model on educational data: lessons and activities from four textbooks (two textbooks from the collection used for training, excluding the exercises used to build our dataset, and two other unseen textbooks), 1293 Fantastiques Exercices provided by the organization Le Cartable Fantastique , and the 79 original reading texts from Alector .

Image-based features encoding: For Image feature extraction, we used CNNbased models, specifically ResNet [10], VGG16 [29], and Inception-v3 [30], renowned for their proficiency in image recognition tasks. We used pre-trained ResNet-50, VGG16, and Inception-v3 models, adjusting their final layers to produce 512dimensional feature vectors that matched CLIP's size. All layers, except the modified final one, are frozen to preserve the knowledge encoded in earlier layers. The images were pre-processed by resizing to ensure compatibility with the ResNet model's input expectations.

We extracted the features using these models, generating the feature vectors of dimensions N × 512 , with N representing the total number of data instances. Following the same method we used for the CLIP model, we extracted data using these models, ensuring uniformity in our approach. Subsequently, we used MLP to train the extracted data, enhancing the analytical capabilities of our research.

## 5.3 Unbalanced Data Management

The initially processed data is highly imbalanced, as shown in Table 2, which can detrimentally affect model performance by favouring the majority class. To mitigate this issue, we used two strategies, either jointly or separately:

- -Class\_weight strategy: Weapplied the class\_weight strategy using scikitlearn [24] to prioritize the minority class. The 'balanced' strategy dynamically adjusts class weights based on their distribution in the training data, giving higher weights to underrepresented classes.
- -Data generation: We augment the initial training set with 150 instances from the Decorative class, consisting of text-only data and random images from textbooks. We then merge this augmented data with the original set, extract features, and apply the same procedure as previous models, passing it through the MLP.

## 6 Result and Discussions

## 6.1 Experimental Setups and Ablation Study

Our MLP has an input layer of size 512, two hidden layers with 256 and 128 neurons, respectively, and an output layer for 3 classes, with ReLU activations in between. It was trained using CrossEntropyLoss and Adam optimizer, with a batch size of 32 over 30 epochs. Based on validation performance, early stopping with patience of 5 epochs was applied. As part of our ablation study, we experimented with different numbers of hidden layers in the MLP: 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 hidden layers. The results indicated that the MLP with 2 hidden layers achieved the best performance.

We also conducted an ablation study on fusion methods to evaluate their impact on model performance, using both Early fusion and Late fusion of text and image modalities. In Early fusion, we combined modalities by computing the maximum, minimum, and concatenation of two features. In Late fusion, we applied the same MLP used previously to the extracted data of both modalities, averaging and taking the weighted average of both outputs to predict the result. The best accuracy result obtained from these methods on the test data was 57.14%, which was not as good as the performance of the Early fusion approach. Therefore, we generated all results using the Early fusion concatenation method. We chose concatenation because it preserves information from both modalities, enriching the feature space and capturing complementary data. The concatenated features, sized 1024, were then input into an MLP.

## 6.3 Qualitative Analysis and Explainability

Table 5 compares results obtained with text-based, image-based, or multimodal models on three random (text, image) pairs. For the given exercises, the textbased model incorrectly labels Exercise 1, related to image description , while the image-based model performs well in both Exercise 1 and Exercise 2, i.e., in image description and image reading but fails to classify Exercise 3. Notably, images are required for all exercises. The cosine similarities for Exercises 1, 2, and 3 are 0.403, 0.456, and 0.422, respectively, reflecting the varying degrees of alignment between text and image features. The text-based model's misclassification of Exercise 1 highlights its limitation in understanding the role of visual content in specific tasks. The multimodal model struggles with all exercises, reflecting difficulties in effectively integrating text and image modalities.

We use LIME [27] to enhance model interpretability for text classification. It provides localized explanations for the predictions, improving transparency. In Table 4, CamemBERT, incorporating CW and DA, achieved the best results, prompting us to perform LIME analysis on our French text data. Fig. 3 illustrates the top 10 words for each class, with their importance scores, where the Y-axis displays the top 10 contributing words and the X-axis shows their weights.

For the Essential class, Family names and firstnames like "Zélie", "Bastide", and "Antoine" appear in the top features. Besides, the feature "i", which is the number 1 in Roman letters, indicates that these texts are closely related to key educational elements like multiple-choice questions. Interestingly, for the Informative class, all top features-"art" (art), "miroir" (mirror), "terre" (earth), "princesse" (princess), "Fourmis" (ants), "terminaison" (ending), "Courgette" (zucchini), "Baleine" (whale), and "Farel" (a proper noun, likely a name) are nouns, with the exception of "1881", which is a number. Unlike the other two classes, no verbs are present in this class. Finally, it seems that the Decorative class contains more grammatical words like "ils" (they), "comme" (like), "ai" (have), and "on" (we) compared to the other two classes and also words related to grammar exercises ("adjectif" (adjective), "pluriel" (plural)).

Overall, the LIME analysis on CamemBERT reveals how the model differentiates between content in children's textbooks. Higher weights for the Informative class indicate the model's focus on contextually significant terms, while lower weights for the Decorative highlight its ability to identify less critical features. These insights are specific to the classification task, and domain expertise remains essential to fully understand the model's decision-making process.

In addition to the promising results, it is essential to acknowledge the limitations of our study. We recognize the dataset's limited size, which may affect the findings. However, given the novel nature of this work, we focused on assessing the feasibility of our methods. Encouraged by the positive outcomes, we plan to expand and validate our approach using larger datasets in future research. Additionally, due to the presence of private data in our dataset, we cannot share it with the community, hindering the reproducibility of our experiments. We are working on annotating publicly available data to share with the community.

## 7 Conclusion

Our study proposes an automatic system to classify textbook images into Essential, Informative , and Decorative classes, aiding inclusive education for visually impaired and NDD students. Using a French textbook dataset of 652 (text, image) pairs, we found that text-based models, particularly CamemBERT on French text data, outperformed multimodal methods. Further, the LIME analysis revealed that for Essential class, the family names and first names "Zélie" and "Bastide" appear in top features, as well as "i", likely denoting the numeral for multiple-choice questions. Interestingly, for the Informative class, we can see that only nouns are accounted as top features. Finally, it seems that the Decorative class contains more grammatical words and those related to grammar exercises. This approach ensures that only images with educational content are retained. In the future, we plan to expand the dataset, incorporating additional sources such as [23] and exploring alternative similarity measures. We aim to explore alternative text generation to enhance textbook adaptation for visually impaired and NDD students. Additionally, future work could introduce a new dataset of images paired with contextual descriptions for the visually impaired, which may be shared with the community.

## WLA4ND: a Wearable Dataset of Learning Activities for Young Adults with Neurodiversity to Provide Support in Education

Hui Zheng Click Therapeutics New York, USA hzheng5@gmu.edu

Huzefa Rangwala George Mason University Fairfax, VA, USA rangwala@gmu.edu

## ABSTRACT

Data-driven assistive wearable technologies are promising to support young adults with neurodiversity in inclusive education. Existing datasets focus on wearable sensor data of various activities from neurotypical people. However, no dataset exists including learning-related activity data from individuals with neurodiversity. The contributions of this paper include (1) WLA4ND, a dataset of learning activities performed by eight young adults with neurodiversity collected from smartwatch sensors. The activities are common learning tasks, including reading, writing, typing, answering follow-up questions, and off-task. (2) Evaluation of classification on WLA4ND with five activity recognition models, including conventional and deep learning methods. The Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) model achieved a balanced accuracy of 92.2% for user-dependent evaluations, while Federated MultiTask Hierarchical Attention Model (FATHOM) achieved 91.8% for user-independent evaluations. This evaluation demonstrates that existing activity recognition technologies can be applied to neurodiverse populations. Also, WLA4ND can be used by researchers as a complement for activity recognition, automatic labeling, and next-generation assistive wearable applications.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Neurodiverse conditions consist of variations in the abilities of individuals in cognitive, motor, and communication skills [19, 56, 57]. Neurodiverse conditions also impact the individual's behaviors and memory, which may impair their executive functions [27, 41]. Approximately 17% of the children in the United States present neurodiverse conditions [18]. Those conditions vary in severity level per individual [40], however they are lifelong and chronic. They also affect individuals' opportunities for employment, education, and independent living. Conversely, neurodiverse conditions have been associated with positive traits as well, including pattern recognition, hyperfocus, and creativity [1]. Because the common traits in neurodiversity affect the individual autonomy in daily activities, support from a caregiver or an assistant is often required [6, 72]. In-person monitoring and interventions by caregivers, aides, or assistants are effective. However, they are limited regarding consistency, accountability, and scalability [72]. Moreover, verbal interventions to moderate behavior may stigmatize and cause discomfort to a neurodiverse individual by explicitly signaling a disability [43]. Verbal interventions may also be error-prone and subjective due to the assistants' fatigue or bias [60].

To facilitate access and improve opportunities to inclusive higher education programs for young adults with neurodiversity, in 2008 the Higher Education Opportunity Act (HEOA) was introduced in the United States. Despite HEOA increasing the education opportunities for adults with neurodiversity, the variations on cognitive skills and adaptive functioning still affect multiple aspects of their lives, such as opportunities for employment, independent living, and engagement in traditional learning activities [47]. Conventionally, in inclusive settings of special/inclusive education programs, support staff is hired to provide continuous assistance for students with neurodiversity. Nonetheless, the support staff can not always be near the students with neurodiversity, for instance, when the students are off-campus or taking online classes. This challenge became particularly important during the COVID-19 pandemic since most academic courses switched to online learning environments, and personal support staff is absent or remote when students with neurodiversity are completing their learning activities.

To fully realize the potential wearable applications to facilitate assistance, it is essential to have a dataset of users' activities subject to monitoring and intervention. For validity, such data has to be collected from users with neurodiversity, ensuring that activity recognition models match end users' profiles [70]. Given the unique abilities and motor skills of neurodiverse users, a learning activity dataset of individuals with neurodiversity collected from wearable sensors complements datasets of activity recognition for neurotypical people. A dataset for neurodiverse users facilitates assistive wearable technologies driven by data and machine learning models, enabling monitoring of students as well as the delivery of less intrusive interventions without requiring in-person assistance. Datasets and activity recognition models pave the way to provide remote support for students with neurodiversity.

To address such shortcomings, there is a promising potential to further explore ubiquitous computing, including mobile and wearable applications that deliver timely interventions and reminders on demand. The possible options for personalization and automation make assistive wearables suitable to meet the needs of users with neurodiversity, especially individuals who need continuous assistance with executive functioning [16, 60]. Specifically, the monitoring of physiological and motion data from wearable sensors yields promising opportunities for wearable assistive technologies [13]. With the data collected from wearable sensors, assistive wearable applications have a great potential to monitor users' activities and provide strategic interventions. Additionally, the involvement of users with neurodiversity is important in the iterative design process to identify requirements and to design suitable assistive applications [20, 56, 58]. Assistive wearable applications can help neurodiverse users with self-regulation, monitoring their behaviors to inform them and raise their awareness, and also to mediate the assistance they receive from caregivers [43]. Although assistive wearables are well-suited to provide support, no inclusive dataset of learning activities involving neurodiverse users exist.

This paper presents WLA4ND, a wearable sensor dataset of learning activities. We designed four learning activities which were performed by eight participants, resulting in a total of 32 learning sessions. The eight young adults who participated in the study are neurodiverse, including conditions such as Autism Spectrum Disorders (ASD), Attention Deficit and Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD), and Intellectual and Developmental Disability (IDD). The learning activities are hand activities (reading with follow-up questions, writing, and typing) which are commonly performed in inclusive education. We used built-in smartwatch sensors (accelerometer, gyroscope, and heart rate sensors) to collect data. The dataset was carefully annotated by three coders following a multi-label approach. The dataset includes six ground truth labels: (1) Read, (2) Write Q&amp;A, (3) Write, (4) Type, (5) Off-task, and (6) Rest. Furthermore, we investigated five state-of-the-art classification approaches and evaluated them on this novel WLA4ND dataset, to explore whether and to what extent the existing activity recognition technologies suit data collected from individuals with neurodiversity.

The contributions of this paper include: (1) WLA4ND, a novel dataset of learning activities performed by young adults with neurodiversity collected via wearable sensors; (2) investigation of classification-based activity recognition models evaluated on this novel WLA4ND dataset. Having high accuracy on classification tasks from activity recognition models accelerates the automation of assistance, creating promising opportunities for applications of AI-based assistive wearable technologies in inclusive education. Ultimately, such contributions will lead to improvement in the quality of life for individuals with neurodiversity.

## 2 RELATED WORK

Assistive technologies (ATs) have great potential to meet users' needs, monitoring their activities and providing them with support on demand. Assistance can be delivered through ubiquitous, mobile, and wearable technologies, and as such reducing potential stigmas, promoting and improving individuals' independence [15]. ATs can augment, increase, maintain, or improve the functional capabilities of persons with disabilities. This type of support is beneficial across user groups regardless of their specific needs. Still, prior work indicates that most assistive technologies that employ artificial intelligence (AI) (including applications of natural language processing [36] and computer vision [35]) are created for users with visual, hearing, speech, and motor impairments. Meanwhile, the ATs for users with neurodiversity are limited. Most technologies have also been developed for children [49]. To provide an overview of the latest research in this domain, in this section, we focus on three aspects: (1) ATs to assist people with neurodiversity in inclusive education and independent living, (2) wearable ATs developed to provide support for people with neurodiversity, and (3) wearable or mobile sensor datasets related to human activities.

## 2.1 Assistive Technologies for Neurodiverse Users in Inclusive Education and Independent Living

ATs designed for people with neurodiversity focus on providing educational support, enhancing communication strategies and skill development, and preparing individuals to live independently. Prior work about ATs include studies about learning games [7, 33], augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) technologies [23, 50, 68], playground equipment [69, 71], multimedia technologies [5], and virtual reality (VR) technologies [52].

With ATs focused on improving cognitive and academic skills, communication, and social interactions, researchers have also explored ATs to teach living skills to neurodiverse users. Video contents were created with a 360 ◦ camera allowing users to wear a head-mounted device to experience common activities of daily living and acquire related skills, such as shopping skills, commuting skills, and vocational skills [52]. Additionally, social media and video content were used to support young adults with IDD to further develop their social skills [5]. An interactive game including

For instance, interactive balls [69, 71], Kinect games [39], and tablet applications [68] were used to encourage social interaction and social bonds among children with ASD.

## 2.2 Assistive Wearables for Neurodiverse Users

Recently, researchers began to investigate how wearables assist neurodiverse users. However, most of the work used wearable ATs targeting children with neurodiversity. Prior work focused on assisting with socialization and communication for children with ASD, using head-mounted wearable devices including virtual reality (VR) [8] and augmented reality (AR) glasses [66], as well as body-worn devices such as headbands [25], wrist-worn bracelets [59], chest-worn bands [9], and interactive clothing pieces [31]. In addition to communication and socialization, researchers also aimed at supporting children in other aspects. Several ATs were designed for children with ADHD. For example, an assessment system used wearable sensors to diagnose ADHD [28]. A chest-worn band application was implemented to intervene in impulsive speaking in classes [53]. Moreover, a prototype of a smartwatch application was studied to explore the tensions and challenges related to children's self-regulation [13]. An assistive prototype that captured real-time activities aimed to help users to sustain their attention [55]. Furthermore, concerning head-mounted devices, researchers explored the usage of HoloLens for children with neurodiversity to learn basic daily tasks [2] and the usage of a VR system for storytelling for children with IDD [21].

Compared to wearable assistive applications for children, studies involving neurodiverse adults are limited. Previous work investigated how wrist-worn devices (smartwatch and personalized band) helped users to manage anxiety for adults with ADHD and ASD [16, 51]. Also, previous research explored the usage of augmented gloves to provide real-time feedback on work-related activities for employees with cognitive impairments [32]. Still, little is known about the potential opportunities for wearable ATs utilizing AI for young adults with neurodiversity. In inclusive education, it is unclear the potential of datasets to ensure training for machine learning models.

## 2.3 Wearable and Mobile Sensor Datasets of Human Activities

Wearables and mobile devices have been used extensively to recognize and monitor various human activities in diverse contexts [38]. Previous studies involving data capture in the wild [34, 62, 65] collected data related to fitness and social activities using wearable or mobile sensors.

To summarize, existing datasets focus mainly on daily wholebody activities, except for the work of Laput and Harrison (2019) whose focus is on hand activities [34]. Also, the datasets for primitive activity recognition focus on locomotion or sports activities [38]. No existing wearable dataset collected data from people with disabilities, which could potentially lead to bias in the trained machine learning models, excluding users with diverse needs [12, 61]. While there are several datasets collected with wearable devices, to the best of our knowledge, there are no hand-activity datasets on learning activities for young adults with neurodiversity. There is a missed opportunity to implement and use assistive wearables that leverage sensor data to harness information and provide individualized support for students with disabilities in inclusive education programs.

The dataset generated by Vaizman et al. (2018) tracked users' behavior in activities such as walking, sitting, and laying down, using self-reported context labels [62]. This dataset contributes to the knowledge of human behavior contexts in natural settings, laying a groundwork for in-the-wild data collection studies. Similarly, wearable sensor data was collected to identify hand activities in the study by Laput and Harrison (2019) [34]. Weiss et al. (2016) collected data of fitness activities and hand activities (e.g. eating) from smartwatches' and smartphones' accelerometers and gyroscopes. They compared smartwatch and smartphone-based activity recognition and showed that smartwatches could identify specialized hand activities more effectively than smartphones [67]. Shahmohammadi et al. (2017) used smartwatches to collect real-time data of fitness activities (standing, sitting, walking, and running) from embedded accelerometers and gyroscopes [48]. Then, they used active learning approaches for identifying samples that should be labeled by the user for boosting classification performance. Ni et al. (2019) released a workout dataset comprising sensor data (e.g., heart rate, speed) and contextual data (e.g., longitude, latitude, altitude). They also evaluated a sequential model for classification based on a recurrent neural network (RNN) [42]. Existing benchmarks and datasets represent data collected from users who were adults without any reported disability. ## 3 METHODS

To understand the learning behaviors of young adults with neurodiversity, we conducted a user study. The protocol for data collection, the session setup and duration, the smartwatch application implemented for data collection, and the data annotation strategy are thoroughly explained in the following subsections.

## 3.1 Data Collection

We collected data from the smartwatch built-in sensors regarding four learning activities. Each participant completed the same set of activities, including an easy-level reading with follow-up Q&amp;A, a medium-level reading with follow-up Q&amp;A, copying slides by typing, and prompt writing. The protocol of each session was defined by three special education experts. Each learning activity lasted at most 10 minutes per session, and there was a short break after each session to prevent fatigue. We focus on academic learning activities as these activities are executed across settings (e.g., employment, education, homework) where self-regulation is needed to improve focus and concentration. These activities frequently require the observation and intervention from aides in inclusive/special education programs.

According to the learning skills of the participants, the coordinator and the special education professor carefully selected materials for all four academic tasks. These included two-level reading and Q&amp;A materials from a book they commonly adopt in special education [29], four slides about mood assessment for the typing task, and a prompt writing topic.

These learning activities were selected after two brainstorming sessions with three special education investigators, including the coordinator of an inclusive higher education program (Mason LIFE) with 18 years of experience who is also a caregiver to a young adult with IDD, and two investigators from the same program with 14 years of special education experience assisting students with IDDs. The activities were decided after discussions involving also an HCI professor, and a PhD student in HCI, both with five years of research on assistive technologies for neurodiverse users. Study participants were recruited from Mason LIFE, an inclusive postsecondary education program that serves 54 students with IDD and employs 100 support staff members.

For the two-level reading with follow-up Q&amp;A sessions, the participants read the article assigned and then they wrote their answers to the questions posed, all related to the contents of the article read during the session. For these two sessions, the reading materials and the Q&amp;A writing materials were shown on paper sheets, as illustrated in the Figures 1a and 1b. For the Typing task, the participants typed using a laptop keyboard. As shown in Figure 1c, four slides about how to assess their mood were displayed on another monitor, placed in front of the participant. The participants copied the main text presented in each slide typing the contents. For the Prompt Writing session, the participants wrote an essay about a given topic on a piece of paper. The essay was related to approaches to deal with anxiety when missing a bus (Figure 1d).

3.1.1 Data Collection Procedure. Upon IRB approval, in the Fall 2019, we recruited nine participants with neurodiversity. The data from one participant was later discarded due to an incomplete reading task. For the recruitment of participants, the coordinator of Mason LIFE reached out to the alumni group by email or phone. Participation was voluntary.

Each of the eight participants performed the four learning tasks. In total, we conducted 32 learning sessions for the data collection. On average, the participants took approximately 11 minutes to complete an easy-level Reading with follow-up Q&amp;A, 9 minutes for typing tasks, 6 minutes for prompt writing, and 8 minutes for medium-level Reading with follow-up Q&amp;A. 3.1.2 Smartwatch Applications for Data Collection. Given customization requirements to implement the app and budget considerations, we chose Wear OS as the platform for the development and Android devices to build our data-collection application. Android watches are more affordable than Apple watches and also more flexible for customized implementations.

The smartwatch application is designed to have two main functions. One is to record the sensor data, and another is to write the sensor data to CSV (comma-separated value) files, as shown in Figure 2. The smartwatch app has one toggle button for starting or pausing data collection, and one button for writing the sensor data collected to the CSV file. In the study session, the researcher helped the participant to wear the smartwatch and clicked the 'start' toggle button, after that the button switched to a 'pause' option and the screen turned to ambient mode. After the data collection session, the researcher running the experiment selected the 'pause' toggle-button, and then, clicked on the 'write' button to append the data of that session to the corresponding CSV file. Specifically, the 'write' function writes the data from different sensors to separate CSV files. Each data point was written as a row in the CSV file with timestamps in milliseconds and Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) zone. To facilitate the synchronization of timestamps between the video recordings and the data collected from the smartwatch sensors, we also built a mobile application that recorded the starting and ending timestamps in UTC per session in a log file.

We designed and built an Android application on Google Wear OS smartwatch (Fossil Gen 5 Smartwatch) to collect data from built-in sensors including accelerometer, gyroscope, and heart rate sensors. For all the learning tasks, we used data collected from all these sensors as diverse cues for feature representation. To clarify, because the learning activities we collected (i.e., reading and Q&amp;A, writing, and typing) are all hand activities, we intentionally set the sample rate to be high via Android Sensor API. The thread of our watch application collects data from five sensors at a rate of 100Hz per sensor, including raw accelerometer, raw gyroscope, linear acceleration, gravity, and heart rate. The raw accelerometer, linear acceleration, and gravity sensors measure the corresponding acceleration vectors, and the raw gyroscope measures rotation vectors. Besides motion sensors, heart rate sensor measures heartbeats per minute (bpm).

## 3.2 Data Annotation

To annotate the data collected, we relied on observing attentively the video recordings and the data from CSV files generated from the smartwatch application. CSV files include sensor data with timestamps in each row. Besides watching the video recordings, the hand-written field notes and logs from the mobile app were used as complementary materials, mainly to identify the start and end time of each session in order to help the time synchronization between wearable data in the CSV format and the video files. We also carefully discarded the irrelevant parts of the data samples, i.e. we trimmed values collected before and after each activity. For the time synchronization, we used the unified timestamps in UTC from both videos and smartwatch sensor data. To clarify, the sensor data was logged in CSV files with timestamps in UTC. Also, each video has timestamps in UTC.

To increase internal reliability, three coders (research assistants) manually annotated the data following a pre-defined agreement on the labeling scheme. They first carefully watched the video recordings and identified six labels (Read, Write, Write Q&amp;A, Type, Off-task, Rest) for each activity based on the participants' behaviors. The labels are described as follows.

- Read: Participants were focused on reading the texts on a pieces of paper.
- Type: Participants typed on the laptop keyboard.
- Write Q&amp;A: Participants read one question and wrote an answer to it in order. This label is only used in two Reading and Q&amp;A sessions.
- Write: Participants wrote on a piece of paper.
- Rest: Participants took a rest during the break after each learning session.
- Off-task: Participants lost their attention and did not perform a given learning task for over 2 seconds.

For the detailed procedure, three coders individually watched and annotated the video recordings by identifying the activities related to the labels listed per session. We observed the change of the learning activities and carefully located the moment when the change of learning behaviors occurred. We identified the activities and located the beginning time and ending time of each activity based on the observed behavior of the participant. Per activity (with a single label), we labeled every second in between the starting and ending time with the corresponding label. For instance, for the Typing session, we identified Type and Off-task. For the Prompt Writing session, we had two activity labels which are Write and Off-task. For the two-level reading and follow-up Q&amp;A sessions, we extracted three kinds of activities including Read, Write Q&amp;A, and Off-task. For the break after each session, we used Rest as a single label. Off-task label is assigned whenever participants lost their focus for more than two seconds in any particular learning task.

For reliability of agreement among three coders' labelling, we calculated the Fleiss' kappa reliability of agreement [37]. For details, we treated each second as a case, three raters, and six categories. For Fleiss' kappa , the κ is 98.79%, which indicates high agreement level of the annotation.

This labelling process was continuously performed by three coders to cross-validate the annotated instances for each second. Then, the three coders met to finalize the activity label for each video second and assigned the same label to the corresponding sensor data points at the same timestamp in the CSV files. If more than two coders agreed with one instance, then the labelling of that instance was finalized. If no agreement was reached, the three coders re-watched the corresponding frames of the video to discuss and finalize the labelling of that instance.

## 4 WLA4ND DATASET

The WLA4ND dataset consists of data collected with a set of wearable sensors, representing learning activities from eight participants with neurodiversity. Among the eight participants, there are three females and five males (according to self-reports), all were alumni from Mason LIFE, the inclusive postsecondary education program for students with intellectual and developmental disabilities. The participants self-reported their disability types. Regarding their demographic profile, seven participants were White-Caucasian, and one was African-American. All eight participants used their right hand as dominant hand. The average age of the participants was 28.5 years old, ranging from 23 to 31 years old. Table 2 lists the details of demographic profile of the eight participants.

Because the difference in hand actions among these learning activities is relatively small, we collected heart rate sensor data to provide more cues apart from motion sensors. For example, 'Rest' and 'Off-task' activities are hard to be distinguished by hand actions. For 'Off-task', the mean beats per minute (bpm) is 63.54 (std = 11.91). For 'Rest', the mean bpm is 73.65 (std = 13.68). However, they are not significantly different by t-test.

The data was collected from five smartwatch built-in sensors, including 13 dimensions of features, being 12 dimensions of features from four motion sensors and one feature from the heart rate sensor. For those four motion sensors that measure vectors in three axes (x, y, z) relative to the smartwatch, we included data from the gyroscope (raw rotations), accelerometer (raw accelerations), linear acceleration (accelerations excluding gravity), and gravity sensors to measure the corresponding acceleration vectors. Heart rate sensors were used to measure the number of beats per minute (bpm) in one vector. For the raw data, around 500 raw time points in total were collected from five sensor sources per second.

The raw sensor signals were pre-processed. The raw time series was chunked by fixed-width sliding windows of 100 data points per window with 50% overlap [3], to generate higher-level feature presentations over this period. Within each sliding window, for each vector per sensor, five metrics were calculated, i.e., the mean, standard deviation, median, 25% percentile, and 75% percentile. These calculations result in 65 features in total. These 65 higherlevel features include 60 features (5*3*4) containing five features per axial (x, y, z) dimension from four motion sensor sources, and five features (5*1) from the heart rate sensor collecting one dimension bpm vector. All preprocessing steps were executed using Python scripts. The dataset contains six categories (classes) of labels related to learning activities, which are Read, Write, WriteQ&amp;A, Type, Offtask, and Rest. Considering that all participants read, wrote, typed, and rested while seated, all of these activities are hand activities.

The WLA4ND Dataset resulted in eight CSV files (one CSV file per participant). Each row of a file has 65 dimensions of features and six columns of six labels in the format of one-hot encoding [26], i.e., a vector representation where its value is 1 and all the remaining elements of the vector are 0. The dataset and the related classification code are publicly available at https://github.com/huizhengfrances/WLA4ND.

In terms of labels, the pre-processed data includes a total of data points: 3,688 for Read; 2,410 for Write; 1,585 for Write Q&amp;A; 3,533 for Type; 2,385 for Rest; and 610 for Off-task. The total number of data points is 14,211. This results in 9 data points per second.

## 5 CLASSIFICATION ON WLA4ND DATASET

The WLA4ND dataset collected from neurodiverse adults comprises learning activities which can be used to develop and train machine learning recognition models. Within the educational setting, such models offer promising support for young adults with neurodiversity in learning activities.

Currently, assistance and interventions are manual. However, this approach can be inconsistent, stigmatizing, and patronizing. With an automated and less intrusive way, assistive wearables relying on activity recognition models can provide discreet interventions on demand. For instance, when an Off-task behavior is detected, the application can gently remind users to take a break. Thus, they can regain focus after a long period of learning activities. Also, the application equipped with automated models could praise users, delivering positive rewards when they concentrate on a task and sustain engagement.

Given the potential benefits of assistive wearable technologies based on the classification of learning activities, we developed and evaluated five classification models. The models are based on time-series data collected with wearable sensors. We adapted state-of-the-art classification models for time-series data using conventional and deep learning models. We applied both conventional and deep learning activity recognition models including Logistic Regression (LR), Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP), Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) [14] model, Single-layer LSTM (S-LSTM) model, and a Multi-task Hierarchical Attention LSTM (FATHOM) model [11]. The details of the five classification models can be found in Section 5.1. We evaluated these models for Userdependent and User-independent evaluation, as explained in the next subsection.

## 5.1 Model Summary

Classification Models. We used five state-of-the-art models for time series data. We used Python as a programming language. To build the models, we used Keras neural network library, TensorFlow library, scikit-learn data analysis tool, matplotlib visualization tool, and NumPy library. For user-dependent training, we performed multi-task learning, with one learning task per one participant. Specifically, multiple learning tasks are solved at the same time for all models except for Logistic Regression (LR). For user-independent training, there is only one single task for all models.

- Logistic Regression (LR) . LR [46] is a conventional statistical analysis technique employed in machine learning. LR is commonly used across domains. When LR is employed for activity recognition of hand movement, each participant will be responsible for its data for both training and prediction models. LR did not use shared parameters among data of different participants.
- Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) . CRNN [14] is a multi-task learning model that uses Convolutional Neural Networks to extract short-term patterns in a temporal dimension to find local dependencies among features. In this framework, we fed the inputs to a CNN layer
- Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) (16,16) . MLP (16,16) [63] represents a multilayer perceptron model with two hidden layers in between the input layer and the output layer. In MLP (16,16), the hidden dimensions used for both hidden layers are 16. For multi-task learning, this model learns parameters from multiple participants jointly.

and replaced the Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) layer with a single LSTM layer containing 64 hidden cells.

- Single Long Short Term Memory (S-LSTM) . LSTM [22] is a technique for sequential data forecasting, known for its capability to capture long-term dependencies. In this model, we used a single layer LSTM. The dimension of the hidden cells is 64.
- Federated Multi-Task Hierarchical Attention Model (FATHOM) . FATHOM [11] is a deep learning model that uses attention models and LSTM for time series data. FATHOM uses a hierarchical attention model to learn a common representation from the data. The inter-feature correlations of each user are captured by user-specific attention layers. Meanwhile, the shared temporal correlations are extracted across all users in all time dimensions. Then, FATHOM uses LSTM networks to learn underlying temporal relationships to process the feature representations generated by attention layers. The model is also capable of key feature selections.

## 6 CLASSIFICATION EVALUATION

For training classification models, we used fixed parameters to generate training models across five models. We used 40 for epochs and 20 for batch size. For evaluation, we report five metrics which are F1 score, Precision, Recall, Accuracy, and the Balanced Accuracy (BA) [63] (designed for imbalanced numbers of labels).

## 6.1 User-Dependent Evaluation

For the user-dependent evaluation, the classification task was conducted per individual participant. Because the data are time-series data with temporal cues, we used temporal adjacent segments to split the time series data per participant into three parts for crossvalidation, including Training data (60%), Validation data (20%), and Test data (20%). The user-dependent classification results among five models are listed in Table 3. To clarify, for user-dependent training, we performed multi-task learning for all models except for LR. Specifically, we calculated the averaged accuracy, BA, F1 score, precision, and recall of the classifications for all participants.

We report that CRNN performs well overall, obtaining the highest measures for four metrics. CRNN shows a 92.2% averaged BA and 83.7% F1 score. FATHOM also performed well with BA (86.0%) and F1 (79.4%). MLP(16,16) also performed relatively well. However, the independently trained LR did not perform as well as the other models. From these results, both deep (e.g. CRNN, FATHOM) and shallow (e.g. MLP(16,16)) neural network models performed better than the regression model (LR). CRNN, FATHOM, and MLP(16,16) outperformed LR in terms of BA by 25.7%, 19.5%, and 16.6% respectively. Figure 3a shows the confusion matrix of the CRNN model. By analyzing the confusion matrix, we noted that the main issue was that the 'WriteQA' samples were misclassified as 'Read' samples. The reason may be because 'WriteQA' is a compound activity that involves reading questions and writing the answers.

Specifically, the attention mechanisms of FATHOM weigh related features corresponding to the sequences of different activities. Based on attention weight matrices of FATHOM, Figure 4 shows the visualization of the attention vector weights across 65 feature dimensions of 150 data points for the 'Read' activity. The short sequence (150 data points) cannot represent either the whole 'Read' activities or all the remaining activities. The purpose of this feature selection section is to illustrate the similarities and variations of the selected features from these 150 data points, to show the potential of using the Attention Mechanism [64] for personalization by feature selection.

For example, the accelerometer sensor data resulted in a common key feature among four sequences of four participants (as illustrated in Figure 4). Therefore, it is not only important to consider the variations among different kinds of neurodiversity and the varied conditions of individuals, but it is also beneficial to learn notable features related to specific activities.

## 6.2 User-Independent Evaluation

To investigate the performance of the classification models across users, we conducted a user-independent evaluation. To specify, we applied Leave-One-User-Out analysis for evaluation. More specifically, we performed the leave-one-user-out task 8 times. Each time, we used a different user as the testing set and the data from all other users as the training set. Thus, each user was used once as a testing dataset.

FATHOM performs better in the user-independent evaluation than the user-dependent one given the size of training data. Specifically, for the user-dependent evaluation, per evaluation task, we just used 60% (adjacent time-series segmentation) of the data from a single user for training per user. While, for user-independent evaluation, per evaluation task, we used all the data of the remaining seven users for training which made the training set much larger than in the user-dependent evaluation. Hence, for FATHOM, the user-independent evaluation provides all training data per evaluation task to feed input to this sophisticated model.

The user-independent classification results (averaged results of the eight experiments) across all users are listed in Table 3. The results indicate that FATHOM performed best, obtaining 91.8% for BA, 91.8% of F1 score, and 97.5% averaged accuracy across all users' data. The CRNN, MLP(16,16), and S-LSTM performed relatively well. Similar to the user-dependent evaluation, LR performed worse when compared to the other models. The confusion matrix (Figure 3b) shows that FATHOM performs very well for leave-One-UserOut evaluation, except in some cases related to the 'Rest' which have been classified as 'Off-task'.

## 6.3 Summary

In a nutshell, through user-dependent and user-independent classifications, the result shows that existing HAR technologies can perform well on the data collected from neurodiverse populations as long as there is a representative dataset for that population.

Overall, deep learning models perform well and better than the conventional method (e.g. LR) on this dataset. To summarize, CRNN performed best (averaged BA of 92.2%) in the user-dependent evaluation, while FATHOM performed best (averaged BA of 91.8%) in the user-independent evaluation among the five models. Meanwhile, FATHOM achieved the second-best performance in the userdependent evaluation, and MLP (a neural network model) performed the second-best in the user-independent evaluation. ## 7 DISCUSSION AND DESIGN IMPLICATIONS

In this section, we reflect on the lessons learned and challenges faced to generate a wearable sensor dataset of learning activities for young adults with neurodiversity. We focus on data collection, data annotation, and classification evaluation. We also provide suggestions and design implications drawing from the research study.

Data Collection in Controlled Settings versus in-the-wild . Collecting data along with self-reported labels in the wild increases the size of the dataset. Despite such benefit, self-reports in the wild would also increase the cognitive load of the participants with neurodiversity during data collection. There are two ways to complete self-reports. One is to send real-time notifications for participants to report activities, and another is to report by recall after finishing the learning task. Real-time self-reports in the wild may interrupt participant's learning activities reducing their concentration on the task. Self-report by recall is less accurate and could be biased, especially for Off-task activities. Besides, some neurodiverse participants present memory limitations which make it harder for them to memorize and recall events accurately. Hence, it leads to a question of how to accurately and efficiently collect more data when involving participants with neurodiversity in research studies. Additionally, we could not collect more data in person to extend the dataset as planned due to the COVID-19 pandemic. One recommendation is to provide a platform that requires caregivers to observe and track the participants' learning activities to get more data with ground truth labels.

Data Annotation . Manual annotation of video recordings is time-consuming, and using online meeting environments for to complete this process remotely adds new challenges to the annotation. Before the quarantine, researchers separately coded each video and met in person to assess reliability and resolve eventual disagreements, re-watching the video and re-labeling the activities based on a common protocol to ensure consistency. Due to the COVID-19, meetings switched to online platforms to complete the annotation process. Specifically, a screen-sharing feature was used to re-watch the videos jointly and to resolve eventual disagreements. To preserve privacy, we never recorded our online meetings and we carefully chose collaboration tools requiring dual-factor authentication, following the university privacy guidelines and IRB policies. We also used encrypted files and secure transmission protocols to exchange data. Multimedia players using top-notch speed control could aid in the process, and teleconferencing tools with real-time synchronous sharing screen function will support distributed annotation sessions. For future work, one potential approach to automate the labeling process is to use the activity recognition models to do the coarse-level annotation first, and the human coders manually refine the generated labels by models. Another approach is to employ crowd-sourcing data annotation with authorized personnel since this process requires considerations of privacy constraints and technical configurations.

Unbalanced Samples . The unbalanced sample noted in the dataset indicates ecological validity by matching the records to real-world situations. Specifically, Read labels are the most frequent because there were two reading sessions among four tasks. Also, the fewer occurrences of Off-task labels are justifiable due to their rare occurrence. We notice the challenge to classify Off-task activities in Figure 3a, where the Off-task instances per user are limited. To overcome this, one recommendation is to use novel one-shot learning [17] and few-shot learning [54]. Both approaches aim to learn classification information given only one or a few samples.

In many cases, sampling can mitigate the issues caused by unbalanced sampling. Popular sampling techniques include oversampling and undersampling. Oversampling (e.g., Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) [10]) duplicates examples in the minority class or synthesize new instances from the samples in the minority class. Undersampling (e.g., random undersampling) deletes or selects a subset of examples from the majority class [4]. As for our case, data volume and data distribution varied among users. However, the overall distribution is a mixture of all user data distributions. Sampling may lead to a change in the data distribution. Thus, we focused on tuning the machine learning models for better prediction accuracy in this study. We will explore these sampling techniques in future work.

Limitations of Smartwatch Sensors . We observed some technical limitations regarding the smartwatch sensors. Although we set a common frequency among all sensors via Android sensor API, we found that the sampling rate is inconsistent among sensors. Specifically, the sampling rate of the accelerometer is the highest, followed by the gyroscope. Moreover, the sampling frequency of the heart rate sensor is much lower than the motion sensors. Therefore, it took longer for the heart rate sensor to begin data collection. Consequently, it recorded new data points at a slower rate. The sensors with high frequency resulted in fairly dense data points within a given period, while the sensors with lower frequencies resulted in sparse data points. Manipulating Android Wear OS Kernel to stream the accelerometer data at higher frequency [34] is a possible choice. Furthermore, more advanced upcoming hardware devices will likely provide more stable sampling rates. Another limitation of the sensors of the Android smartwatch is that it has just heart rate sensors measuring bpm. To address the lack of physiological data, in addition to the Android smartwatch, other wearable devices can be used to collect data, such as the E4 wristband. This approach implies in additional costs though.

Besides, according to key feature visualizations, applications and models with feature selection can be developed to collect data only from specific sensors, e.g., accelerometer and gyroscope sensors but not necessarily heart rate. Such an approach may also address the inherent limitations of wearable/mobile computing capability and the streaming bandwidth, that facilitates ATs based on ubiquitous computing.

Dataset for Young Adults with Neurodiversity . Although there are mobile and wearable datasets for various human activities from people without disabilities, there is no wearable dataset for learning activities collected from neurodiverse individuals. Without an inclusive dataset collected from diverse users' populations, it is unclear whether the state-of-the-art machine learning models will properly suit these users. Although various AI-based ATs were developed for users with visual/hearing impairments, young adults with neurodiversity are still less supported by ATs utilizing AI due to the lack of inclusive dataset [45]. Despite some limitations (e.g., demographics, types of disability, and population size) in our dataset, it is critical to start a discussion about the possibility of inclusive AI applications to assist young adults with neurodiversity in inclusive learning environments.

## 8 LIMITATIONS

As discussed in Section 7, due to the hardware limitation of smartwatch sensors, the sampling frequencies among the sensors varied. Besides, there is a limitation regarding the requirement to wear a smartwatch on the dominant hand in this study. Still, there are activities (e.g., typing, off-task) that involve two hands. Also, people may prefer to wear a smartwatch on their non-dominant hand. Additionally, there is a limitation in designing a within-subject experiment because participants needed to perform four learning tasks. Performing more tasks would cause fatigue, therefore, we limited the number of tasks and did not assign to the participants additional learning tasks (e.g., math calculating).

Although neurodiverse users commonly need assistance to execute learning tasks, the level of execution of the learning activities varies among them. In this study, we did not assess each participant's learning skills. Assessing their specific learning ability (e.g., motor coordination, eye-hand coordination, and cognitive level) and incorporating these differences will be explored as future work. Moreover, due to the difficulty of recruitment and getting the ground-truth data in the studies with controlled settings (rather than using self-reports), the sample size of WLA4ND dataset is relatively small. We plan to collect data with more participants and involve the community to improve the quality of the WLA4NDD dataset.

## 9 CONTRIBUTIONS

The three main contributions of this paper are stated as follows:

Dataset . WLA4ND dataset was collected from young adults with neurodiversity, a less represented group in Human Activity Recognition (HAR) research. By providing a dataset from populations with neurodiversity, we hope to contribute to inclusive AI technologies. Specifically, WLA4ND can be used to develop and to evaluate activity recognition models, aiming to further facilitate the assistance of people with neurodiversity in inclusive education programs.

Classification . The investigation of classification methods of five models showed that existing HAR technologies can be applied to neurodiverse populations as long as there is a representative dataset collected from that population. It sheds a light to use existing HAR to leverage wearable computing for assistance in inclusive settings. Additionally, we believe that the usage of HAR approaches would alleviate the burden of manual annotation to get more inclusive data with ground truth for people with neurodiversity.

Possible Assistive Applications . An application based on AI models trained on this novel WLA4ND dataset can help young adults with neurodiversity in education by tracking and monitoring their learning activities. Without an inclusive dataset and AI-based ATs, traditionally a support staff must observe the students with neurodiversity while performing academic learning activities, to monitor, track their performance, intervene when needed, and provide positive reinforcement. These assistants are absent when the students are off class or studying remotely. Possible assistive applications will be able to monitor learning activities via HAR. As the interventions to help students to be focused and to positively reinforce them are the most frequent assistance in inclusive education [73], the ATs can use HAR to detect Off-task timing and frequency to provide interventions on demand in order to help students concentrate while studying. Besides, the HAR can be used to monitor the sustained duration of their learning activities to positively reinforce their performance if the duration exceeds a set time threshold. Moreover, by tracking, analyzing, and/or visualizing the monitored learning activity patterns, it will aid the educators and caregivers to know better the students' learning performance to provide tailored programs and suitable study plans for each individual. Moreover, the smartwatch is less-stigmatizing, more affordable, accountable, and consistent (compared to supportive staff). Besides education settings, those applications can be used in training scenarios or inclusive employment, whenever reading, writing, and typing are required.

## 10 CONCLUSION

This paper presents a novel dataset of learning activities. WLA4ND was collected using built-in smartwatch sensors (accelerometer, gyroscope, and heart rate sensors). The data was collected in 32 learning sessions involving eight participants with neurodiversity, including ASD, ADHD, IDD, and Down Syndrome. The six groundtruth labels used to annotate the dataset are reading, answering follow-up Q&amp;A, prompt writing, typing, being Off-task, and taking a break. Such activities are common in postsecondary education and especially important for assistance in inclusive education. For reliability, WLA4ND was carefully annotated, reaching 98.79% (Fleiss' Kappa) alignment agreement rate among three coders. A classification evaluation using five state-of-the-art machine learning methods was conducted. For the user-dependent evaluation, an averaged balanced accuracy of 92.2% was achieved by CRNN. While, for the user-independent evaluation, 91.8% was achieved using FATHOM which is an attention-based deep learning model. The key features of four short reading sequences were also visualized. The dataset will allow future researchers to explore activity recognition models on it. WLA4ND will pave the way for researchers to implement AI-based assistive technologies (e.g., monitoring, intervention) to support young adults with neurodiversity in inclusive education settings.
